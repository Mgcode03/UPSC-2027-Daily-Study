# Ethics: Case Studies Approach and Ethical Dilemmas in Public Service

## Nature of Ethical Dilemmas in Public Service

Public servants frequently face situations in which competing values, duties, or interests create ethical dilemmas. An ethical dilemma arises when a person is confronted with a choice between two or more courses of action, each of which is supported by some ethical principle, but which cannot all be pursued simultaneously. In public administration, dilemmas often involve conflicts between:

- Personal values and organisational or political directives
- Loyalty to the organisation or superior and loyalty to the public interest or the Constitution
- Efficiency and equity
- Short-term political or administrative convenience and long-term public good
- Confidentiality and the public's right to know
- Rule-based compliance and contextual justice

Recognising the existence of a dilemma is the first step. The next steps involve clarifying the facts, identifying the stakeholders and the values at stake, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating them against ethical principles and consequences, and choosing a course that can be publicly justified.

## Framework for Analysing Case Studies

A systematic approach to case studies in ethics helps in arriving at reasoned and defensible decisions. A useful framework includes the following stages:

### 1. Identify the Facts
Separate facts from assumptions, opinions, and emotions. Clarify what is known, what is uncertain, and what additional information may be needed. In real administrative situations, incomplete information is common, and decisions often have to be made under conditions of uncertainty.

### 2. Identify the Stakeholders
List all individuals, groups, and institutions that are affected by or have an interest in the decision. Stakeholders may include the public at large, specific communities, colleagues, superiors, political executives, the organisation, future generations, and the decision-maker himself or herself.

### 3. Identify the Ethical Issues and Values at Stake
Articulate the competing values or principles. Common values in public service include integrity, impartiality, objectivity, accountability, transparency, compassion, justice, equity, efficiency, and respect for the rule of law. Clarify which values support which course of action.

### 4. Generate Alternative Courses of Action
Avoid the trap of seeing only two extreme options. Creative generation of intermediate or alternative options often opens possibilities that better reconcile competing values. Consider options that involve consultation, delay for more information, referral to higher authority, public disclosure, or procedural safeguards.

### 5. Evaluate the Alternatives
Evaluate each alternative using multiple ethical lenses:
- Consequentialist (utilitarian) lens: Which option produces the greatest overall good or the least harm for the greatest number?
- Deontological (duty-based) lens: Which option is consistent with duties, rules, and principles that should be followed regardless of consequences?
- Virtue ethics lens: Which option is consistent with the character and virtues expected of a public servant (honesty, courage, temperance, justice, compassion)?
- Justice and fairness lens: Does the option treat people equitably? Does it avoid discrimination? Does it give special consideration to the vulnerable where required by justice?
- Public interest and constitutional values lens: Is the option consistent with the Constitution, the rule of law, and the broader public interest?

### 6. Choose and Justify
Select the course of action that, on balance, best satisfies the relevant ethical considerations. Be prepared to justify the decision publicly and to take responsibility for it. Document the reasoning where appropriate.

### 7. Reflect on Implementation and Consequences
Consider how the decision will be implemented, what safeguards are needed, and how consequences will be monitored. Ethical decision-making does not end with the choice; it includes responsibility for the outcomes.

## Common Types of Ethical Dilemmas in Administration

### Conflict of Interest
A conflict of interest arises when a public servant's private interests (financial, familial, or personal) could improperly influence the performance of official duties. Even the appearance of a conflict can undermine public trust. The ethical response is to disclose the interest, recuse oneself from the decision where appropriate, and follow prescribed procedures for managing conflicts.

### Pressure from Political Executives or Superiors
Public servants may face pressure to act in ways that are legally or ethically questionable. The constitutional duty is to uphold the law and the Constitution. Advice should be tendered fearlessly and frankly. Where a direction is illegal or unconstitutional, the public servant has a duty to point this out and, in extreme cases, to refuse compliance through proper channels.

### Whistleblowing and Loyalty
When a public servant becomes aware of serious wrongdoing within the organisation, there is a tension between loyalty to the organisation and the duty to protect the public interest. Lawful whistleblowing mechanisms, internal reporting channels, and protection for bona fide whistleblowers are important institutional supports. The decision to go public should be a last resort after internal channels have been exhausted, and should be guided by the seriousness of the wrongdoing and the public interest.

### Resource Allocation and Competing Claims
In situations of scarcity, decisions about who receives benefits or bears burdens involve questions of justice. Utilitarian calculations of overall welfare, rights-based claims, need-based priority, and procedural fairness all have a role. Transparent criteria and reasoned justification help maintain legitimacy.

### Compassion versus Rule of Law
Cases involving vulnerable individuals (the poor, the displaced, the ill, or those affected by disasters) often create tension between strict application of rules and compassionate response. While rules exist for good reasons, rigid application without regard to context can produce injustice. Ethical administration seeks to interpret and apply rules in a manner that is consistent with their purpose and with constitutional values of dignity and justice, while remaining within the bounds of legality.

## Foundational Values for Public Service

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission and various codes of conduct emphasise certain foundational values for civil servants:
- Integrity: Consistency between public and private behaviour; honesty in the use of public resources and authority
- Impartiality and Non-partisanship: Decisions based on merit and public interest, not on political or personal affiliations
- Objectivity: Decisions based on evidence and rational analysis
- Dedication to Public Service: Commitment to the welfare of the people and the constitutional vision
- Empathy and Compassion: Sensitivity to the conditions of the weak and vulnerable
- Accountability and Transparency: Willingness to explain and justify decisions and to subject them to scrutiny

These values provide the normative compass for navigating ethical dilemmas.

## Role of Codes, Training, and Institutional Design

Written codes of ethics and conduct, ethics training, leadership by example, protection for those who act ethically, and institutional mechanisms for advice and oversight all contribute to an ethical organisational culture. Ethical behaviour is more likely when the system rewards integrity and makes it costly to act unethically.

## Conclusion

Ethical case studies in the examination test the ability to recognise dilemmas, apply ethical frameworks, balance competing values, and arrive at reasoned decisions that can be justified in terms of public interest and constitutional principles. Mastery of the analytical approach, combined with a firm grounding in the foundational values of public service, enables a candidate to respond to case studies with clarity, consistency, and moral seriousness.
