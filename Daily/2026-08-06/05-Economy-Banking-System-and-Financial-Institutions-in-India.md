# Banking System and Financial Institutions in India

## Structure of the Indian Banking System

The Indian banking system is a multi-tiered structure consisting of the Reserve Bank of India at the apex, commercial banks, cooperative banks, and specialised financial institutions. The system has evolved from a largely private and foreign-dominated structure in the colonial period to a predominantly public-sector-oriented system after nationalisation, and more recently to a mixed system with significant private and foreign participation under a strong regulatory framework.

## Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the central bank of the country. It was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934. It was nationalised in 1949. The RBI performs several critical functions:

- Monetary authority: Formulates, implements, and monitors monetary policy to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth.
- Issuer of currency: Has the sole right to issue banknotes in India (except one-rupee notes and coins, which are issued by the Government of India).
- Banker and debt manager to the Government: Manages the public debt of the Central and State Governments and acts as banker to them.
- Banker to banks: Maintains banking accounts of all scheduled banks and provides financial accommodation to them.
- Regulator and supervisor of the financial system: Prescribes broad parameters of banking operations within which the banking and financial system functions.
- Manager of foreign exchange: Manages the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, to facilitate external trade and payment and promote orderly development of the foreign exchange market.
- Developmental role: Performs a wide range of promotional functions to support national objectives.

The RBI is governed by a Central Board of Directors appointed by the Government of India. The Governor is the chief executive. The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), constituted under the RBI Act, determines the policy interest rate required to achieve the inflation target.

## Commercial Banks

Commercial banks are the backbone of the banking system. They accept deposits from the public and provide loans and advances. Commercial banks in India are classified as:

### Scheduled Commercial Banks
These are banks included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934. They must satisfy certain conditions regarding paid-up capital and reserves and must not conduct their affairs in a manner detrimental to the interests of depositors. Scheduled banks enjoy certain privileges, such as access to the RBI's lending facilities and lower cash reserve requirements in some contexts historically.

Scheduled Commercial Banks are further divided into:
- Public Sector Banks (including the State Bank of India and other nationalised banks, and their associates before mergers)
- Private Sector Banks (old private banks and new private banks licensed after 1991)
- Foreign Banks
- Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)

### Public Sector Banks
The major phase of bank nationalisation occurred in 1969 (14 major banks) and 1980 (6 more banks). The objectives were to ensure that banking facilities reached the unbanked areas, that credit was directed to priority sectors such as agriculture and small industry, and that the banking system served the broader goals of economic development. Over time, public sector banks have undergone consolidation through mergers to create stronger and more efficient entities.

### Private Sector Banks and Foreign Banks
Liberalisation of the banking sector after 1991 led to the entry of new private sector banks and expansion of foreign banks. These banks have introduced technological innovations, improved customer service, and increased competition. Foreign banks operate through branches or wholly-owned subsidiaries under the regulatory framework of the RBI.

### Regional Rural Banks
Regional Rural Banks were established under the Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976, to provide credit and other facilities to small and marginal farmers, agricultural labourers, artisans, and small entrepreneurs in rural areas. They are jointly owned by the Central Government, the concerned State Government, and a sponsor bank.

## Cooperative Banks

Cooperative banks form an important part of the rural and semi-urban credit structure. They operate on the principle of cooperation and mutual help. The structure is generally three-tier in most states:
- Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) at the village level
- District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) at the intermediate level
- State Cooperative Banks (StCBs) at the apex level

Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) serve the needs of urban and semi-urban areas. Cooperative banks are regulated by the RBI in respect of banking functions and by the Registrar of Cooperative Societies of the concerned State in respect of management and other matters. Dual regulation has been a long-standing issue, and recent reforms have sought to strengthen the regulatory framework.

## Development Financial Institutions and Non-Banking Financial Companies

In the earlier decades of planning, specialised Development Financial Institutions (DFIs) such as the Industrial Development Bank of India (IDBI), Industrial Finance Corporation of India (IFCI), and Industrial Credit and Investment Corporation of India (ICICI) played a major role in providing long-term finance for industrial development. Many of these institutions have since transformed into commercial banks or have been restructured.

Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) provide a range of financial services including loans, advances, acquisition of shares and securities, hire-purchase, and leasing. They do not accept demand deposits in the same way as banks and are subject to a different regulatory regime. NBFCs have grown significantly and play an important role in credit delivery, especially to segments that may not be fully served by traditional banks.

## Priority Sector Lending

Banks in India are required to lend a specified portion of their Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) or Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure, whichever is higher, to priority sectors. Priority sectors include agriculture, micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs), export credit, education, housing, social infrastructure, renewable energy, and others as defined by the RBI. This policy ensures that the formal credit system serves the needs of weaker sections and sectors critical for inclusive growth.

## Financial Inclusion

Financial inclusion refers to the process of ensuring access to appropriate financial products and services needed by all sections of society, particularly vulnerable groups, at an affordable cost in a fair and transparent manner. Initiatives such as the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, the expansion of banking correspondents, the use of technology (including Aadhaar-enabled payments and Unified Payments Interface), and the licensing of small finance banks and payments banks have significantly expanded the reach of formal finance.

## Key Regulatory and Supervisory Aspects

The RBI regulates and supervises banks through licensing, prescription of capital adequacy norms (aligned with Basel standards), asset classification and provisioning norms, exposure limits, and on-site and off-site supervision. The Banking Regulation Act, 1949, provides the statutory framework for regulation of banking companies. Deposit insurance is provided by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), a wholly-owned subsidiary of the RBI.

## Significance for the Economy

A sound banking system is essential for the mobilisation of savings, allocation of credit to productive uses, transmission of monetary policy, and maintenance of financial stability. Challenges such as non-performing assets, capital adequacy, governance, technological disruption, and inclusion continue to shape the evolution of the Indian banking system. Understanding the structure, functions, and regulation of banks and financial institutions is fundamental to the study of the Indian economy.
