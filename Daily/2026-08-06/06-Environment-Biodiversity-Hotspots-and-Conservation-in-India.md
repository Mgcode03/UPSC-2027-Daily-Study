# Biodiversity Hotspots and Conservation in India

## Understanding Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variability among living organisms from all sources, including terrestrial, marine, and other aquatic ecosystems, and the ecological complexes of which they are part. It includes diversity within species (genetic diversity), between species (species diversity), and of ecosystems (ecosystem diversity).

India is one of the 17 megadiverse countries in the world. It accounts for about 7-8 per cent of the recorded species of the world while occupying only about 2.4 per cent of the world's land area. India has a wide range of ecosystems, from the Himalayas in the north to the coastal and marine ecosystems in the south, from the deserts of the west to the rainforests of the northeast and the Western Ghats.

## Biodiversity Hotspots

The concept of biodiversity hotspots was developed by Norman Myers. A biodiversity hotspot is a biogeographic region with significant levels of biodiversity that is under threat from humans. To qualify as a hotspot, a region must meet two strict criteria:
- It must contain at least 1,500 species of vascular plants as endemics (species found nowhere else).
- It must have lost at least 70 per cent of its primary native vegetation.

There are 36 biodiversity hotspots in the world. Four of these are present in India (fully or partially):

### The Himalayas
The Himalayan hotspot extends across the Himalayan range, including parts of India, Nepal, Bhutan, and Pakistan, and into southern Tibet and northern Myanmar. It is characterised by extraordinary altitudinal variation and a corresponding diversity of ecosystems, from subtropical forests at lower elevations to alpine meadows and cold deserts at higher elevations. The region is home to numerous endemic plant species and iconic fauna such as the snow leopard, red panda, and Himalayan tahr. Threats include deforestation, habitat fragmentation, climate change, and infrastructure development.

### The Western Ghats
The Western Ghats, also known as Sahyadri, run parallel to the western coast of India from Gujarat to Tamil Nadu. They are one of the world's eight hottest hotspots of biodiversity. The region has a high degree of endemism, particularly among amphibians, reptiles, and flowering plants. The Western Ghats influence the monsoon patterns of peninsular India and are the source of many important rivers. Threats include plantation agriculture, mining, hydroelectric projects, and urbanisation.

### The Indo-Burma Region
This hotspot includes parts of northeastern India (east of the Brahmaputra), Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, and parts of southern China. It is one of the most biologically diverse regions on Earth, with a high concentration of endemic species. In the Indian portion, the northeast is known for its rich forest cover, unique flora and fauna, and high ethnic diversity. Threats include shifting cultivation, logging, and infrastructure development.

### Sundaland
The Sundaland hotspot includes the Nicobar Islands of India, along with Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei, and Singapore. The Nicobar Islands have unique ecosystems and endemic species. The region as a whole is under severe pressure from deforestation and conversion of land for agriculture and plantations.

## Biogeographic Zones of India

India has been divided into ten biogeographic zones for the purpose of conservation planning:
1. Trans-Himalayan zone
2. Himalayan zone
3. Desert zone
4. Semi-arid zone
5. Western Ghats zone
6. Deccan Peninsula zone
7. Gangetic Plain zone
8. Northeast zone
9. Coastal zone
10. Islands zone

These zones help in identifying representative areas for protection and in understanding the distribution of biodiversity across the country.

## Protected Area Network

India has an extensive network of protected areas under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972. The main categories are:

- National Parks: Areas of adequate ecological, faunal, floral, geomorphological, natural, or zoological significance, notified for the purpose of protecting and propagating or developing wildlife and its environment. Grazing and private rights are not allowed.
- Wildlife Sanctuaries: Areas where wildlife is protected and hunting is prohibited, but certain other activities may be permitted under regulation.
- Conservation Reserves: Areas adjacent to national parks or sanctuaries, or linking protected areas, declared for the protection of landscapes, seascapes, flora, fauna, and their habitat.
- Community Reserves: Areas where a community or an individual has volunteered to conserve wildlife and its habitat.
- Biosphere Reserves: Large areas of terrestrial or coastal/marine ecosystems, or a combination thereof, recognised under UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere Programme. They are intended to conserve biodiversity, promote research and monitoring, and support sustainable development. India has several biosphere reserves, some of which are also part of the World Network of Biosphere Reserves.

In addition, there are Tiger Reserves under Project Tiger and Elephant Reserves under Project Elephant, which provide landscape-level conservation for these flagship species and their habitats.

## Threatened Species and Conservation Status

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List categorises species according to their risk of extinction: Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern, and Data Deficient. India has a significant number of species in the threatened categories, including the tiger, Asian elephant, one-horned rhinoceros, snow leopard, lion-tailed macaque, and many endemic amphibians and plants of the Western Ghats and the northeast.

Conservation strategies include in-situ conservation (protection in natural habitats through protected areas and habitat management) and ex-situ conservation (protection outside natural habitats through zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, and captive breeding programmes).

## Legal and Policy Framework

Key legal instruments for biodiversity conservation in India include:
- Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972
- Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980
- Environment (Protection) Act, 1986
- Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (which provides for the conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising out of the use of biological resources and knowledge)
- Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006

India is a party to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and has prepared National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans. The National Biodiversity Authority, State Biodiversity Boards, and Biodiversity Management Committees form the institutional structure under the Biological Diversity Act.

## Challenges and the Way Forward

Major challenges to biodiversity conservation in India include habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation of resources, invasive alien species, pollution, and climate change. Human-wildlife conflict is an increasing concern in many landscapes. Conservation efforts must integrate the needs of local communities, secure livelihoods, and ensure that development projects incorporate robust environmental safeguards. Landscape-level planning, ecological connectivity, restoration of degraded habitats, and strengthened enforcement are essential for long-term conservation success.

A thorough understanding of biodiversity hotspots, the protected area system, and the legal and institutional framework is essential for answering questions on environment and ecology in both Prelims and Mains.
