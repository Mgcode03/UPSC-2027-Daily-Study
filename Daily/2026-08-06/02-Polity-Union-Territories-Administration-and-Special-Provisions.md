# Union Territories: Administration and Special Provisions

## Constitutional Position of Union Territories

India is a Union of States. The Constitution originally provided for four categories of territories: Part A, Part B, Part C, and Part D States. After the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, and subsequent amendments, the territories of India are classified into States and Union Territories.

Union Territories are administered by the President acting through an Administrator appointed by him. Article 239 provides that every Union Territory shall be administered by the President acting, to such extent as he thinks fit, through an Administrator to be appointed by him with such designation as he may specify.

Unlike States, which have their own elected governments with legislative and executive powers under the Constitution, Union Territories are under the direct control of the Central Government. This arrangement allows the Centre to administer areas that are strategically important, culturally distinct, or not yet ready for full statehood.

## List of Union Territories

As of the current constitutional position, the Union Territories of India are:
- Andaman and Nicobar Islands
- Chandigarh
- Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu
- Lakshadweep
- National Capital Territory of Delhi
- Puducherry
- Jammu and Kashmir
- Ladakh

The status of some territories has changed over time. Goa, which was a Union Territory, became a full-fledged State in 1987. Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Tripura, and others followed similar paths. The creation of new Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh in 2019 following the reorganisation of the former State of Jammu and Kashmir is a significant recent development.

## Administration of Union Territories

The Administrator of a Union Territory is appointed by the President and is generally designated as Lieutenant Governor or Administrator or Chief Commissioner, depending on the territory. The Administrator exercises powers as directed by the President.

For certain Union Territories, the Constitution provides for a Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers. These are:
- National Capital Territory of Delhi (Article 239AA)
- Puducherry (Article 239A)
- Jammu and Kashmir (after the 2019 reorganisation, with provisions under the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act)

In these territories, there is a dual structure: an elected Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers responsible to it, along with a Lieutenant Governor who represents the Centre and has significant residual and discretionary powers.

## Special Provisions for the National Capital Territory of Delhi

Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1991, provides a special status to Delhi. It creates a Legislative Assembly for the National Capital Territory with the power to make laws on all matters in the State List and Concurrent List except public order, police, and land. These three subjects remain with the Central Government.

There is a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor. However, in case of difference of opinion between the Lieutenant Governor and the Council of Ministers on any matter, the Lieutenant Governor is required to refer it to the President for decision. Pending such decision, the Lieutenant Governor can take action if the matter is urgent.

The Supreme Court has clarified the constitutional position in several judgments. The elected government has primacy in matters within its legislative competence, while the Lieutenant Governor retains a role in ensuring that the dual control arrangement functions smoothly. The relationship requires constitutional statesmanship and cooperative federalism within the unique framework of the national capital.

## Puducherry

Puducherry has a Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers under Article 239A. The Administrator (Lieutenant Governor) has powers similar in many respects to those in Delhi, though the specific legislative competence and residual powers differ. Puducherry consists of four geographically separated regions: Puducherry, Karaikal, Mahe, and Yanam, which were former French establishments.

## Other Union Territories

For Union Territories without legislatures (Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, Lakshadweep, and Ladakh), the Administrator exercises executive powers under the directions of the President. Laws for these territories are made by Parliament. The President can also make regulations for the peace, progress, and good government of these territories under Article 240.

Article 240 empowers the President to make regulations for certain Union Territories. These regulations have the same force and effect as Acts of Parliament and can even repeal or amend an Act of Parliament in relation to that territory.

## Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh

Following the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, the former State of Jammu and Kashmir was bifurcated into two Union Territories: the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir (with a Legislative Assembly) and the Union Territory of Ladakh (without a Legislative Assembly).

The Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir has a Legislative Assembly with powers similar to other states in many respects, subject to the provisions of the Reorganisation Act and the Constitution as applicable. The Lieutenant Governor has significant powers, particularly in matters of police, public order, and All India Services.

Ladakh is administered by a Lieutenant Governor without a Legislative Assembly. Special provisions for the protection of the interests of the people of Ladakh, including cultural and land-related safeguards, are provided through administrative arrangements and the Constitution.

## Significance of Union Territories

Union Territories serve several important purposes:
- Administration of strategically sensitive border areas
- Governance of small territories that may not be viable as full states
- Accommodation of unique historical or cultural circumstances (as in the case of former French and Portuguese possessions)
- Special arrangement for the national capital to balance local democracy with national interests

The existence of Union Territories reflects the flexible federal design of the Indian Constitution, which allows different degrees of autonomy and central control according to the needs of different regions.

## Relationship with the Centre

The Centre exercises greater control over Union Territories than over States. Parliament can make laws on any subject for Union Territories, including subjects in the State List. The Administrator is the agent of the President and is bound by the directions of the Central Government.

In territories with legislatures, a delicate balance exists between the elected government and the Lieutenant Governor. Constitutional conventions, judicial interpretations, and political maturity play a crucial role in the smooth functioning of these arrangements.

Understanding the administration of Union Territories is important for comprehending the asymmetric federalism of India, the special position of the national capital, and the mechanisms through which the Centre maintains control over sensitive and strategically important areas.
