# India’s Relations with Major Powers – Conceptual Foundations

## Introduction

India’s foreign policy towards major powers – the United States, China, Russia, and the European Union / major European countries – is shaped by the pursuit of strategic autonomy, economic development, security interests, and a desire for a multipolar world order. Relations are multi-dimensional: political-strategic, economic-trade, defence, technology, people-to-people, and multilateral cooperation or competition.

Understanding these relationships requires attention to historical legacies, current convergences and divergences of interest, and the evolving global balance of power.

## India–United States Relations

**Historical Trajectory**:
During the Cold War, relations were often distant or strained due to India’s non-alignment, close ties with the Soviet Union, and US alliances with Pakistan. The 1971 Bangladesh crisis and the USS Enterprise episode marked a low point. Post-Cold War, and especially after the 1998 nuclear tests and the subsequent engagement, relations transformed.

**Key Drivers of Transformation**:
- Shared concerns about the rise of China and the need for a stable balance in the Indo-Pacific
- Economic liberalisation in India creating opportunities for trade and investment
- Counter-terrorism cooperation after 2001
- Civil nuclear cooperation (2005 agreement and subsequent 123 Agreement) ending India’s nuclear isolation
- Defence cooperation: foundational agreements (LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA), joint exercises, and growing defence trade
- Technology, energy, and people-to-people ties (large Indian diaspora in the US, education, and professional links)

**Areas of Convergence**: Democracy, counter-terrorism, maritime security in the Indo-Pacific, technology and innovation, climate and clean energy (with nuances), and support for India’s rise as a balancing power.

**Areas of Divergence / Friction**: Trade issues (tariffs, market access, intellectual property), differences on approaches to Russia (especially after 2022), human rights and democracy narratives at times, and the US relationship with Pakistan.

**Strategic Framework**: The relationship is often described as a Comprehensive Global Strategic Partnership. The Quad (India, US, Japan, Australia) is a key plurilateral platform reflecting shared Indo-Pacific interests without being a formal military alliance.

## India–China Relations

**Historical Context**:
Civilisational contacts and the 1950s Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai phase gave way to the 1962 border war, which left a deep imprint. The boundary dispute (Western, Middle, and Eastern sectors) remains unresolved. Relations have oscillated between engagement and confrontation.

**Dimensions**:
- **Boundary Question**: Ongoing Special Representatives dialogue and various confidence-building measures, but periodic crises (Sumdorong Chu, Doklam 2017, Galwan 2020 and subsequent standoffs) highlight the fragility.
- **Economic**: China is a major trading partner; however, the trade deficit is large and persistent. Issues of market access, investment scrutiny, and dependence in critical sectors (pharmaceuticals, electronics, solar) have grown.
- **Strategic and Geopolitical**: Competition in the neighbourhood (South Asia, Indian Ocean), Belt and Road Initiative (India’s objections to CPEC passing through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir), and differing visions of Asian and global order.
- **Multilateral**: Cooperation in BRICS, SCO, and climate forums coexists with rivalry in other domains.

**Current Character**: Relations are characterised by a mix of cooperation, competition, and contestation. Managing the boundary, reducing economic vulnerabilities, and maintaining strategic space while avoiding uncontrolled escalation are central challenges. The principle of mutual respect for each other’s core interests and sensitivities remains aspirational and contested.

## India–Russia Relations

**Historical Depth**:
The Indo-Soviet relationship during the Cold War provided India with diplomatic support, defence equipment, and industrial assistance. The 1971 Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation was a landmark. After the Soviet collapse, relations were recalibrated but retained strategic content.

**Contemporary Pillars**:
- **Defence**: Russia remains a major supplier of military hardware (platforms, spares, joint production such as BrahMos). Diversification of India’s defence sources is underway, but the Russian share remains significant.
- **Energy**: Oil, gas, and nuclear cooperation (Kudankulam and potential further projects).
- **Strategic Autonomy and Multipolarity**: Shared interest in a multipolar world order and resistance to unilateralism.
- **Connectivity and Eurasia**: Discussions on corridors and cooperation in Central Asia and the Arctic.

**Challenges**: Russia’s growing closeness with China, the impact of Western sanctions on Russia (affecting defence supplies and payments), and India’s need to balance relations with both Russia and the West/US. India’s continued engagement with Russia reflects the value placed on strategic autonomy and diversified partnerships.

## India–Europe Relations

Europe is an important economic partner (trade, investment, technology), a source of development experience, and a partner on issues such as climate change, connectivity, and multilateralism. Relations with the European Union and key countries (France, Germany, UK, etc.) have strengthened.

**France** has emerged as a particularly close strategic partner: defence (Rafale, joint exercises, Indian Ocean cooperation), nuclear, space, and a shared vision of strategic autonomy and multipolarity. The India-France relationship is often cited as a model of trusted partnership.

**Broader Europe**: Free trade agreement negotiations with the EU, cooperation on green transition, digital, and critical technologies, and shared concerns about rules-based order. Differences can arise on trade, data, human rights narratives, and approaches to specific geopolitical crises.

## Overarching Themes in India’s Major Power Diplomacy

- **Strategic Autonomy**: Avoiding exclusive alignment; maintaining freedom of choice and diversified partnerships.
- **Multi-alignment**: Engaging multiple powers simultaneously on the basis of issue-based convergence rather than rigid blocs.
- **Economic and Technological Security**: Reducing critical dependencies, building domestic capacity, and seeking reliable partnerships for technology and investment.
- **Indo-Pacific Focus**: Growing emphasis on maritime security, connectivity, and a free, open, and inclusive Indo-Pacific.
- **Multilateral Engagement**: Using platforms such as the UN, G20, BRICS, SCO, Quad, and others to advance interests and shape norms.

## Analytical Points for Examination

Questions often require assessment of the nature of a bilateral relationship (converging interests vs. structural constraints), the impact of global shifts (US-China rivalry, Russia-Ukraine conflict, technology competition), and India’s policy responses. Balanced answers recognise both opportunities and limitations, historical legacies and current drivers, and the linkage between foreign policy and domestic developmental priorities.

India’s approach remains pragmatic, interest-driven, and oriented towards maximising strategic space in an increasingly contested international environment.
