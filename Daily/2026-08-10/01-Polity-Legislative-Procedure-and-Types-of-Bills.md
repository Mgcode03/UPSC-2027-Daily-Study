# Legislative Procedure in Parliament and Types of Bills

## Introduction to the Legislative Process

The Parliament of India is the supreme legislative body. Its primary function is to make laws for the country. The process of law-making is detailed, deliberate, and designed to ensure thorough scrutiny, debate, and democratic legitimacy. Understanding the legislative procedure is essential for grasping how policy becomes enforceable law and how the constitutional balance between the Executive, Legislature, and Judiciary operates in practice.

Article 107 to 122 of the Constitution deal with the legislative procedure in Parliament. The process differs slightly between ordinary bills, money bills, financial bills, and constitution amendment bills. The path a bill takes reflects the constitutional principle of parliamentary sovereignty tempered by federalism, fundamental rights, and judicial review.

## Stages of an Ordinary Bill

An ordinary bill (also called a non-money bill) can be introduced in either House of Parliament – Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha – by a Minister or a private member.

### 1. First Reading

The bill is introduced. The member in charge asks for leave to introduce the bill. If leave is granted (usually without discussion), the bill is introduced. The title and objectives are read. No debate on the merits takes place at this stage. After introduction, the bill is published in the Official Gazette.

### 2. Second Reading

This is the most important stage and consists of three sub-stages:

**Stage of General Discussion**: The principles and provisions of the bill are discussed in general. The House may refer the bill to a Select Committee of the House, a Joint Committee of both Houses, or circulate it for eliciting public opinion. If no reference is made, the bill proceeds to clause-by-clause consideration.

**Committee Stage**: If referred to a committee, the committee examines the bill in detail, may take evidence from experts and stakeholders, and submits a report with recommendations. The committee stage allows detailed, less partisan scrutiny.

**Consideration Stage (Clause-by-Clause)**: Each clause is discussed and voted upon. Amendments can be moved. This is where the detailed shape of the law is determined.

### 3. Third Reading

The bill is debated as a whole. Only formal amendments (to correct errors or improve language) are allowed. After the debate, the bill is put to vote. If passed, it is transmitted to the other House.

### 4. Passage in the Second House

The second House may:
- Pass the bill without amendments
- Pass it with amendments (returned to the first House for concurrence)
- Reject the bill
- Take no action (in which case, after six months, the bill is deemed rejected for the purpose of joint sitting)

If there is disagreement between the two Houses on an ordinary bill, the President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108. In a joint sitting, the bill is passed by a simple majority of the total members present and voting. Joint sittings are rare; only a few have been held in independent India (e.g., Dowry Prohibition Bill, Banking Service Commission Repeal Bill, Prevention of Terrorism Bill).

### 5. Assent of the President

After both Houses pass the bill (or it is passed in joint sitting), it is presented to the President for assent under Article 111. The President may:
- Give assent (bill becomes Act)
- Withhold assent (absolute veto – rarely used)
- Return the bill for reconsideration (suspensive veto – except money bills)
- Reserve the bill for consideration of the President if it is a State bill affecting Centre-State relations or other specified matters (in practice for State bills)

Once the President gives assent, the bill becomes an Act of Parliament and is notified in the Gazette.

## Types of Bills

### Ordinary Bills

These deal with any matter other than financial subjects. They can originate in either House. Both Houses have equal powers. Disagreement can lead to joint sitting.

### Money Bills (Article 110)

A Money Bill contains only provisions dealing with all or any of the following matters:
- Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax
- Regulation of borrowing of money or giving of any guarantee by the Government of India
- Custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund, payment into or withdrawal from such funds
- Appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India
- Declaring any expenditure as charged on the Consolidated Fund
- Receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or public account, or custody or issue of such money
- Any matter incidental to the above

The Speaker of Lok Sabha certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill. The decision of the Speaker is final and cannot be questioned in any court.

Special features of Money Bills:
- Can be introduced only in Lok Sabha
- Can be introduced only on the recommendation of the President
- Rajya Sabha cannot amend or reject a Money Bill. It can only make recommendations within 14 days. Lok Sabha may accept or reject the recommendations. If Rajya Sabha does not return the bill within 14 days, it is deemed passed by both Houses in the form passed by Lok Sabha.
- President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration.

### Financial Bills

There are two categories:

**Financial Bill Category I (Article 117(1))**: A bill that contains any of the matters of a Money Bill but does not contain only those matters (i.e., it has other provisions as well). It can be introduced only in Lok Sabha on the recommendation of the President. In all other respects, it is treated like an ordinary bill (both Houses have equal powers, joint sitting possible).

**Financial Bill Category II (Article 117(3))**: A bill that involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India but does not include any of the matters of a Money Bill. It can be introduced in either House. The recommendation of the President is required only at the stage of consideration (not introduction). It is otherwise treated as an ordinary bill.

### Constitution Amendment Bills (Article 368)

These bills seek to amend the Constitution. They can be introduced in either House. They require a special majority: a majority of the total membership of that House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

For certain amendments (those affecting federal features listed in the proviso to Article 368), ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures is also required. There is no provision for joint sitting for Constitution Amendment Bills. The President must give assent; he cannot withhold or return such a bill (after the 24th Amendment).

### Private Members’ Bills

Bills introduced by members who are not Ministers. They are taken up only on Fridays. The success rate is very low because the government controls the legislative agenda. However, they serve as a means for members to highlight issues and sometimes influence government policy.

## Key Concepts and Distinctions

**Difference between Money Bill and Financial Bill**:
- Money Bill: only money matters listed in Article 110; Speaker’s certification final; Rajya Sabha has limited role.
- Financial Bill I: money matters + other matters; introduced only in Lok Sabha; both Houses equal thereafter.
- Financial Bill II: involves expenditure but no Article 110 matters; can be introduced in either House.

**Joint Sitting**: Applicable only to ordinary bills and Financial Bills of Category I when there is deadlock. Not available for Money Bills or Constitution Amendment Bills.

**Role of President**: The President is an integral part of Parliament (Article 79). His recommendation is mandatory for Money Bills and certain Financial Bills. His assent is the final step for ordinary legislation.

**Guillotine**: In the context of the Budget, when discussion on Demands for Grants is cut short due to lack of time and all remaining demands are put to vote together.

**Cut Motions**: During discussion on Demands for Grants, members can move cut motions to reduce the amount of a demand. There are three types: Policy Cut, Economy Cut, and Token Cut. These are devices of financial control by the Legislature over the Executive.

## Significance for Governance

The elaborate legislative procedure ensures:
- Democratic deliberation and accountability
- Protection of federal balance (especially through Rajya Sabha and special procedures for certain amendments)
- Financial control of the Legislature over the Executive (through Money Bill and Budget procedures)
- Opportunity for expert and public input via committees

At the same time, the dominance of the ruling party in Lok Sabha and the control of the legislative timetable by the government mean that most government bills are passed, while private members’ bills rarely succeed. The real quality of legislation often depends on the strength of parliamentary committees and the quality of debate rather than the formal stages alone.

Understanding these procedures helps in analysing parliamentary functioning, the relative powers of the two Houses, the role of the Speaker, and the constitutional design of checks and balances in the Indian political system.
