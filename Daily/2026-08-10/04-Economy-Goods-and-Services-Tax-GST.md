# Goods and Services Tax (GST) in India

## Introduction and Background

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) is a comprehensive, multi-stage, destination-based tax on value addition levied on the supply of goods and services. It replaced a complex web of central and state indirect taxes and is widely regarded as one of the most significant tax reforms in independent India.

GST was introduced on 1 July 2017 through the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016. It created a dual GST structure reflecting India’s federal character: Central GST (CGST) levied by the Centre, State GST (SGST) levied by States, and Integrated GST (IGST) levied by the Centre on inter-state supplies (with revenue shared with States).

## Pre-GST Indirect Tax Regime – Problems

Before GST, India had a fragmented indirect tax system:
- Central taxes: Central Excise Duty, Service Tax, Additional Duties of Customs (CVD), Special Additional Duty (SAD), Central Sales Tax (CST), etc.
- State taxes: VAT/Sales Tax, Entry Tax, Luxury Tax, Entertainment Tax, Octroi, etc.

**Major issues**:
- Cascading effect (tax on tax) because input tax credit was not fully available across the central-state divide and across different taxes.
- Multiple taxes, multiple compliance requirements, and different procedures across states.
- Classification disputes and inverted duty structures.
- Check-posts and barriers hindering free movement of goods (the “inspector raj” and logistics inefficiencies).
- Lack of a common national market.

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

The 101st Amendment inserted Article 246A (concurrent power of Centre and States to levy GST), Article 269A (IGST on inter-state trade), and Article 279A (GST Council). It also amended the Seventh Schedule (deleted/modified certain entries related to taxes subsumed in GST).

The GST Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising state finance ministers, makes recommendations on rates, exemptions, model laws, and other matters. Decisions are taken by a three-fourths majority weighted voting (Centre has one-third weight, states together two-thirds).

Key legislations: CGST Act, SGST Acts (each state), IGST Act, and UTGST Act, along with GST (Compensation to States) Act.

## Structure and Features of GST

**Dual Model**: Both Centre and States levy GST on a common base. For intra-state supply: CGST + SGST. For inter-state supply: IGST.

**Destination-Based**: Tax accrues to the state where goods or services are consumed, not where they are produced. This shifted the earlier origin-based bias.

**Value Addition and Input Tax Credit (ITC)**: Tax is levied at each stage of the supply chain on the value added. Registered taxpayers can claim credit for tax paid on inputs, input services, and capital goods, subject to conditions. This eliminates cascading.

**Threshold Exemption**: Small suppliers below a turnover threshold are exempt from registration (with some exceptions). Composition scheme is available for smaller taxpayers with simplified compliance and lower rates but restricted ITC.

**Tax Slabs**: Multiple rate structure – typically 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%, with some items attracting cess (especially demerit and luxury goods). Rate rationalisation has been an ongoing process through GST Council decisions.

**Exemptions and Special Categories**: Certain goods and services are exempt (e.g., unprocessed food items, healthcare, education in specified cases). Petroleum products, alcohol for human consumption, and electricity remain outside GST (for the time being), continuing under earlier tax regimes.

## Administration and Compliance

- **GSTN (Goods and Services Tax Network)**: Provides the IT backbone for registration, return filing, payment, and matching of invoices.
- **Returns**: Regular taxpayers file returns (GSTR-1 for outward supplies, GSTR-3B as a summary return, etc.). The system has evolved with simplifications over time.
- **E-way Bill**: Electronic way bill for movement of goods above a certain value, replacing multiple state permits and facilitating seamless inter-state movement.
- **Invoice Matching and Scrutiny**: Aimed at reducing tax evasion through invoice-level matching (though full real-time matching has faced practical challenges).

## Compensation to States

To address states’ concerns about revenue loss (especially manufacturing states under the destination principle), the GST (Compensation to States) Act provided for compensation for five years (2017–2022) based on a projected 14% annual growth over the base year. Compensation was funded by a cess on certain luxury and sin goods. The compensation period was extended in a modified form during the COVID period due to revenue shortfalls.

## Achievements and Benefits

- Creation of a more unified national market; removal of many inter-state barriers.
- Reduction in cascading of taxes; improvement in competitiveness.
- Increased formalisation of the economy through the ITC chain and digital compliance.
- Broadening of the tax base over time.
- Logistics efficiency gains (faster movement of goods, reduced waiting at check-posts).
- Greater transparency and data availability for tax administration.

## Challenges and Criticisms

- **Complexity of Rate Structure**: Multiple slabs and frequent changes create compliance burden and classification disputes.
- **Inverted Duty Structure**: In some sectors, tax on inputs is higher than on output, leading to accumulated ITC and working capital blockage.
- **Compliance Burden for SMEs**: Despite thresholds and composition scheme, return filing, invoice matching, and technology requirements remain challenging for smaller businesses.
- **Revenue Concerns of States**: Especially after the initial compensation period; debates on revenue neutrality and growth assumptions.
- **Exclusion of Key Items**: Petroleum, alcohol, and electricity remain outside, limiting the full benefits of a comprehensive GST and creating continuing cascading in those chains.
- **Technical and Administrative Glitches**: Early years saw significant IT and procedural issues; continuous simplification efforts are ongoing.
- **Federal Tensions**: Occasional differences within the GST Council on rates, exemptions, and compensation.

## Ongoing Evolution

GST continues to evolve through GST Council decisions on rate rationalisation, simplification of returns, expansion of e-invoicing, measures against fake invoicing and evasion, and discussions on bringing excluded items into the GST net. The long-term vision remains a simpler rate structure, broader base, and smoother compliance.

## Conceptual Significance

GST represents a major shift from a production-based, fragmented tax system to a consumption-based, value-added national tax system. It tests the working of cooperative federalism through the GST Council. For the economy, it aims to improve efficiency, reduce distortions, and support a common market. For public finance, it is a major source of revenue for both Centre and States and a tool for formalisation and data-driven administration.

Understanding GST requires grasping the concepts of value addition, input tax credit, destination principle, dual levy, and the institutional role of the GST Council – all of which are frequently tested in both Prelims and Mains.
