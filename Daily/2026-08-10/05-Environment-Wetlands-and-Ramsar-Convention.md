# Wetlands and the Ramsar Convention

## What are Wetlands?

Wetlands are areas where water is the primary factor controlling the environment and the associated plant and animal life. They occur where the water table is at or near the surface of the land or where the land is covered by shallow water.

According to the Ramsar Convention definition (broad and widely accepted), wetlands include areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, whether natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, with water that is static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt, including areas of marine water the depth of which at low tide does not exceed six metres.

**Types of Wetlands**:
- Inland wetlands: marshes, swamps, peatlands, lakes, rivers, floodplains, oases
- Coastal/marine wetlands: mangroves, estuaries, lagoons, coral reefs, intertidal mudflats
- Human-made: reservoirs, ponds, irrigated agricultural land, salt pans, wastewater treatment ponds

## Ecological Importance of Wetlands

Wetlands are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. Their key functions include:

**Hydrological Functions**:
- Water storage and flood regulation (act as sponges, absorbing excess water during rains and releasing it slowly)
- Groundwater recharge and discharge
- Maintenance of water quality through filtration and sediment trapping
- Shoreline stabilisation and erosion control

**Biological Functions**:
- High biodiversity: support a wide range of flora and fauna, including many endemic and threatened species
- Critical habitats for migratory birds (stopover, breeding, and wintering sites)
- Breeding and nursery grounds for fish and other aquatic organisms
- Support for food chains and nutrient cycling

**Climate-Related Functions**:
- Carbon sequestration (especially peatlands and mangroves store large amounts of carbon)
- Microclimate regulation

**Socio-Economic Functions**:
- Fisheries and aquaculture
- Agriculture (recession agriculture in floodplains)
- Water supply
- Recreation, tourism, and cultural/religious values
- Livelihoods for local communities

Despite their importance, wetlands have been among the most threatened ecosystems due to drainage for agriculture and urbanisation, pollution, over-extraction of water, invasive species, and climate change.

## Ramsar Convention on Wetlands

The Convention on Wetlands of International Importance especially as Waterfowl Habitat (Ramsar Convention) was adopted in 1971 at Ramsar, Iran, and came into force in 1975. It is the first modern intergovernmental treaty for the conservation and wise use of a specific ecosystem type.

**Key Features**:
- Parties commit to the “wise use” of all wetlands in their territory (wise use = maintenance of ecological character within the context of sustainable development).
- Each Party designates wetlands of international importance for inclusion in the List of Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar List). Designation is based on criteria related to ecology, botany, zoology, limnology, or hydrology, with special emphasis on waterfowl.
- Parties promote the conservation of listed sites and cooperate on transboundary wetlands and shared water systems.
- The Convention has a Conference of the Parties (COP), a Standing Committee, and a Scientific and Technical Review Panel. The Secretariat is hosted by IUCN in Gland, Switzerland.

**Three Pillars of the Convention**:
1. Wise use of all wetlands
2. Designation and management of Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar Sites)
3. International cooperation

## India and the Ramsar Convention

India became a Party to the Ramsar Convention in 1982. As of recent years, India has a significant number of Ramsar Sites (the number has increased substantially through successive designations), covering diverse wetland types – high-altitude lakes in the Himalayas, floodplain wetlands in the Gangetic and Brahmaputra plains, coastal lagoons and mangroves, and human-made reservoirs.

**Notable Indian Ramsar Sites** (illustrative examples across regions):
- Chilika Lake (Odisha) – one of the largest brackish water lagoons; important for migratory birds and Irrawaddy dolphins
- Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur, Rajasthan) – formerly a duck hunting reserve, now a major waterfowl sanctuary
- Loktak Lake (Manipur) – known for floating phumdis and the only floating national park (Keibul Lamjao)
- Sundarbans (West Bengal) – mangrove ecosystem, shared with Bangladesh
- Wular Lake (Jammu & Kashmir), Tso Moriri, and other high-altitude lakes
- Point Calimere, Gulf of Mannar, and other coastal sites
- Several sites in the Indo-Gangetic plain and Deccan

India has also framed policies and programmes for wetland conservation, including the National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems (NPCA), which subsumed earlier schemes for lakes and wetlands. The Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules provide a regulatory framework for notified wetlands.

## Threats to Wetlands in India

- Encroachment and conversion for agriculture, housing, and infrastructure
- Pollution from domestic, industrial, and agricultural sources (eutrophication is common)
- Unsustainable water extraction and alteration of hydrological regimes (upstream dams, diversion)
- Invasive alien species (e.g., water hyacinth)
- Siltation
- Climate change impacts (altered precipitation, temperature, sea-level rise affecting coastal wetlands)
- Over-exploitation of resources (fish, plants, etc.)

## Conservation Approaches

Effective wetland conservation requires:
- Protection of hydrological regimes (environmental flows, catchment management)
- Pollution control and treatment of inflows
- Prevention of encroachment and regulated land use in the catchment and buffer zones
- Community participation and sustainable livelihood options
- Scientific monitoring of ecological character
- Integration of wetland concerns into river basin and coastal zone management
- International cooperation for shared and migratory species habitats
