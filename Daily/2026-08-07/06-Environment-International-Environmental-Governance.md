# International Environmental Governance

International environmental governance refers to the set of institutions, rules, processes, and mechanisms through which the global community addresses environmental problems that transcend national boundaries. Climate change, biodiversity loss, ozone depletion, desertification, and marine pollution are examples of such problems.

## Key Principles of International Environmental Law

Several principles guide international environmental cooperation:

- **Principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)**: All states share responsibility for environmental protection, but the nature and extent of responsibility differ according to their contribution to the problem and their respective capabilities. This principle is central to the climate regime.
- **Precautionary Principle**: Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures.
- **Polluter Pays Principle**: Those who produce pollution should bear the costs of managing it.
- **Sustainable Development**: Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Inter-generational and Intra-generational Equity**.
- **Public Participation and Access to Information**.

## Major Multilateral Environmental Agreements

### Climate Change Regime

**United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 1992**: The foundational treaty. It recognises climate change as a common concern of humankind and establishes the Conference of the Parties (COP) as the supreme decision-making body.

**Kyoto Protocol, 1997**: Set binding emission reduction targets for developed countries (Annex I parties) for the period 2008–2012. Introduced flexible mechanisms: emissions trading, Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), and Joint Implementation.

**Paris Agreement, 2015**: A landmark agreement under the UNFCCC. Key features include:

- Goal of limiting global temperature rise to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.
- Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) submitted by each party, to be updated every five years with progressive ambition.
- Global stocktake every five years to assess collective progress.
- Enhanced transparency framework.
- Recognition of the need for finance, technology transfer, and capacity-building for developing countries.
- The Agreement is legally binding in terms of process but the content of NDCs is nationally determined.

### Biodiversity Regime

**Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992**: Objectives are conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from genetic resources.

**Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety**: Regulates the transboundary movement of living modified organisms.

**Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing**: Implements the third objective of the CBD.

**Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022)**: Sets targets for 2030, including protection of 30% of land and sea areas, restoration of degraded ecosystems, and reduction of pollution and harmful subsidies.

### Ozone Regime

**Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer, 1985**, and the **Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer, 1987**: Widely regarded as the most successful environmental treaty regime. The Montreal Protocol has led to the phase-out of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and other ozone-depleting substances. The Kigali Amendment (2016) added hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) to the list of controlled substances because of their high global warming potential.

### Other Important Agreements

- **United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), 1994**.
- **Basel Convention** on the control of transboundary movements of hazardous wastes.
- **Stockholm Convention** on persistent organic pollutants.
- **Minamata Convention** on mercury.
- **Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)**.
- **Ramsar Convention** on wetlands of international importance.

## Institutional Architecture

- **United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)**: Coordinates environmental activities within the UN system and assists developing countries.
- **Global Environment Facility (GEF)**: Provides financing for projects related to biodiversity, climate change, international waters, land degradation, and chemicals.
- **Green Climate Fund (GCF)**: Established under the UNFCCC to support mitigation and adaptation in developing countries.
- **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)**: Assesses the science related to climate change. Its Assessment Reports provide the scientific basis for policy.
- **Conference of the Parties (COP)** meetings under various conventions serve as the decision-making forums.

## India’s Position and Engagement

India is a party to the major environmental conventions. Its negotiating stance has consistently emphasised equity, CBDR, the right to development, and the need for finance and technology from developed countries.

India’s Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement include targets for reducing the emissions intensity of GDP, increasing non-fossil fuel capacity, creating additional carbon sinks, and adapting to climate impacts.

India has also launched or joined several initiatives such as the International Solar Alliance, the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure, and LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment).

## Challenges in International Environmental Governance

- Fragmentation of institutions and agreements.
- Insufficient finance relative to needs, especially for adaptation and loss and damage.
- Gaps between commitments and implementation.
- Tensions between development priorities and environmental protection in developing countries.
- Rise of unilateral measures (such as carbon border adjustment mechanisms) that can affect trade.
- Difficulty in addressing consumption patterns and lifestyle issues.

## Conceptual Clarity

International environmental governance operates in a system of sovereign states. Agreements are effective only to the extent that states consent to them and implement them domestically. The principle of CBDR attempts to reconcile the universal nature of environmental problems with the differentiated historical responsibilities and capacities of states.

The shift from the Kyoto Protocol’s top-down, binding targets for a limited set of countries to the Paris Agreement’s bottom-up, nationally determined contributions reflects a change in the political economy of climate negotiations. Understanding this evolution is important for analysing current climate politics.

Science (IPCC), finance (GCF, GEF), technology, and capacity-building form the supporting pillars of the regime. Progress on each of these pillars determines the overall effectiveness of global efforts.
