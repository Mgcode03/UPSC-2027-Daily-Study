# Fiscal Policy and the Budget Process in India

## Meaning of Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy refers to the use of government revenue collection (taxation) and expenditure to influence the economy. It is one of the two main instruments of macroeconomic management, the other being monetary policy (controlled by the Reserve Bank of India).

The objectives of fiscal policy typically include:

- Promoting economic growth
- Maintaining price stability
- Achieving full employment or reducing unemployment
- Ensuring equitable distribution of income and wealth
- Maintaining external balance
- Providing public goods and correcting market failures

In the Indian context, fiscal policy also seeks to promote inclusive development, support infrastructure creation, and maintain fiscal sustainability.

## Components of the Government Budget

The annual financial statement (Budget) is presented under Article 112 of the Constitution. It has two main parts: the Revenue Budget and the Capital Budget.

### Revenue Budget

**Revenue Receipts**:
- Tax revenue: Corporation tax, income tax, goods and services tax (GST), customs duties, excise duties (on a few items), and other taxes.
- Non-tax revenue: Interest receipts, dividends and profits from public sector enterprises, fees, fines, and external grants.

**Revenue Expenditure**: Expenditure that does not create assets. Includes interest payments, salaries, subsidies, pensions, defence revenue expenditure, and grants to states.

### Capital Budget

**Capital Receipts**:
- Debt receipts: Market borrowings, external loans, short-term borrowings.
- Non-debt capital receipts: Recovery of loans, proceeds from disinvestment, and other capital receipts.

**Capital Expenditure**: Expenditure that creates assets or reduces liabilities. Includes loans to states, capital outlay on infrastructure, defence capital expenditure, and equity investments in public enterprises.

## Key Fiscal Deficit Concepts

- **Revenue Deficit**: Excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts. It indicates that the government is borrowing to finance current consumption.
- **Fiscal Deficit**: Excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings. It is the most widely watched indicator of the government’s borrowing requirement.
- **Primary Deficit**: Fiscal deficit minus interest payments. It shows the deficit excluding the burden of past borrowing.
- **Effective Revenue Deficit**: Revenue deficit minus grants for the creation of capital assets. Introduced to distinguish pure consumption deficit from deficit that supports capital formation via grants to states.

## Fiscal Policy Rules and FRBM

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, and subsequent amendments aim to ensure inter-generational equity and fiscal discipline. The Act originally targeted the elimination of revenue deficit and reduction of fiscal deficit to a specified percentage of GDP.

Targets have been revised over time in response to economic conditions, including the global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. Escape clauses allow temporary deviations under specified circumstances such as national security, natural calamities, or structural reforms.

The FRBM framework also requires the government to present medium-term fiscal policy statements, fiscal policy strategy statements, and macroeconomic framework statements along with the Budget.

## The Budget Process

1. **Preparation**: Ministries and departments submit estimates. The Ministry of Finance consolidates and prepares the draft Budget in consultation with the Prime Minister and Cabinet.
2. **Presentation**: The Finance Minister presents the Budget in the Lok Sabha, usually on 1 February (advanced from the traditional last working day of February since 2017).
3. **General Discussion**: Debate on the Budget as a whole.
4. **Departmental Scrutiny**: Demands for Grants are examined by Departmentally Related Standing Committees.
5. **Voting on Demands for Grants**: The Lok Sabha votes on the demands. The Rajya Sabha can discuss but not vote on them.
6. **Appropriation Bill**: Authorises the government to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund of India.
7. **Finance Bill**: Contains the taxation proposals. It must be passed for the tax measures to come into effect.

The Budget is presented in two parts in election years or when a full Budget cannot be presented: an interim Budget or Vote on Account.

## Consolidated Fund, Contingency Fund, and Public Account

- **Consolidated Fund of India** (Article 266): All revenues, loans raised, and money received in repayment of loans. No money can be appropriated without parliamentary approval.
- **Contingency Fund of India** (Article 267): A fund placed at the disposal of the President for unforeseen expenditure. Parliamentary approval is obtained later.
- **Public Account of India**: Transactions where the government acts as a banker (provident funds, small savings, etc.). Parliamentary authorisation is not required for withdrawals in the same way as for the Consolidated Fund.

## Goods and Services Tax and Fiscal Federalism

The introduction of GST in 2017 was a major reform in indirect taxation. It subsumed a large number of central and state taxes into a dual GST (CGST and SGST) with an Integrated GST (IGST) for inter-state supplies.

The GST Council, a constitutional body, makes recommendations on rates, exemptions, and other matters. It is an important institution of cooperative federalism. Decision-making requires a three-fourths majority of weighted votes, giving both the Centre and the states significant influence.

GST has improved tax compliance through the invoice-matching system and has broadened the tax base, though challenges remain regarding rate rationalisation, compensation to states, and the treatment of certain sectors.

## Subsidies and Fiscal Space

Subsidies form a significant part of revenue expenditure. Major subsidies include food, fertiliser, and petroleum. While subsidies serve social and political objectives, they also constrain fiscal space for capital expenditure. Rationalisation of subsidies, better targeting through Direct Benefit Transfer, and reduction of leakages are ongoing policy priorities.

## Conceptual Points

Fiscal policy works with a lag and is subject to political constraints. Crowding-out effects can occur if large government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private investment. Crowding-in can occur if public investment raises the productivity of private capital.

The quality of fiscal adjustment matters: reducing the deficit by cutting capital expenditure may harm long-term growth, whereas reducing unproductive revenue expenditure or raising the efficiency of tax collection is more desirable.

Understanding the distinction between revenue and capital items, the meaning of different deficit concepts, the institutional framework of the FRBM Act, and the role of the GST Council is essential for analysing contemporary budgetary and fiscal issues.
