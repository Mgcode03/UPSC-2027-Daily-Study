# Vice-President of India and the Council of Ministers

## Vice-President of India

The Vice-President of India holds a unique position as the second-highest constitutional office in the country. The office is modelled partly on the American Vice-Presidency but adapted to the parliamentary system.

### Election

The Vice-President is elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament. Unlike the Presidential election, elected members of State Legislative Assemblies do not participate. The election is conducted by the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, and voting is by secret ballot.

Any citizen of India who has completed 35 years of age and is qualified for election as a member of the Rajya Sabha can contest. The Vice-President cannot be a member of either House of Parliament or of a State Legislature at the time of assuming office. If a sitting member is elected, the seat is deemed vacated.

### Term and Removal

The term is five years. The Vice-President may resign by writing to the President. Removal is possible by a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by a majority of all the then members and agreed to by the Lok Sabha. No ground is specified in the Constitution for removal, giving Parliament wide latitude.

### Functions and Powers

The primary function is to act as the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. In this capacity, the Vice-President presides over the sittings of the Upper House, maintains order, decides points of order, and interprets the rules of procedure. The Chairman has a casting vote in case of a tie.

When the President is unable to discharge functions due to absence, illness, or any other cause, or when the office of President falls vacant due to death, resignation, or removal, the Vice-President acts as President. During such periods, the Vice-President does not perform the duties of the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha; those are discharged by the Deputy Chairman.

The Vice-President is not a member of the Cabinet and does not participate in the day-to-day executive functioning of the government. The office is largely ceremonial in the executive domain except when discharging presidential duties.

### Key Distinctions from the President

- The electoral college for the Vice-President excludes state legislators.
- Removal procedure is simpler than the impeachment process for the President.
- The Vice-President has no independent executive powers while the President holds residual executive authority under the Constitution.

## Council of Ministers

Article 74 provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President, who shall, in the exercise of functions, act in accordance with such advice. The 42nd and 44th Amendments clarified that the President is bound by the advice of the Council of Ministers, subject only to the limited power to require reconsideration once.

### Composition and Structure

The Council of Ministers consists of three categories:

1. **Cabinet Ministers**: Senior ministers heading important ministries. They form the core decision-making body known as the Cabinet.
2. **Ministers of State**: May be given independent charge of ministries or departments, or attached to Cabinet Ministers.
3. **Deputy Ministers**: Assist Cabinet or State Ministers; they do not hold independent charge.

The size of the Council is limited by the 91st Constitutional Amendment (2003). The total number of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, cannot exceed 15% of the total strength of the Lok Sabha. A similar limit applies at the state level (15% of the Legislative Assembly strength).

### Appointment and Tenure

The Prime Minister is appointed by the President. Other Ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. All Ministers must be members of Parliament. If a non-member is appointed, that person must secure a seat in either House within six months, failing which the ministership ceases.

Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President, which in practice means the pleasure of the Prime Minister. Collective responsibility under Article 75(3) requires that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. If the Lok Sabha passes a no-confidence motion, the entire Council, including Ministers from the Rajya Sabha, must resign.

### Collective Responsibility vs Individual Responsibility

Collective responsibility means that all Ministers swim or sink together. Decisions of the Cabinet bind every Minister. A Minister who cannot publicly defend a Cabinet decision is expected to resign.

Individual responsibility means that each Minister is answerable for the working of the department or ministry under charge. The Minister can be removed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister without the entire Council having to resign.

### Cabinet vs Council of Ministers

The Cabinet is a smaller body within the Council. It consists of senior Ministers and is the real centre of executive power. The full Council of Ministers rarely meets as a collective body for policy decisions. Most decisions are taken in the Cabinet or in Cabinet Committees.

Cabinet Committees are of two types: standing (permanent) and ad-hoc (temporary). Important standing committees include the Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs, Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, Cabinet Committee on Security, and Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs.

### Role of the Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers, the leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha, and the chief link between the President and the Council. The Prime Minister allocates portfolios, can ask a Minister to resign, and can advise the President to dismiss a Minister. The Prime Minister also chairs the Cabinet and its important committees.

In a coalition government, the Prime Minister’s authority is constrained by coalition partners. Portfolio allocation often reflects political bargaining rather than pure administrative logic.

### Legal and Constitutional Position of Advice

The advice tendered by the Council of Ministers is binding on the President after the 44th Amendment. The President can, however, require the Council to reconsider the advice. Once reconsidered advice is tendered, the President is bound to act upon it.

The courts do not inquire into the nature of advice tendered by Ministers to the President (Article 74(2)). This provides a constitutional shield to the deliberative process within the executive.

### Key Facts to Remember

- Vice-President is the only constitutional functionary who is elected by Parliament alone and not by an electoral college including state representatives.
- The 91st Amendment imposed a numerical ceiling on the size of the Council of Ministers.
- Collective responsibility is to the Lok Sabha only, not to the Rajya Sabha.
- A Minister who is a member of the Rajya Sabha can speak and take part in the proceedings of the Lok Sabha but cannot vote there.
- The principle of collective responsibility was borrowed from the British parliamentary system.

### Conceptual Clarity Points

The Indian executive is parliamentary in form. Real executive power resides in the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, while the President is the formal constitutional head. The Vice-President’s role is primarily legislative (as Chairman of the Rajya Sabha) and residual (as acting President). Understanding the distinction between formal and real executive authority is central to answering questions on the nature of the Indian polity.

The interplay between collective responsibility and individual responsibility ensures both team cohesion and personal accountability. In practice, the Prime Minister’s position has grown stronger over decades, especially under single-party majorities, while coalition eras have distributed power more widely among Cabinet colleagues and alliance partners.
