# Features of Indian Federalism

Indian federalism is often described as a unique blend of federal and unitary features. The Constitution establishes a dual polity with the Union at the centre and the States at the periphery, yet it contains several provisions that tilt the balance in favour of the Centre, especially during emergencies or situations of national importance.

## Federal Features

### Dual Government

There exist two sets of government: one at the Union level and another at the State level. Each is endowed with powers derived from the Constitution itself, not from the other government. This division is the hallmark of a federal system.

### Written and Rigid Constitution

The Constitution is a written document that defines the structure, powers, and functions of both the Union and the States. Amendment of federal provisions generally requires a special majority in Parliament plus ratification by at least half of the State legislatures (Article 368).

### Division of Powers

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative powers into three lists:

- **Union List** (List I): Subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, banking, currency, atomic energy, and interstate trade. Parliament has exclusive power.
- **State List** (List II): Subjects of local and regional importance such as public order, police, public health, agriculture, and local government. State legislatures have exclusive power in normal circumstances.
- **Concurrent List** (List III): Subjects on which both can legislate, such as criminal law, marriage, bankruptcy, education, forests, and protection of wildlife. In case of conflict, Union law prevails (Article 254).

Residuary powers (subjects not enumerated in any list) vest in the Union Parliament under Article 248.

### Supremacy of the Constitution

Neither the Union nor the States can override the Constitution. All organs of government derive their authority from it. The Supreme Court acts as the guardian and interpreter of the Constitution and can strike down laws that violate the federal division of powers.

### Independent Judiciary

An independent judiciary headed by the Supreme Court adjudicates disputes between the Union and States or between States. Article 131 confers original jurisdiction on the Supreme Court in such federal disputes.

### Bicameral Legislature at the Centre

The Rajya Sabha represents the States. Although representation is not equal (it is based roughly on population), the existence of a second chamber with a federal character is a federal feature.

## Unitary or Non-Federal Features

### Strong Centre

The Union List is the longest and most important. Residuary powers lie with the Centre. During emergencies, the federal structure can be transformed into a unitary one.

### Single Constitution

Unlike classical federations such as the United States, India has a single Constitution that applies to both the Union and the States (with limited exceptions for Jammu and Kashmir before 2019 and certain special provisions).

### Single Citizenship

India provides for single citizenship. There is no dual citizenship of the Union and the State.

### Flexibility of the Constitution

Many provisions can be amended by Parliament alone. Only a limited set of federal provisions require State ratification.

### Unequal Representation in the Upper House

States do not have equal representation in the Rajya Sabha. Larger States have more seats. This departs from the classical federal principle of equal state representation.

### Emergency Provisions

Under Articles 352, 356, and 360, the Centre can assume extraordinary powers. During a national emergency, the federal distribution of powers is substantially modified. Under President’s Rule (Article 356), the State executive and legislature can be taken over by the Centre.

### Appointment of Governors

Governors are appointed by the President and hold office during the pleasure of the President. They act as a link between the Centre and the States and can reserve Bills for the consideration of the President.

### Integrated Judiciary and All-India Services

The judiciary is integrated rather than dual. All-India Services (IAS, IPS, IFS) are common to the Union and the States and are controlled ultimately by the Centre.

### Parliament’s Power over State List

In certain situations, Parliament can legislate on State List subjects: when Rajya Sabha passes a resolution under Article 249, during national emergency, when two or more States request, or for implementation of international agreements.

### Absence of Dual Citizenship and Dual Judiciary

These classical federal features are missing.

## Nature of Indian Federalism: Scholarly Views

K.C. Wheare described the Indian Constitution as “quasi-federal”. He observed that it is federal in form but unitary in spirit.

Granville Austin characterised it as “cooperative federalism”, emphasising the need for consultation and collaboration between the Union and the States.

Some scholars prefer the term “centralised federalism” or “federalism with a strong unitary bias”.

In practice, the working of Indian federalism has evolved. Periods of single-party dominance at the Centre witnessed greater centralisation. The rise of regional parties and coalition governments strengthened the bargaining power of States. Institutions such as the Inter-State Council, NITI Aayog, and the GST Council represent mechanisms of cooperative and competitive federalism.

## Competitive and Cooperative Federalism

**Cooperative federalism** stresses collaboration between the Union and States in policy formulation and implementation. Examples include the GST Council (where States have significant voting power), centrally sponsored schemes with state participation, and inter-governmental bodies.

**Competitive federalism** encourages States to compete with each other for investment, better governance rankings, and efficient service delivery. NITI Aayog’s indices on health, education, water, and sustainable development goals promote such competition.

Both models coexist. The balance between them shifts with political configurations and policy priorities.

## Key Constitutional Articles

- Article 1: India as a Union of States
- Articles 245–255: Distribution of legislative powers
- Article 246: Subject-matter of laws made by Parliament and State Legislatures
- Article 248: Residuary powers
- Article 249: Power of Parliament to legislate on State List in national interest
- Article 250: Legislation during emergency
- Article 252: Legislation by consent of States
- Article 254: Inconsistency between Union and State laws
- Article 256–263: Administrative relations
- Article 268–281: Financial relations
- Article 352, 356, 360: Emergency provisions affecting federal balance

## Conceptual Takeaways

Indian federalism is not a carbon copy of the American or Australian models. It was designed to hold together a vast, diverse, and newly independent country facing the challenges of partition, princely state integration, and nation-building. The strong Centre was a deliberate choice of the Constitution-makers.

Over time, political and economic changes have produced a more balanced working arrangement. Understanding both the formal constitutional provisions and the evolving political practice is essential for analysing centre-state relations, the role of Governors, the use of Article 356, the functioning of the GST Council, and contemporary debates on fiscal federalism.

The tension between unity and diversity, between central direction and state autonomy, remains a permanent feature of Indian political life. The Constitution provides the framework; political processes determine the actual balance at any given time.
