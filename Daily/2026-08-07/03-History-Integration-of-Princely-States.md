# Integration of Princely States

At the time of independence in 1947, British India was divided into two categories of territories: the provinces under direct British rule and the princely states under Indian rulers who enjoyed varying degrees of autonomy under British paramountcy. There were approximately 562 princely states covering about 40% of the territory of pre-independence India.

The Indian Independence Act, 1947, ended British paramountcy. The rulers of the princely states were theoretically free to accede to India, accede to Pakistan, or remain independent. The prospect of Balkanisation posed a serious threat to the unity and security of the new nation.

## Role of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V.P. Menon

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as the Minister for States, and his secretary V.P. Menon played the decisive role in the integration process. Their strategy combined persuasion, diplomacy, and, where necessary, the measured use of force.

The Instrument of Accession was the legal document through which a ruler agreed to join the Indian Union. By signing it, the ruler ceded control over defence, external affairs, and communications to the Dominion of India while retaining autonomy in other matters. A Standstill Agreement maintained the existing administrative arrangements until new arrangements could be worked out.

## Process of Integration

### Accession of Most States

By 15 August 1947, the vast majority of states had signed the Instrument of Accession. Patel and Menon used a combination of personal meetings, letters, and the implicit threat of popular movements or isolation to secure signatures. The rulers were assured that their personal privileges, privy purses, and limited autonomy would be respected initially.

### Difficult Cases

Several states presented special problems.

**Junagadh**: The Nawab was Muslim while the population was overwhelmingly Hindu. The Nawab announced accession to Pakistan. India rejected the accession as it violated the principle of geographical contiguity and popular will. A provisional government was formed by the people, Indian troops entered, and a plebiscite was held in February 1948 in which the population voted overwhelmingly to join India.

**Hyderabad**: The largest princely state, ruled by the Nizam, a Muslim sovereign over a predominantly Hindu population. The Nizam sought independence and delayed accession. Communal tensions rose, and the Razakar militia created disorder. In September 1948, the Indian government launched “Operation Polo”, a police action that lasted a few days. The Nizam surrendered, and Hyderabad was integrated into India. A subsequent plebiscite confirmed the accession.

**Kashmir**: The Maharaja, Hari Singh, was Hindu while the majority population was Muslim. He initially hoped to remain independent. In October 1947, tribal invaders from Pakistan, supported by elements of the Pakistani army, entered Kashmir. The Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947 in return for military assistance. Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar. The conflict led to the first India-Pakistan war and the involvement of the United Nations. The question of Kashmir remained complex and unresolved in subsequent decades.

**Other States**: States such as Travancore initially contemplated independence but were persuaded to accede. Jodhpur’s ruler briefly considered Pakistan under inducements but was brought into the Indian fold through firm diplomacy.

## Administrative Integration and Democratisation

Accession was only the first step. Full administrative and political integration followed.

Many small states were merged into larger administrative units or neighbouring provinces. Examples include the merger of numerous states in Orissa, the Central Provinces, and the formation of unions such as the United State of Rajasthan, Madhya Bharat, PEPSU (Patiala and East Punjab States Union), and Saurashtra.

The process of democratisation involved the introduction of responsible government. Popular ministries replaced the personal rule of the princes. The Constituent Assembly of India included representatives from the states, and the Constitution of India ultimately applied uniformly.

Privy purses and privileges were guaranteed under the original Constitution as a quid pro quo for the rulers’ cooperation. These were later abolished by the 26th Constitutional Amendment in 1971.

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

Article 1 of the Constitution describes India as a Union of States. The states and territories are specified in the First Schedule. The integration of princely states was reflected in the gradual reorganisation of this Schedule.

The States Reorganisation Act, 1956, later redrew boundaries on linguistic lines, further transforming the map that had emerged from the integration process.

## Significance

The successful integration of the princely states was one of the most significant achievements of the early years of independent India. It prevented the fragmentation of the subcontinent into dozens of independent or semi-independent units. It created a contiguous territorial base for the Indian Union and enabled the establishment of a unified administrative, legal, and economic system.

The process demonstrated the effectiveness of a combination of firmness and flexibility. Where popular will and geographical logic were clear, accession was achieved with relative ease. Where rulers resisted, the government used a calibrated mix of political pressure and, in extreme cases, limited military action.

The integration also raised important questions about the nature of federalism, the rights of former rulers, and the balance between national unity and regional identity. These questions continued to influence Indian politics in subsequent decades through demands for statehood, autonomy, and special status.

## Key Personalities

- **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**: Political leadership, strategic direction, and personal authority.
- **V.P. Menon**: Administrative skill, drafting of instruments, and detailed negotiations.
- **Lord Mountbatten**: As the last Viceroy and first Governor-General, he supported the process of accession to India or Pakistan and discouraged independence.
- **Local leaders and popular movements**: In several states, public pressure and political organisations of the people accelerated the process of accession and democratisation.

## Conceptual Points for Clarity

The integration of princely states was not merely a cartographic exercise. It involved the transfer of sovereignty, the dismantling of autocratic regimes, the extension of democratic institutions, and the creation of a single political community. The speed and relative smoothness of the process, despite the complexity of the problem, remain a landmark in the history of nation-building in the modern world.

Understanding the distinction between accession (transfer of limited sovereignty) and subsequent full integration (administrative, political, and constitutional merger) is important. Equally important is the recognition that while the Instrument of Accession was a legal document, the real force behind integration was the combination of political will at the Centre, the aspiration of the people in the states for democratic governance, and the practical impossibility of sustained independence for most of the smaller states.
