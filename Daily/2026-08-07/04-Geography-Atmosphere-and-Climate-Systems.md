# Atmosphere and Climate Systems

## Structure of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is a thin envelope of gases surrounding the Earth, held by gravity. It is divided into layers based on temperature variation with height.

### Troposphere

Extends from the surface to about 8–18 km (higher at the equator, lower at the poles). Temperature decreases with height at an average rate of 6.5°C per kilometre (environmental lapse rate). Almost all weather phenomena—clouds, rainfall, storms—occur in this layer. It contains about 75–80% of the total atmospheric mass and nearly all water vapour.

### Stratosphere

Extends from the tropopause to about 50 km. Temperature increases with height due to absorption of ultraviolet radiation by ozone. The ozone layer, concentrated between 15 and 35 km, protects life from harmful UV radiation. The stratosphere is relatively stable and lacks significant vertical mixing.

### Mesosphere

From about 50 km to 80–85 km. Temperature decreases with height, reaching the coldest temperatures in the atmosphere (around –90°C). Meteors burn up in this layer.

### Thermosphere

Above 80–85 km. Temperature rises sharply with height due to absorption of high-energy solar radiation. The International Space Station orbits in the lower thermosphere. The ionosphere, a region of ionised gases within the thermosphere, is important for radio communication.

### Exosphere

The outermost layer, gradually merging into interplanetary space. Molecules can escape Earth’s gravity.

## Composition of the Atmosphere

Dry air consists mainly of nitrogen (about 78%) and oxygen (about 21%). Argon is about 0.93%. Carbon dioxide is approximately 0.04% but is rising due to human activity. Trace gases include neon, helium, methane, krypton, and hydrogen.

Water vapour is a variable component, ranging from near zero in cold, dry regions to about 4% in warm, humid tropical air. It plays a crucial role in weather and climate through the hydrological cycle and the greenhouse effect.

Aerosols—tiny solid or liquid particles—affect radiation balance and act as condensation nuclei for cloud formation.

## Heating of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is heated primarily from below. Solar radiation (insolation) passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the Earth’s surface. The surface then radiates long-wave terrestrial radiation, which is absorbed by greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, etc.), warming the lower atmosphere.

Processes of heat transfer:

- **Conduction**: Direct transfer of heat from the surface to the air in contact with it. Important only in a thin layer near the ground.
- **Convection**: Vertical movement of air. Warm air rises, expands, cools, and may form clouds. This is the primary mechanism of heat transfer in the troposphere.
- **Advection**: Horizontal transfer of heat by winds. Responsible for heat transport from low to high latitudes and for temperature changes associated with air-mass movements.
- **Radiation**: Transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves. The Earth-atmosphere system is in approximate radiative balance over long periods.

## Atmospheric Pressure and Winds

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the column of air above a unit area. It decreases with height. Horizontal differences in pressure create pressure gradients that drive winds.

The pressure gradient force acts from high to low pressure. The Coriolis force, resulting from Earth’s rotation, deflects winds to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Friction near the surface slows winds and affects direction.

At the surface, winds cross isobars at an angle; in the upper atmosphere (geostrophic balance), winds blow parallel to isobars.

### Global Pressure Belts and Wind Systems

- Equatorial low-pressure belt (doldrums): Intense heating causes air to rise.
- Subtropical high-pressure belts (horse latitudes) around 30° N and S: Descending air.
- Subpolar low-pressure belts around 60° N and S.
- Polar high-pressure areas.

Planetary winds:

- Trade winds: Blow from subtropical highs toward the equatorial low (northeast trades in the Northern Hemisphere, southeast trades in the Southern).
- Westerlies: Blow from subtropical highs toward subpolar lows.
- Polar easterlies: Blow from polar highs toward subpolar lows.

These systems shift seasonally with the apparent movement of the sun.

## Monsoon as a Special Wind System

The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction, most prominently developed over the Indian subcontinent. It is driven by differential heating of land and sea, the shift of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), the presence of the Himalayas, and the role of the Tibetan Plateau as a heat source in summer.

In summer, low pressure develops over northwest India and the Tibetan Plateau. Moisture-laden winds from the Indian Ocean are drawn inland, producing the southwest monsoon. In winter, high pressure over the land leads to northeast winds that are generally dry, except where they pick up moisture from the Bay of Bengal and cause rainfall on the southeast coast.

## Climate Controls

The climate of a place is determined by:

- Latitude (angle of solar radiation)
- Altitude
- Distance from the sea (continentality)
- Ocean currents
- Prevailing winds
- Relief (orographic effects)
- Presence of mountain barriers

## Classification of Climates

The Köppen classification is widely used. It is based on temperature and precipitation and their seasonal distribution. Major groups include tropical rainy climates (A), dry climates (B), temperate climates (C), continental climates (D), and polar climates (E). Each group is further subdivided.

For India, the dominant climate type is tropical monsoon, with significant regional variations: tropical wet, tropical wet and dry, semi-arid, arid, humid subtropical, and mountain climates.

## Key Concepts to Memorise

- Environmental lapse rate: approximately 6.5°C/km in the troposphere.
- Ozone concentration peak in the lower stratosphere.
- Greenhouse effect is a natural process; enhanced greenhouse effect due to anthropogenic emissions causes global warming.
- ITCZ is a zone of converging trade winds and rising air near the equator; its seasonal shift is central to monsoon dynamics.
- Jet streams are high-speed upper-air westerly winds that influence surface weather systems, including the monsoon and western disturbances.

## Conceptual Linkages

Understanding atmospheric structure and processes is essential for explaining weather phenomena, the distribution of climatic regions, the functioning of the monsoon, the causes and consequences of climate change, and the occurrence of extreme events such as cyclones, heat waves, and droughts. These topics link geography with environment, disaster management, and current affairs related to climate policy.
