# Indian Social Structure: Foundational Concepts

## Understanding Social Structure

Social structure refers to the relatively stable patterns of social relationships, institutions, and hierarchies that organise society. In the Indian context, several institutions and principles have historically shaped and continue to influence social organisation: the caste system, the joint family, village community, kinship, religion, and, in modern times, class, gender, and market relations.

## Caste System: Core Features

The caste system is a form of social stratification characterised by:

- **Heredity**: Membership is determined by birth.
- **Endogamy**: Marriage within the group is prescribed.
- **Occupational association**: Traditional link between caste and occupation, though this has weakened.
- **Hierarchy**: Castes are ranked in a status hierarchy, traditionally associated with notions of purity and pollution.
- **Commensality rules**: Restrictions on eating together and accepting food from certain groups.

The classical textual model of four varnas—Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra—provided a broad ideological framework. In practice, the operative units have been the thousands of jatis (local endogamous groups) that vary by region.

### Concepts Associated with Caste

- **Sanskritisation**: A process described by M.N. Srinivas whereby a lower caste adopts the customs, rituals, and way of life of a higher caste in order to improve its status. It is a form of cultural mobility within the caste framework.
- **Dominant Caste**: Also associated with Srinivas. A caste that is numerically strong, controls significant land, and occupies a decisive position in the local power structure, even if it is not the highest in ritual ranking.
- **Westernisation and Modernisation**: Processes that have introduced new sources of status (education, occupation in the modern economy, political power) and have weakened some traditional restrictions.

### Constitutional and Legal Position

The Constitution abolishes untouchability (Article 17) and prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (Article 15). It provides for affirmative action through reservations in education, employment, and political representation for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes.

Despite legal equality, caste continues to influence marriage, social networks, political mobilisation, and, in some areas, access to resources and dignity.

## Family and Kinship

The traditional Indian family has often been described as joint or extended. Characteristics of the ideal joint family include co-residence, common property, common worship, and the authority of the senior male.

In reality, family forms have always varied by region, caste, class, and community. Nuclear households have increased with urbanisation, migration, and the growth of the modern economy. Yet kinship obligations, emotional ties, and mutual support often persist even when co-residence declines.

Kinship systems in India include both patriarchal and, in some communities (particularly in the northeast and among certain southern groups), matrilineal elements. Rules of inheritance, residence after marriage (patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal), and descent vary.

## Village Community

Historically, the village was an important unit of social and economic organisation. Features often associated with the traditional village included relative self-sufficiency, a local hierarchy of castes performing interdependent functions (the jajmani system in some regions), and institutions of local dispute resolution.

Modern forces—market penetration, state institutions, political parties, education, media, and migration—have transformed village life. The village remains significant as a site of residence, agriculture, and local politics, but it is no longer an isolated or self-contained unit.

## Class and Economic Stratification

Alongside caste, class has become an increasingly important axis of stratification. Class is based primarily on economic criteria: ownership of assets, occupation, income, and education. In rural areas, the distribution of land remains a major determinant of class position. In urban areas, occupation in the formal or informal sector, education, and access to capital shape class location.

Caste and class often overlap but are not identical. There are prosperous members of lower castes and economically weak members of higher castes. Political mobilisation sometimes occurs along caste lines, sometimes along class lines, and often through a combination of both.

## Gender and Social Structure

Gender is a fundamental principle of social organisation. Patriarchal norms have historically shaped property rights, decision-making authority, division of labour, and access to education and public space. Women’s labour, both paid and unpaid, has been central to the economy, yet often undervalued.

Constitutional guarantees of equality, legal reforms (concerning marriage, inheritance, violence, and workplace rights), education, and women’s political participation have brought significant changes. Persistent challenges include gender-based violence, unequal property ownership, under-representation in higher economic and political positions, and the burden of unpaid care work.

## Religion and Secularism in Social Life

India is a multi-religious society. Religion provides meaning, community, and ethical guidance for large numbers of people. It also intersects with politics, identity, and social conflict.

The Indian constitutional framework is secular in the sense that the state does not establish an official religion and is expected to treat all religions with equal respect. In social practice, religious identity remains a powerful marker and can become a basis for mobilisation as well as for prejudice.

## Continuity and Change

Indian social structure is characterised by both continuity and change. Institutions such as caste and family have shown remarkable resilience, adapting to new economic and political conditions rather than simply disappearing. At the same time, urbanisation, education, democratic politics, legal equality, and market forces have opened new avenues of mobility and have challenged traditional hierarchies.

Understanding Indian society requires attention to regional variation, the interplay of multiple identities (caste, class, gender, religion, language, region), and the gap that often exists between formal rules and actual social practice.

## Key Thinkers and Concepts for Reference

- M.N. Srinivas: Sanskritisation, dominant caste, westernisation.
- André Béteille: Work on caste, class, and power; distinction between harmonic and disharmonic social systems.
- Louis Dumont: Ideology of purity and pollution and the holistic nature of the caste system (Homo Hierarchicus).
- G.S. Ghurye: Early systematic study of caste.
- Contemporary approaches emphasise intersectionality, the role of the state, and the impact of globalisation.
