# Society: Communalism, Secularism and Social Harmony in India

## Understanding Communalism

Communalism in the Indian context refers to a politics and ideology that bases identity and political mobilisation primarily on religious community, often in opposition to other religious communities. It involves the belief that people of the same religion have common secular interests (economic, political, social) that are different from, and often opposed to, the interests of people of other religions.

Communalism is distinct from religiosity or orthodoxy. A deeply religious person need not be communal; conversely, communal mobilisation can occur among people who are not particularly devout. Communalism becomes particularly dangerous when it leads to the construction of the "other" as an enemy and legitimises violence or discrimination.

### Stages or Forms of Communalism (analytical framework often used in academic discussion)

1. **Mild or liberal communalism**: Assertion of religious community identity within a broader nationalist or democratic framework; demand for safeguards or representation.
2. **Strong or extreme communalism**: The belief that religious communities have irreconcilable interests and that the political system should be organised primarily around religious communities (in its extreme form, the demand for separate nations).
3. **Communal violence**: The outbreak of riots, pogroms, or targeted violence based on communal identity.

These are not rigid stages but analytical distinctions.

## Historical Roots

Communal identities and tensions have complex historical roots:

- Pre-colonial society had multiple overlapping identities (caste, region, sect, occupation). Religious boundaries were often fluid.
- Colonial policies of enumeration (census), separate electorates, and the framing of history in religious terms contributed to the solidification of religious community identities.
- The politics of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the growth of both nationalist and communal organisations. The partition of India in 1947 was the most tragic expression of communal politics, accompanied by massive violence and displacement.
- Post-independence, communal violence has recurred in various forms — riots in the 1960s–80s, the demolition of the Babri Masjid and subsequent violence in 1992–93, the Gujarat violence of 2002, and other incidents. Each episode has its specific triggers, local dynamics, and political contexts.

## Secularism in the Indian Context

Indian secularism differs in important respects from the classical Western model of strict separation of church and state.

### Features of Indian Secularism

1. **Equal respect and equal distance**: The state maintains a principled distance from all religions. It can intervene in religious practices if they violate constitutional values (for example, reforms against untouchability, temple entry, or gender discrimination), but it does not privilege any one religion.

2. **Freedom of religion**: Articles 25–28 guarantee freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, and health, and to the other provisions of Part III.

3. **No state religion**: The Constitution does not establish any religion as the state religion.

4. **Protective discrimination and reform**: The state can undertake social reform and can make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes, which may intersect with religious communities in complex ways.

5. **Positive secularism**: Indian secularism is often described as positive or inclusive — it is not anti-religious but seeks to ensure that religion does not become a basis for exclusion or domination in the public-political sphere.

### Constitutional Provisions

- Preamble: "Secular" was added by the 42nd Amendment (1976); the Supreme Court has held that secularism is part of the basic structure.
- Articles 25–28: Freedom of religion.
- Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
- Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment.
- Article 29–30: Cultural and educational rights of minorities.
- Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties also reinforce the spirit of harmony and common brotherhood.

### Judicial Interpretation

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed secularism as a basic feature of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, S.R. Bommai, and subsequent cases). In S.R. Bommai, the Court held that secularism means that the state shall not discriminate against any citizen on the ground of religion and that politics and religion should not be mixed in a manner that undermines the secular character of the state.

## Challenges to Secularism and Social Harmony

1. **Communal political mobilisation**: Use of religious identity for electoral gains.
2. **Hate speech and polarisation**: Amplified by social media and sections of the media.
3. **Discrimination and exclusion**: In housing, employment, and access to services, often subtle and difficult to prove.
4. **Majoritarianism and minority insecurity**: Perceptions (and sometimes realities) of majoritarian dominance and minority vulnerability feed mutual distrust.
5. **Weak institutional response**: Delayed or biased investigation and prosecution of communal crimes; inadequate riot-control preparedness in some instances.
6. **Educational and cultural narratives**: Contested histories and stereotypes transmitted through informal and formal channels.

## Pathways to Social Harmony

- **Rule of law and equal protection**: Swift, fair, and visible justice in cases of communal violence and hate crimes.
- **Institutional neutrality**: Police, administration, and educational institutions must act in a non-partisan manner.
- **Inter-community dialogue and civil society initiatives**: People-to-people contact, shared cultural spaces, and joint social action reduce prejudice.
- **Economic and social inclusion**: Addressing material inequalities that can be exploited by communal narratives.
- **Responsible political leadership and media**: Avoiding dog-whistle politics and sensationalism.
- **Education for critical thinking and constitutional values**: Curricula and pedagogy that promote scientific temper, empathy, and constitutional morality.
- **Strengthening of institutions**: National and state human rights commissions, minority commissions, and local peace committees.

## Conceptual Understanding for UPSC

Communalism and secularism are not merely abstract ideas; they shape the everyday experience of citizenship, security, and belonging. Indian secularism is a distinctive response to a deeply diverse society. Its success depends on the continuous practice of constitutional morality by the state and by citizens. Social harmony is not the absence of difference but the presence of institutional and cultural mechanisms that allow differences to coexist without descending into domination or violence.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Communalism: politics based on religious community identity, often oppositional.
- Indian secularism: principled distance, equal respect, freedom of religion, no state religion; positive and reformist.
- "Secular" in Preamble (42nd Amendment); basic structure doctrine.
- Articles 25–28: freedom of religion; Articles 15, 16, 29, 30: equality and minority rights.
- S.R. Bommai: secularism as basic feature; politics-religion separation in state action.
- Harmony requires rule of law, institutional neutrality, dialogue, inclusion, and constitutional education.
