# Polity: Governor's Discretionary Powers and Role in Federalism

## Introduction to the Office of the Governor

The Governor is the constitutional head of a state in India, appointed by the President under Article 155 of the Constitution. Article 153 provides for a Governor for each state, though the same person can be appointed as Governor for two or more states. The office is modelled on the British colonial system but adapted to the federal parliamentary framework of independent India.

The Governor is expected to act as a link between the Union and the State. While the President is the formal head of the Union, the Governor occupies an analogous position at the state level. However, the Governor's role has unique features because of the federal structure and the possibility of different political parties controlling the Centre and the states.

## Constitutional Position

Article 154 vests the executive power of the state in the Governor, to be exercised by him either directly or through officers subordinate to him. Article 163 provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, except in so far as he is by or under the Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion.

This "except" clause is the foundation of the Governor's discretionary powers. The Constitution deliberately left certain areas where the Governor is not bound by the advice of the Council of Ministers.

## Discretionary Powers of the Governor

The discretionary powers of the Governor can be classified into two broad categories: constitutional discretion and situational discretion.

### Constitutional Discretion

These are powers explicitly or implicitly recognised by the Constitution as exercisable by the Governor in his discretion:

1. **Reservation of Bills for the consideration of the President (Article 200)**: When a Bill is presented to the Governor after being passed by the state legislature, he may give assent, withhold assent, return the Bill (if it is not a Money Bill), or reserve it for the consideration of the President. Reservation is mandatory in certain cases, such as when a Bill endangers the position of the High Court, or when it deals with subjects that require Presidential assent under the Constitution (for example, Bills relating to the acquisition of property under Article 31A, or Bills affecting the powers of the High Court).

2. **Recommendation for the imposition of President's Rule (Article 356)**: The Governor reports to the President if he is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. This report forms the basis for the imposition of President's Rule. The Supreme Court in the S.R. Bommai case (1994) held that the Governor's report is subject to judicial review and that the floor test is the proper method to determine majority support.

3. **Seeking information from the Chief Minister (Article 167)**: The Governor can call for any information relating to the administration of the affairs of the state and proposals for legislation. The Chief Minister is constitutionally obliged to furnish such information.

4. **Appointment of the Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority**: In a hung assembly, the Governor has the discretion to invite the leader of the largest party or a coalition that claims majority support. The constitutional convention is to invite the leader who is most likely to command the confidence of the House, and a floor test should be held at the earliest opportunity.

5. **Dismissal of a Ministry**: The Governor can dismiss a Ministry if it loses the confidence of the House and refuses to resign. However, this power is to be exercised only after the loss of confidence is established on the floor of the House.

### Situational Discretion

These arise from the practical working of the Constitution and political circumstances:

- Determining the quantum of majority support in a hung assembly.
- Deciding the timing of the floor test.
- Deciding whether to prorogue or dissolve the assembly on the advice of a Chief Minister who has lost majority.
- Reserving Bills that appear to be ultra vires or contrary to the national interest.

## Judicial Interpretation and Limitations

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Governor is not an agent of the Centre and that discretionary powers must be exercised in a constitutional manner, not for political considerations.

Key cases:

- **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)**: Laid down that the majority of a government must be tested on the floor of the House. The Governor's report under Article 356 is justiciable. Dissolution of the assembly should be the last resort.

- **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)**: The dissolution of the Bihar Assembly on the basis of the Governor's report was held unconstitutional. The Court emphasised that the Governor cannot act on mere speculation about horse-trading.

- **Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)**: The Governor cannot interfere with the functioning of the legislature or prevent the Speaker from conducting a floor test.

- **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)**: The Governor is a constitutional head and must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in the limited areas of discretion.

## Role in Federalism

The Governor is often described as a "linchpin of the federal structure". His dual role creates tension:

- As the constitutional head of the state, he is expected to act in the interests of the state.
- As an appointee of the Centre, he is sometimes perceived as an agent of the Union government.

This tension becomes acute when different political parties are in power at the Centre and in the state. The Sarkaria Commission (1988), the Punchhi Commission (2010), and various Supreme Court judgments have recommended that the Governor should be a person of eminence, non-partisan, and that the appointment process should involve consultation with the Chief Minister of the state.

The Punchhi Commission suggested a fixed tenure of five years for the Governor and that he should not be removed except on grounds of proven misbehaviour or incapacity after an inquiry by a panel of judges.

## Practical Challenges

1. **Political appointments**: Many Governors have been active politicians of the ruling party at the Centre, leading to perceptions of bias.
2. **Frequent transfers and removals**: Governors are often shifted or asked to resign when the party at the Centre changes, undermining the dignity of the office.
3. **Interference in day-to-day administration**: Some Governors have sought to control universities, local bodies, or police administration beyond their constitutional role.
4. **Delay in assent to Bills**: Reservation of Bills for long periods without clear reasons creates legislative uncertainty.

## Recommendations for Reform

- Transparent and consultative process for appointment of Governors involving the Chief Minister and a collegium or panel.
- Fixed tenure with removal only through a quasi-judicial process.
- Clear guidelines on the exercise of discretionary powers, especially regarding reservation of Bills and reports under Article 356.
- Strengthening the Inter-State Council as a forum for resolving Centre-State and inter-state issues so that the Governor's role becomes less confrontational.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Article 153: Governor for each state (or more than one).
- Article 155: Appointment by the President.
- Article 156: Tenure at the pleasure of the President; normally five years.
- Article 163: Council of Ministers to aid and advise; discretion exception.
- Article 200: Assent, withholding, return, or reservation of Bills.
- Article 356: Report for President's Rule.
- S.R. Bommai (1994): Floor test is the proper method; judicial review of Article 356.
- Governor is a constitutional head, not an agent of the Centre in the strict sense, but the appointment process creates a structural link.

## Conceptual Understanding

The discretionary powers of the Governor are necessary safety valves in a federal system with a strong parliamentary tradition. They prevent constitutional breakdown and protect the larger national interest. However, their misuse can undermine democratic federalism. The balance between the two is maintained through judicial oversight, constitutional conventions, and the political maturity of the actors involved.

The office of the Governor, when occupied by persons of integrity and constitutional wisdom, can serve as a stabilising force. When occupied by partisan actors, it becomes a source of friction. The evolution of this office continues to shape the practical working of Indian federalism.
