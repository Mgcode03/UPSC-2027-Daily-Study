# Geography: Tropical Cyclones, Western Disturbances and Indian Weather Systems

## Overview of Indian Weather Systems

India's climate is dominated by the monsoon system, but several other weather phenomena significantly influence rainfall, temperature, and extreme events. Two of the most important are tropical cyclones that form over the Indian Ocean and the Bay of Bengal, and western disturbances that originate in the Mediterranean and West Asia. Understanding these systems is essential for disaster management, agriculture, and climate studies.

## Tropical Cyclones

### Definition and Formation

A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating storm system characterised by a low-pressure centre, strong winds, and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms that produce heavy rain. In the North Indian Ocean, these systems are called cyclones (in the Atlantic they are hurricanes, in the western Pacific typhoons).

Favourable conditions for formation (generally following the criteria identified by scientists such as Palmen and Gray):

1. Warm sea surface temperature (usually above 26.5°C to a depth of about 60 metres).
2. Coriolis force sufficient to provide rotation (hence rare within about 5° of the equator).
3. Pre-existing low-level disturbance or cyclonic vorticity.
4. Low vertical wind shear so that the storm can organise vertically.
5. High humidity in the mid-troposphere.
6. Conditional instability of the atmosphere.

The energy of the cyclone comes from the latent heat released when moist air rises and condenses.

### Structure

- **Eye**: The relatively calm, clear centre of the storm, typically 10–50 km in diameter, with descending air.
- **Eyewall**: The ring of intense thunderstorms surrounding the eye; the region of strongest winds and heaviest rainfall.
- **Spiral rainbands**: Bands of clouds and thunderstorms that spiral inward toward the eyewall.
- **Outflow**: Upper-level divergence that helps maintain the low pressure at the surface.

### Life Cycle

Stages typically include: tropical disturbance → tropical depression → tropical storm → severe cyclonic storm / very severe cyclonic storm → extremely severe or super cyclonic storm (as per IMD classification). The system weakens when it moves over land or into cooler waters, or when wind shear increases.

### IMD Classification of Cyclones (North Indian Ocean)

- Depression: 17–27 knots
- Deep Depression: 28–33 knots
- Cyclonic Storm: 34–47 knots
- Severe Cyclonic Storm: 48–63 knots
- Very Severe Cyclonic Storm: 64–89 knots
- Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm: 90–119 knots
- Super Cyclonic Storm: 120 knots and above

### Seasonal and Regional Patterns

The North Indian Ocean has two cyclone seasons:

- Pre-monsoon (April–June)
- Post-monsoon (October–December), which is more intense and accounts for the majority of severe cyclones.

The Bay of Bengal produces more cyclones than the Arabian Sea because of higher sea surface temperatures, greater moisture availability, and more favourable atmospheric conditions. However, Arabian Sea cyclones have increased in intensity in recent decades, partly linked to climate change and rising sea surface temperatures.

Major historical cyclones that have affected India include the 1970 Bhola cyclone (then East Pakistan), the 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone, Cyclone Phailin (2013), Hudhud (2014), Fani (2019), Amphan (2020), Tauktae (2021), and Mocha (2023). These events cause storm surges, extreme rainfall, flooding, and wind damage, particularly along the eastern coast (Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal) and increasingly along the western coast.

### Impacts and Management

Impacts include loss of life, destruction of infrastructure, agricultural damage, saline intrusion into coastal aquifers, and disruption of livelihoods. India has significantly improved its cyclone warning system through the India Meteorological Department (IMD), Doppler radars, satellite monitoring, and coastal multi-hazard warning systems. Evacuation protocols, cyclone shelters, and coastal embankments have reduced mortality dramatically compared to earlier decades.

## Western Disturbances

### Origin and Nature

Western disturbances are extratropical storms that originate in the Mediterranean region or the Caspian Sea and move eastward across Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan into northern India. They are carried by the subtropical westerly jet stream.

Unlike tropical cyclones, western disturbances are associated with frontal systems and derive energy from horizontal temperature gradients rather than latent heat from warm oceans.

### Characteristics and Effects

- They bring winter rainfall (December–March) to the northwestern parts of India — Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, western Uttar Pradesh, and the western Himalayas.
- In the Himalayas, they cause snowfall, which is crucial for the health of glaciers and for the summer flow of rivers originating from the region.
- They moderate the winter cold in the plains; active western disturbances can raise temperatures temporarily.
- Excessive activity can cause flooding, landslides, and avalanches in the hills, and damage to the rabi crop (especially wheat) if accompanied by untimely heavy rain or hail.
- Weak western disturbances may lead to colder conditions and fog in the plains.

Western disturbances are also responsible for the occasional winter rainfall in the Gangetic plains and can interact with other systems to produce complex weather patterns.

### Significance for Agriculture

The rabi crop, particularly wheat in the northwestern plains, depends significantly on moisture from western disturbances. Timely and moderate western disturbances support good yields; excessive or untimely ones can cause lodging, disease, and harvest losses.

## Other Related Weather Systems

### Monsoon Depressions and Low-Pressure Areas

During the southwest monsoon, low-pressure areas and monsoon depressions form over the Bay of Bengal and move northwestward, bringing widespread rainfall to the monsoon core zone. These are distinct from tropical cyclones but share some dynamical features.

### Cloud Bursts and Localised Extreme Rainfall

In the Himalayas and Western Ghats, orographic lifting combined with moisture-laden winds can produce extremely intense short-duration rainfall (cloudbursts), leading to flash floods and landslides.

### Heat Waves and Cold Waves

These are temperature extremes influenced by large-scale circulation patterns, including the position of the subtropical ridge and the passage or absence of western disturbances.

## Climate Change Linkages

Rising sea surface temperatures in the Indian Ocean are linked to an increase in the intensity of cyclones, even if the total number may not show a clear upward trend in all basins. The Arabian Sea has shown a more pronounced increase in intense cyclones. Changes in the subtropical jet stream may affect the frequency and intensity of western disturbances. Understanding these linkages is critical for long-term disaster risk reduction and adaptation planning.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Tropical cyclone formation requires warm SST (>26.5°C), Coriolis force, low shear, and moisture.
- Eye, eyewall, and rainbands form the basic structure.
- IMD scale: Depression → Super Cyclonic Storm.
- Bay of Bengal more active than Arabian Sea; post-monsoon season more severe.
- Western disturbances: extratropical systems from Mediterranean; winter rain and snow to northwest India and Himalayas.
- Crucial for rabi agriculture and Himalayan snowpack.
- Improved early warning has drastically reduced cyclone mortality in India.

## Conceptual Understanding

Indian weather is the product of multiple interacting systems operating at different scales — planetary (monsoon, jet streams), synoptic (cyclones, western disturbances, monsoon depressions), and mesoscale (thunderstorms, cloudbursts). Mastery of these concepts enables better interpretation of weather forecasts, understanding of regional climatic differences, and appreciation of the challenges of disaster management in a monsoon-dominated, cyclone-prone, and topographically diverse country.
