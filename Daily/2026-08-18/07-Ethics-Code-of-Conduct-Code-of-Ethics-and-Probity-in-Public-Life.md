# Ethics: Code of Conduct, Code of Ethics and Probity in Public Life

## Understanding Codes in Public Service

Public servants exercise significant power and discretion that affect the lives of citizens. To guide their behaviour and maintain public trust, formal instruments such as Codes of Conduct and Codes of Ethics are developed. Although the two terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they have distinct emphases.

### Code of Conduct

A Code of Conduct is a set of specific rules and standards that prescribe acceptable and unacceptable behaviour. It tends to be more rule-based, detailed, and enforceable. Violations can attract disciplinary action. Examples include restrictions on accepting gifts, rules about conflict of interest, norms regarding political activity, and requirements of financial disclosure.

### Code of Ethics

A Code of Ethics articulates the broader values and principles that should guide decision-making. It is more aspirational and principle-based. It addresses questions of integrity, impartiality, accountability, compassion, and commitment to public service. While it may not always be directly enforceable in a legal sense, it shapes organisational culture and individual conscience.

In practice, many organisations combine both into a single document or maintain complementary instruments.

## Sources of Ethical Guidance for Indian Civil Servants

1. **Constitution of India**: The Preamble, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and Fundamental Duties provide the foundational value framework. Values such as justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, secularism, and the dignity of the individual are constitutional ideals that civil servants are expected to uphold.

2. **Conduct Rules**: The Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, and corresponding All India Services and state service rules lay down specific do's and don'ts. These cover political neutrality, acceptance of gifts, public demonstrations, criticism of government, and financial propriety.

3. **Code of Ethics / Values statements**: Various committees and the Department of Personnel and Training have articulated lists of values. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC) emphasised values such as integrity, impartiality, commitment to public service, empathy, and dedication to the Constitution.

4. **Judicial pronouncements and CVC guidelines**: The Central Vigilance Commission and court judgments provide guidance on corruption, conflict of interest, and standards of public life.

5. **International frameworks**: The Nolan Principles (UK), OECD recommendations, and UN conventions against corruption influence thinking on public service ethics.

## Core Values for Civil Services (commonly emphasised)

- **Integrity**: Honesty, consistency between words and actions, and refusal to be corrupted.
- **Impartiality and Non-partisanship**: Decisions based on merit and evidence, not on political or personal considerations; treating all citizens equally.
- **Objectivity**: Basing advice and decisions on rigorous analysis and evidence.
- **Accountability**: Being answerable for decisions and actions to the appropriate authorities and, ultimately, to the public.
- **Transparency**: Openness in decision-making processes, subject to legitimate confidentiality requirements.
- **Dedication to Public Service**: Putting the public interest above personal or group interest.
- **Empathy and Compassion**: Understanding the conditions of the vulnerable and acting to reduce suffering.
- **Tolerance and Responsiveness**: Respect for diversity and timely response to citizens' needs.

## Probity in Public Life

Probity means integrity, uprightness, and honesty. In public life, it refers to the quality of having strong moral principles and adhering to them in the exercise of public power. Probity requires that public officials:

- Avoid conflicts of interest or manage them transparently.
- Do not use public office for private gain.
- Maintain confidentiality where required and do not misuse information.
- Are truthful in their dealings and communications.
- Accept responsibility for their actions.

Probity is both an individual virtue and an institutional requirement. Institutions promote probity through transparent procedures, audit mechanisms, disclosure norms, whistle-blower protection, and a culture that rewards ethical behaviour and sanctions misconduct.

## Conflict of Interest

A conflict of interest arises when a public official's private interests could improperly influence the performance of official duties. Conflicts may be:

- Actual: A real conflict exists.
- Potential: A conflict may arise in the future.
- Perceived: Even if no actual conflict exists, the situation creates a reasonable perception of bias.

Management strategies include disclosure, recusal, divestment of interests, and establishing clear procedures for handling situations where conflict is unavoidable.

## Challenges to Ethical Conduct

- Political pressure and the culture of transfers and postings.
- Low probability of detection and weak enforcement of rules.
- Social norms that tolerate or even expect certain forms of favouritism or gift-giving.
- Complex and discretionary regulatory environments that create opportunities for rent-seeking.
- Inadequate training and internalisation of ethical values.
- Workload and resource constraints that push officials toward shortcuts.

## Building an Ethical Organisation

Ethics is not only an individual attribute; it is sustained by organisational systems:

- Clear and communicated values and codes.
- Leadership that models ethical behaviour.
- Training and continuous dialogue on ethical dilemmas.
- Safe channels for raising concerns (including whistle-blower mechanisms).
- Fair and consistent enforcement.
- Recognition of ethical conduct.
- Reduction of unnecessary discretion through process simplification and technology where appropriate.

## Case Study Approach in Ethics Paper

In the UPSC Ethics paper, candidates are often given situational dilemmas. A structured approach includes:

1. Identifying the facts and the stakeholders.
2. Identifying the ethical issues and values in conflict.
3. Considering options and their consequences (short-term and long-term, for individuals and institutions).
4. Applying relevant principles (constitutional values, code of conduct, public interest, fairness, compassion).
5. Choosing a course of action and justifying it.
6. Suggesting measures to prevent recurrence if systemic issues are involved.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Code of Conduct: rule-based, enforceable standards.
- Code of Ethics: principle-based, aspirational values.
- Core values: integrity, impartiality, objectivity, accountability, transparency, public service commitment, empathy.
- Probity: uprightness and honesty in public life.
- Conflict of interest: actual, potential, perceived; managed by disclosure and recusal.
- Second ARC and constitutional values as foundational references.
- Ethical organisation requires systems, leadership, and culture, not only individual goodwill.

## Conceptual Understanding

Ethics in public administration is the bridge between formal rules and the living practice of governance. Rules alone cannot cover every situation; values guide the exercise of discretion. Conversely, values without institutional support remain rhetorical. The cultivation of probity requires simultaneous attention to individual character, organisational design, political culture, and societal expectations. For a civil servant, ethical competence is as essential as technical and managerial competence.
