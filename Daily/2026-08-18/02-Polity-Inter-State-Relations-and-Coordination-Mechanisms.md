# Polity: Inter-State Relations and Coordination Mechanisms

## Nature of Indian Federalism and Inter-State Relations

Indian federalism is often described as quasi-federal or a "Union of States" with a strong central bias. The Constitution divides powers between the Union and the States through three lists in the Seventh Schedule: Union List, State List, and Concurrent List. Despite this division, the states are interdependent in many areas — river waters, trade, communication, security, and economic development. This interdependence necessitates institutional mechanisms for coordination and dispute resolution.

Inter-state relations cover three major dimensions:

1. Legislative relations (distribution of powers and residual powers).
2. Administrative relations (direction and control by the Union over states in certain matters).
3. Financial relations (tax sharing, grants, and Finance Commission).

Beyond these formal relations, there exist specific institutional arrangements for resolving disputes and promoting cooperation.

## Constitutional Provisions on Inter-State Relations

### Full Faith and Credit (Article 261)

Full faith and credit shall be given throughout the territory of India to public acts, records, and judicial proceedings of the Union and of every state. This ensures that a decree or order of a court in one state is recognised and enforceable in another state, subject to the provisions of law.

### Inter-State Trade and Commerce (Articles 301–307)

Article 301 declares that trade, commerce, and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free. This freedom is subject to reasonable restrictions in the public interest (Article 302 for Parliament, Article 304 for state legislatures). Parliament can impose restrictions in the public interest, while states can impose reasonable restrictions on goods coming from other states, subject to Presidential sanction in certain cases.

The idea is to create a common market within India while allowing limited regulatory powers to the states.

### Disputes Relating to Waters (Article 262)

Parliament may by law provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint with respect to the use, distribution, or control of the waters of, or in, any inter-state river or river valley. Parliament may also provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction in respect of any such dispute.

The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 (as amended) provides the statutory framework. Tribunals are constituted for specific disputes (for example, Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Ravi-Beas). The 2019 amendment sought to create a permanent tribunal and a dispute resolution committee to speed up the process.

### Coordination through the President and Parliament

Under Article 263, the President may establish an Inter-State Council if it appears to him that public interest would be served by such a body. The Council is charged with:

- Inquiring into and advising upon disputes between states.
- Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all of the states, or the Union and one or more states, have a common interest.
- Making recommendations upon any such subject, particularly for better coordination of policy and action.

## Institutional Mechanisms

### Inter-State Council

The Inter-State Council was established in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. It is a recommendatory body. Its composition includes:

- Prime Minister as Chairman.
- Chief Ministers of all states.
- Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislatures.
- Administrators of Union Territories without legislatures.
- Six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister.

The Council has a Standing Committee and a Secretariat. It has discussed issues such as Centre-State relations, internal security, and economic and social planning. Its effectiveness has been limited because its recommendations are not binding and meetings have been infrequent.

### Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils were set up under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. There are five Zonal Councils: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, and Southern. The North-Eastern Council was created separately by a 1971 Act.

Each Zonal Council is chaired by the Union Home Minister. Chief Ministers and two other ministers from each state in the zone, along with administrators of Union Territories, are members. Zonal Councils discuss matters of common interest such as economic and social planning, border disputes, inter-state transport, and linguistic minorities.

They are advisory bodies and have played a useful role in fostering cooperation at the regional level.

### National Development Council and NITI Aayog

The National Development Council (NDC) was the apex body for approving Five-Year Plans and discussing developmental issues. With the replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog in 2015, the NDC has become largely inactive. NITI Aayog functions as a think-tank and a platform for cooperative federalism through its Governing Council, which includes Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors.

NITI Aayog's emphasis on competitive and cooperative federalism has introduced new modes of Centre-State engagement, such as ranking of states on various indices and sharing of best practices.

### Other Mechanisms

- **Finance Commission**: Though primarily concerned with vertical and horizontal fiscal transfers, it also addresses issues of fiscal imbalance among states.
- **GST Council**: A unique federal body created by the 101st Constitutional Amendment. It is a joint forum of the Centre and the states for decisions on GST rates, exemptions, and related matters. Decisions are taken by a three-fourths majority, with the Centre having one-third of the weighted votes and the states collectively having two-thirds.
- **River Boards and Tribunals**: For inter-state river disputes.
- **National Integration Council**: Focuses on communal harmony and national unity, with representation from political parties, states, and civil society.

## Major Areas of Inter-State Friction

1. **River water disputes**: Cauvery (Karnataka-Tamil Nadu-Kerala-Puducherry), Krishna, Godavari, Ravi-Beas (Punjab-Haryana-Rajasthan), Mahadayi, Vansadhara, etc. These disputes are emotionally charged and politically sensitive.

2. **Border disputes**: Maharashtra-Karnataka (Belagavi), Assam-Nagaland, Assam-Meghalaya, Assam-Arunachal Pradesh. Many of these have historical roots in colonial boundary demarcations and the reorganisation of states.

3. **Migration and resource allocation**: Issues arising from inter-state migration, especially in metropolitan cities, and sharing of central resources.

4. **Trade and taxation barriers**: Although Article 301 aims at free trade, residual barriers and differential policies create friction.

5. **Security and law and order**: Cross-border crime, naxalism, and terrorism require coordinated police action.

## Cooperative vs Competitive Federalism

Cooperative federalism emphasises collaboration between the Centre and the states and among the states themselves for national goals. Competitive federalism encourages states to compete with each other for investment, better governance rankings, and improved service delivery. Both concepts coexist in contemporary India. NITI Aayog actively promotes both.

The challenge is to ensure that competition does not degenerate into conflict and that cooperation is not reduced to central dominance.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Article 261: Full faith and credit.
- Articles 301–307: Freedom of trade, commerce, and intercourse.
- Article 262: Inter-state water disputes; Parliament can bar jurisdiction of courts.
- Article 263: Inter-State Council.
- Inter-State Council established in 1990 on Sarkaria Commission recommendation.
- Five Zonal Councils + North-Eastern Council.
- GST Council: unique federal body with weighted voting.
- S.R. Bommai and subsequent cases reinforce federal principles even while recognising the strong Centre.

## Conceptual Understanding

Inter-state relations in India are shaped by the dual forces of diversity and unity. The constitutional design provides multiple forums for dialogue and adjudication. The effectiveness of these forums depends less on formal powers and more on political will, mutual trust, and the quality of leadership at both the Centre and the states. As India pursues rapid economic growth and faces complex challenges such as climate change, water scarcity, and internal security, the quality of inter-state coordination will be a decisive factor in the success of the federal experiment.
