# Comptroller and Auditor General of India and Mechanisms of Accountability

## Constitutional Position and Appointment

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) is a constitutional authority under Article 148 of the Constitution. The CAG is appointed by the President of India by warrant under his hand and seal. The tenure is six years or until the age of sixty-five years, whichever is earlier. The CAG can be removed from office only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a Judge of the Supreme Court, that is, by an address of both Houses of Parliament supported by a special majority on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

The salary and other conditions of service of the CAG are determined by Parliament and cannot be varied to the disadvantage of the CAG after appointment. The CAG is not eligible for further office under the Government of India or of any State after demitting office. These provisions are designed to ensure independence and impartiality.

## Duties and Powers

Article 149 provides that the CAG shall perform such duties and exercise such powers in relation to the accounts of the Union and of the States and of any other authority or body as may be prescribed by or under any law made by Parliament. The CAG’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971 elaborates these functions.

The CAG audits:
- All receipts and expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India and of each State and Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly.
- All transactions relating to the Contingency Fund and Public Accounts.
- All trading, manufacturing, profit and loss accounts, balance sheets and other subsidiary accounts kept in any Government department.
- Accounts of government companies, corporations and bodies substantially financed by government grants or loans, as prescribed.
- Accounts of bodies and authorities when required by the President or Governor.

The CAG also has the power to conduct propriety audit, examining not only the legality of expenditure but also its wisdom, faithfulness and economy. This includes scrutiny of whether expenditure has been incurred with due regard to financial propriety and whether the results justify the expenditure.

## Audit Reports and Parliamentary Oversight

The CAG submits three audit reports to the President (for Union) or the Governor (for States):
1. Audit Report on Appropriation Accounts
2. Audit Report on Finance Accounts
3. Audit Report on Public Undertakings (or commercial accounts)

These reports are laid before the respective legislatures. In the case of the Union, the reports are examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). The PAC, headed by a member of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha by convention, scrutinises the reports and examines witnesses from the executive. The CAG acts as a friend, philosopher and guide to the PAC.

## Role in Financial Accountability

The CAG is often described as the guardian of the public purse. Through audit, the CAG ensures that:
- Money has been spent for the purpose for which it was granted by the legislature.
- Expenditure has been incurred in accordance with the rules and procedures.
- There has been no waste, extravagance or loss due to negligence.
- Revenues have been properly assessed, collected and accounted for.

The CAG’s reports have historically exposed major irregularities, leading to systemic reforms. The institution strengthens the principle of parliamentary control over public finance, which is a cornerstone of responsible government in a parliamentary democracy.

## Relationship with the Executive and Legislature

While the CAG is independent of the executive, the executive is responsible for maintaining accounts. The CAG does not prepare accounts but audits them. After the separation of accounts from audit in the 1970s, the Controller General of Accounts (under the Ministry of Finance) is responsible for compiling Union accounts, while the CAG continues to audit them.

The CAG can be called upon by the President to undertake additional audits or investigations. The CAG also advises the President on the form in which accounts of the Union and States are to be kept (Article 150).

## Comparative Perspective and Evolution

The office of the CAG draws inspiration from the British Comptroller and Auditor General, but the Indian CAG combines both the functions of comptroller (control over issue of money) and auditor. In practice, the comptroller function has been largely residual because the system of appropriation is managed through the executive’s financial rules.

Over the decades, the scope of CAG’s audit has expanded to include performance audit, environmental audit, and audit of public-private partnerships and regulatory bodies in certain cases. The institution has adapted to the changing nature of public expenditure and the growth of the welfare state.

## Key Concepts for Retention

- Article 148: Appointment and independence of CAG
- Tenure: 6 years or 65 years of age
- Removal: Like Supreme Court Judge
- Article 149: Duties and powers
- Article 150: Form of accounts
- Article 151: Audit reports to President/Governor for laying before legislature
- Propriety audit versus regularity audit
- PAC and COPU as mechanisms of legislative accountability
- Independence safeguards: salary charged on Consolidated Fund, no further office, removal procedure

The CAG remains one of the most important institutions ensuring that the executive remains accountable to the legislature in the realm of public finance. Its reports form the factual basis on which parliamentary committees exercise financial oversight, thereby completing the chain of accountability in India’s democratic system.
