# Regional Kingdoms of the Medieval Deccan and South India

## Historical Context after the Decline of Delhi Sultanate Authority

Following the weakening of the Delhi Sultanate in the fourteenth century, particularly after the reign of Muhammad bin Tughlaq, several independent kingdoms emerged in the Deccan and South India. The two most significant political formations were the Bahmani Sultanate in the Deccan and the Vijayanagara Empire further south. These kingdoms shaped the political, cultural and economic landscape of peninsular India for over two centuries.

## The Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527)

The Bahmani Sultanate was founded in 1347 by Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah (also known as Hasan Gangu) after a successful rebellion against the Tughlaq authority. The capital was initially at Gulbarga (Ahsanabad) and later shifted to Bidar.

### Political Organisation and Administration

The Bahmani state was organised on the model of the Delhi Sultanate with adaptations. The Sultan was the supreme authority. The kingdom was divided into tarafs (provinces) governed by tarafdars. Important officials included the wakil (deputy), mir jumla (finance), and amir-ul-umara (military). The nobility was divided into Deccanis (local converts and earlier settlers) and Afaqis (newcomers from Central Asia, Iran and other regions). This division often led to factional conflicts that weakened the sultanate in its later years.

### Cultural and Architectural Contributions

The Bahmani rulers patronised Persian culture and architecture. Notable structures include the Jama Masjid at Gulbarga, the madrasa of Mahmud Gawan at Bidar, and various tombs. Mahmud Gawan, a Persian merchant who rose to the position of prime minister, introduced administrative reforms and expanded the kingdom’s frontiers. His execution in 1481 due to court intrigues marked a turning point leading to the decline of central authority.

### Decline and Successor States

By the early sixteenth century, the Bahmani Sultanate fragmented into five successor states known as the Deccan Sultanates: Bijapur (Adil Shahi), Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahi), Golconda (Qutb Shahi), Berar (Imad Shahi) and Bidar (Barid Shahi). These states continued the Indo-Persian cultural synthesis and engaged in constant warfare among themselves and with Vijayanagara.

## The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1565)

The Vijayanagara Empire was founded in 1336 by Harihara and Bukka of the Sangama dynasty on the southern bank of the Tungabhadra river. The empire rose as a response to the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate into the south and became the dominant power in South India for more than two centuries.

### Dynasties and Expansion

Four dynasties ruled Vijayanagara: Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva and Aravidu. The empire reached its zenith under Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529) of the Tuluva dynasty. Krishnadevaraya expanded the empire, defeated the Bahmani successor states, and maintained diplomatic relations with the Portuguese. His reign is remembered for military success, administrative efficiency and cultural florescence.

### Administration and Society

The empire was divided into provinces (rajyas or mandalams), further subdivided into nadus and sthala. The nayankara system was a distinctive feature: military chiefs (nayakas) were granted territories (amaram) in return for maintaining troops and rendering military service. This system provided a flexible military organisation but also created powerful local lords who later asserted independence.

Land revenue was the principal source of income. The state encouraged irrigation through tanks and canals. Trade flourished, particularly in spices, textiles and precious stones. The ports of the Coromandel and Malabar coasts were important centres of overseas commerce with the Portuguese, Arabs and Southeast Asia.

### Cultural Achievements

Vijayanagara was a major centre of Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada and Tamil literature. Krishnadevaraya himself was a noted scholar and author of Amuktamalyada in Telugu. Temple architecture reached new heights with the construction and expansion of the Vitthala temple, Hazara Rama temple and numerous other complexes at Hampi. The empire patronised both Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions and maintained a policy of religious tolerance towards Jains and Muslims within its territories.

### The Battle of Talikota (1565) and Aftermath

In 1565, a coalition of the Deccan Sultanates defeated the Vijayanagara forces at the Battle of Talikota (also known as Rakshasa-Tangadi). The capital was sacked and largely destroyed. Although the Aravidu dynasty continued to rule from Penukonda and later Chandragiri and Vellore, the empire never recovered its former glory. The defeat marked the end of the last major indigenous empire in South India before the rise of regional powers and eventual European dominance.

## Interaction between Bahmani and Vijayanagara

The two powers were locked in almost continuous conflict over the Raichur Doab, the fertile region between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers. Control of this area was both economically and strategically vital. The rivalry shaped the military and diplomatic history of the Deccan for two centuries. Cultural exchanges also occurred, particularly in architecture, music and courtly practices.

## Significance in Indian History

The Bahmani and Vijayanagara kingdoms represent the flowering of regional polities in medieval India. They developed sophisticated administrative systems, promoted distinctive architectural styles (Indo-Islamic in the Deccan and Dravidian with unique Vijayanagara features in the south), and fostered literary and artistic traditions in regional languages. Their history illustrates the resilience of South Indian political and cultural systems in the face of northern expansion and the complex interplay of conflict and coexistence between Islamic and Hindu political formations.

## Key Points for Conceptual Clarity

- Bahmani founded 1347 by Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah; capital Gulbarga then Bidar
- Nobility: Deccanis versus Afaqis
- Mahmud Gawan’s reforms and fall
- Fragmentation into five Deccan Sultanates
- Vijayanagara founded 1336 by Harihara and Bukka
- Peak under Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529)
- Nayankara system of military tenure
- Battle of Talikota 1565 and subsequent decline
- Cultural synthesis and regional literary florescence

These kingdoms form an essential bridge between the early medieval period and the early modern era of South Indian history, providing the political and cultural background against which later developments, including the rise of the Marathas and European trading companies, unfolded.
