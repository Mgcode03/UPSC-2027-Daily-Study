# Water Resources of India: Availability, Challenges and Conservation

## Water Availability in India

India accounts for about 18 per cent of the world’s population but has only about 4 per cent of the world’s renewable water resources. The average annual precipitation is approximately 4000 billion cubic metres (BCM), of which a significant portion is lost to evaporation and runoff. The estimated utilisable water resources are around 1123 BCM, comprising 690 BCM of surface water and 433 BCM of replenishable groundwater.

Per capita water availability has been declining due to population growth and is approaching the threshold of water stress. Spatial and temporal variability of rainfall further complicates the situation: the bulk of precipitation occurs in a few monsoon months, and large parts of the country face seasonal or chronic water scarcity.

## Surface Water Resources

The major river systems of India are the Himalayan rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) and the peninsular rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery, Mahanadi, Narmada, Tapi). Himalayan rivers are perennial, fed by both rainfall and snowmelt, while peninsular rivers are largely rain-fed and seasonal.

Inter-state river water disputes arise because river basins cut across state boundaries. The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act provides the legal framework for adjudication. Major disputes have involved the Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Ravi-Beas and other rivers. The concept of river basin organisations and the principle of equitable apportionment are central to resolution efforts.

## Groundwater Resources

Groundwater is the mainstay of irrigation and rural and urban drinking water supply in large parts of India. Over-extraction has led to declining water tables, particularly in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and parts of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. Critical and over-exploited blocks identified by the Central Ground Water Board require regulated extraction and artificial recharge.

The dependence on groundwater has increased with the expansion of tube-well irrigation and the Green Revolution. While it has contributed to agricultural growth, it has also created sustainability concerns and issues of water quality, including fluoride, arsenic and salinity contamination in several regions.

## Water Use Pattern

Agriculture accounts for the largest share of water use (around 80–85 per cent), followed by domestic and industrial uses. The efficiency of water use in agriculture remains low in many canal-irrigated areas due to flood irrigation practices, conveyance losses and inadequate water pricing. Shift towards micro-irrigation (drip and sprinkler) is a key policy thrust for improving water productivity.

Industrial and urban water demand is rising rapidly with economic growth and urbanisation. Treatment and reuse of wastewater, rainwater harvesting and demand management are essential components of urban water security.

## Major Challenges

- Declining per capita availability and increasing water stress
- Over-exploitation of groundwater and falling water tables
- Pollution of surface and groundwater from domestic, industrial and agricultural sources
- Inequitable distribution and inter-state conflicts
- Climate change impacts on monsoon variability, glacier melt and extreme events
- Inadequate storage capacity relative to the volume of monsoon flows
- Low water-use efficiency in agriculture and high non-revenue water in urban supply

## Conservation and Management Strategies

### Supply-Side Measures
- Creation of additional storage through multipurpose projects and smaller structures
- Inter-linking of rivers (under study and selective implementation)
- Artificial recharge of groundwater through check dams, percolation tanks and injection wells
- Watershed development and catchment area treatment
- Desalination in coastal areas where economically viable

### Demand-Side Measures
- Promotion of micro-irrigation and precision farming
- Crop diversification away from water-intensive crops in water-stressed regions
- Rational pricing of water and electricity for irrigation to discourage wasteful use
- Water auditing and recycling in industry
- Rainwater harvesting mandates in urban areas
- Public awareness and community participation in water management

### Institutional and Legal Framework
- National Water Policy emphasising integrated water resources management, prioritisation of drinking water, and participatory approaches
- Central Ground Water Authority for regulation in critical areas
- State water policies and regulatory authorities
- Command Area Development and Water Management Programme
- Atal Bhujal Yojana for community-led groundwater management in water-stressed areas
- Jal Jeevan Mission for providing functional household tap connections in rural areas
- Namami Gange and other river rejuvenation programmes focusing on pollution abatement and ecological flow

## Concept of Integrated Water Resources Management

Integrated Water Resources Management (IWRM) seeks to coordinate the development and management of water, land and related resources to maximise economic and social welfare without compromising the sustainability of ecosystems. It emphasises the river basin as the unit of planning, stakeholder participation, and the balancing of competing uses.

## Key Points for Retention

- Utilisable water resources ≈ 1123 BCM (surface + groundwater)
- Agriculture dominant user; low irrigation efficiency
- Groundwater over-exploitation in several states
- Inter-state river water disputes and legal framework
- Micro-irrigation, rainwater harvesting, wastewater reuse as key solutions
- National Water Policy, Jal Jeevan Mission, Atal Bhujal Yojana
- Integrated Water Resources Management approach
- Climate change as an exacerbating factor

Sustainable water management is central to food security, health, economic growth and ecological balance in India. Balancing the competing demands of agriculture, industry, domestic use and the environment requires both technological solutions and institutional reforms grounded in equity and efficiency.
