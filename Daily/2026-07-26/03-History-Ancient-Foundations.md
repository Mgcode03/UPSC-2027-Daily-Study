# Ancient Indian History – Foundations for UPSC

Ancient Indian history forms a significant part of both Prelims and Mains. Focus on political, social, economic, religious and cultural developments, sources, and continuity and change.

## Sources of Ancient Indian History

Literary sources: Vedic literature (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva Vedas; Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads), Epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata), Puranas, Buddhist literature (Tripitakas – Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma Pitaka in Pali; Jatakas), Jain literature (Angas in Prakrit), Dharmashastras and Smritis, secular literature (Arthashastra of Kautilya, works of Kalidasa, Banabhatta etc.).

Archaeological sources: Inscriptions (Ashokan edicts, Prashastis like Allahabad Pillar inscription of Samudragupta), coins (punch-marked, Indo-Greek, Kushana, Gupta gold coins), monuments and architecture, pottery (Painted Grey Ware, Northern Black Polished Ware), tools and implements.

Foreign accounts: Greek (Megasthenes’ Indica, accounts of Alexander’s companions), Chinese (Fa-Hien, Hiuen Tsang, I-Tsing), Arab (Al-Biruni).

## Prehistoric Period

Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age): Hunting-gathering, crude stone tools, sites like Bhimbetka, Soan valley, Madras. Lower, Middle and Upper Palaeolithic phases. No agriculture or pottery.

Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age): Microliths, beginning of domestication of animals, rock art at Bhimbetka, sites in Rajasthan, Gujarat, central India.

Neolithic (New Stone Age): Agriculture, animal domestication, polished stone tools, pottery, settled life. Sites: Mehrgarh (earliest evidence of agriculture in Indian subcontinent, wheat, barley, cotton), Burzahom (Kashmir – pit dwellings, dog burial), Chirand (Bihar), Daojali Hading (Northeast), Hallur and Paiyampalli (South).

Chalcolithic: Use of copper along with stone. Cultures like Ahar-Banas (Rajasthan), Malwa, Jorwe (Maharashtra), Kayatha. Generally rural, with some craft specialisation.

## Indus Valley Civilisation / Harappan Civilisation

Time frame: Mature Harappan roughly 2600–1900 BCE. Early and Late phases extend the overall period.

Geographical extent: From Sutkagendor (Baluchistan) in the west to Alamgirpur (Uttar Pradesh) in the east; from Manda (Jammu) in the north to Daimabad (Maharashtra) in the south. Major sites: Harappa (Punjab, Pakistan), Mohenjo-daro (Sindh), Dholavira (Gujarat – unique water management and large inscription), Lothal (Gujarat – dockyard), Kalibangan (Rajasthan – ploughed field, fire altars), Banawali, Rakhigarhi (Haryana – one of the largest sites), Chanhudaro (bead-making).

Town planning: Grid pattern, citadel and lower town, standardised burnt bricks, elaborate drainage system, great bath at Mohenjo-daro, granaries. Uniformity in planning across sites suggests centralised authority or shared cultural norms.

Economy: Agriculture (wheat, barley, peas, sesame, mustard, cotton – first to produce cotton), animal husbandry, trade (internal and external with Mesopotamia – evidence of seals, carnelian beads; Lothal dockyard), crafts (bead-making, pottery, metallurgy – bronze, copper, gold, silver; no iron).

Society: Generally egalitarian appearance from archaeology, though variations in house sizes exist. Seals depict animals (unicorn most common), script (undeciphered, written right to left, boustrophedon in some cases). Religion: Mother Goddess figurines, Pashupati seal (proto-Shiva?), tree and animal worship, fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal, no large temples found.

Decline: Multiple theories – climate change, drying of Saraswati (Ghaggar-Hakra), flood, decline in trade, Aryan invasion (now largely discarded as primary cause), ecological degradation. The civilisation did not disappear suddenly but transformed into Late Harappan cultures.

## Vedic Period

Early Vedic (Rigvedic) Period: Approx. 1500–1000 BCE. Geographical focus on Sapta Sindhu (Punjab and adjoining areas). Sources mainly Rigveda.

Political organisation: Tribe (jana) was the primary unit. Rajan was the chief, assisted by senani, purohita, and assemblies – Sabha and Samiti. No standing army or elaborate administration. Battles for cattle (gavishti).

Society: Patriarchal, but women had relatively better status (could attend assemblies, compose hymns – Lopamudra, Ghosha, Apala). Varna system emerging but flexible. Occupation-based rather than rigid birth-based initially.

Economy: Pastoral with agriculture secondary. Cattle as main wealth. Barter system. No regular taxation; bali was voluntary offering.

Religion: Nature worship – Indra (most important, warrior god), Agni, Varuna, Soma, Ushas. Yajnas and sacrifices. No idol worship or temples.

Later Vedic Period: Approx. 1000–600 BCE. Expansion into Gangetic plains. Sources: Later Vedas, Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads, and archaeological Painted Grey Ware culture.

Political changes: Larger kingdoms (janapadas). Rajan becomes more powerful; rituals like Rajasuya, Ashvamedha, Vajapeya enhance status. Sabha and Samiti decline in importance. Emergence of officials.

Society: Varna system becomes rigid. Gotra and ashrama systems develop. Position of women declines (no longer attend assemblies freely, child marriage begins to appear in texts). Rise of Brahmanas as dominant priestly class.

Economy: Agriculture becomes primary. Iron technology (Around 1000 BCE onwards) aids clearing of forests. Trade and craft specialisation increase. Coins not yet in regular use; nishka was gold ornament used in exchange.

Religion: Ritualism peaks in Brahmanas. Reaction in Upanishads – emphasis on knowledge (jnana), Brahman and Atman, karma and transmigration. Gods like Prajapati, Vishnu, Rudra gain prominence.

## Mahajanapadas and Rise of Magadha

By 6th century BCE, sixteen Mahajanapadas are mentioned in Buddhist and Jain texts. Important ones: Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, Kuru, Panchala, Gandhara, Kamboja, Anga, Kashi, Malla, Chedi, Vajji (confederacy), Matsya, Surasena, Asmaka.

Magadha emerged as the most powerful due to geographical advantages (iron ore, fertile land, forests, elephants, strategic location with rivers), ambitious rulers, and effective policy.

Rulers: Bimbisara (Haryanka dynasty) – expansion through marriage and conquest, contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira. Ajatashatru – further expansion, fortified Rajagriha, used new weapons. Later Sisunaga dynasty and Nandas (Mahapadma Nanda – first great empire builder, large army). Nandas were overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya.

## Religious Movements – Buddhism and Jainism

Causes for rise: Complex Vedic rituals, dominance of Brahmanas, varna rigidities, rise of new social groups (vaishyas wanting better status), Upanishadic ideas creating intellectual ferment, urbanisation and trade creating new ethos.

Jainism: Vardhamana Mahavira (24th Tirthankara). Born at Kundagrama near Vaishali. Five doctrines – satya, ahimsa, aparigraha, asteya, brahmacharya (added by Mahavira). Three jewels – right faith, right knowledge, right conduct. Sects – Digambara and Shvetambara. Spread through trade routes and royal patronage (Chandragupta Maurya tradition, Kharavela of Kalinga).

Buddhism: Siddhartha Gautama (Buddha). Born at Lumbini, enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, first sermon at Sarnath (Dharmachakra Pravartana), mahaparinirvana at Kushinagar. Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path. Middle Path. Rejection of extreme asceticism and ritualism. Sangha open to all castes and women (with conditions). Spread widely under Ashoka and later through trade and missions. Sects – Hinayana (Theravada) and Mahayana; later Vajrayana.

Both religions used Prakrit/Pali, rejected Vedic authority and animal sacrifice, emphasised ethics and individual effort, and contributed to art, architecture and literature.

## Mauryan Empire

Founded by Chandragupta Maurya with help of Chanakya (Kautilya). Defeated Nandas and Seleucus Nicator (treaty – territory and marriage alliance; Megasthenes came as ambassador).

Bindusara extended the empire. Ashoka is the most celebrated. Kalinga War (261 BCE) transformed him; he adopted Dhamma. Rock and Pillar Edicts propagate principles of tolerance, non-violence, welfare, respect for all sects. Major Rock Edicts, Minor Rock Edicts, Pillar Edicts, and the Schism Edict. Dhamma was not a new religion but a code of ethics and social responsibility.

Administration: Highly centralised. King assisted by Mantriparishad. Provinces under kumara or governors. Districts, villages. Large standing army. Espionage system. Economic control – state monopoly in some sectors, taxation (bhaga, bali etc.). Arthashastra provides idealised but detailed picture.

Decline after Ashoka: Weak successors, Brahmanical reaction, external invasions, economic issues, and the vastness of the empire. Last Mauryan king Brihadratha killed by Pushyamitra Shunga.

## Post-Mauryan Developments

Shungas, Kanvas in the north. Satavahanas in the Deccan (important for trade, land grants to Brahmanas and Buddhists, Prakrit literature). Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushanas in the north-west and north. Kushana ruler Kanishka – important for Buddhism (Fourth Buddhist Council, patronage of Mahayana), trade (Silk Route), and art (Gandhara and Mathura schools).

Sangam Age in South India: Three Sangams, literature in Tamil, chieftains of Chera, Chola, Pandya, and many smaller velir chiefs. Trade with Rome (evidence of gold coins, pottery at Arikamedu etc.).

## Key Concepts to Internalise

- Continuity and change from Harappan to Vedic to Mahajanapada to Mauryan.
- Role of iron technology in the second urbanisation (Gangetic plains).
- Difference between Early and Later Vedic society and polity.
- Ashoka’s Dhamma as a policy of governance and social ethics, not conversion.
- Sources must be critically evaluated – literary texts have ideological biases; archaeology provides material evidence.

This foundation enables understanding of later historical developments and helps answer questions on culture, society, economy and polity of ancient India.
