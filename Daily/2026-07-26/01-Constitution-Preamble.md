# The Constitution of India – Foundation

The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the land. It lays down the framework defining fundamental political principles, establishes the structure, procedures, powers and duties of government institutions, and sets out fundamental rights, directive principles and the duties of citizens.

It was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. This date is celebrated as Republic Day.

The Constitution is the longest written constitution of any sovereign country in the world. It originally contained 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. Through amendments it has grown substantially.

## Sources of the Constitution

The framers drew from multiple sources while adapting them to Indian conditions:

- Government of India Act 1935 provided the federal structure, provincial autonomy, emergency provisions, public service commissions and the office of Governor.
- British Constitution contributed parliamentary form of government, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges and bicameralism.
- US Constitution influenced Fundamental Rights, independent judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, and the post of Vice-President.
- Irish Constitution inspired Directive Principles of State Policy, nomination of members to Rajya Sabha, and the method of election of the President.
- Canadian Constitution contributed federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, appointment of state governors by the Centre, and advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
- Australian Constitution influenced concurrent list, freedom of trade and commerce, and joint sitting of the two Houses.
- Weimar Constitution of Germany contributed suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency.
- Soviet Constitution influenced Fundamental Duties and the ideal of justice (social, economic and political) in the Preamble.
- French Constitution contributed the ideals of Republic and the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity in the Preamble.
- South African Constitution influenced the procedure for amendment and election of members of Rajya Sabha.
- Japanese Constitution contributed the procedure established by law.

The Constitution is neither purely federal nor purely unitary. It is a unique blend often described as quasi-federal or federal with a strong unitary bias.

## Preamble

The Preamble is the introductory statement that sets out the objectives and guiding principles of the Constitution. It is often called the soul or identity card of the Constitution.

### Text of the Preamble

We, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:

JUSTICE, social, economic and political;

LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;

EQUALITY of status and of opportunity;

and to promote among them all

FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;

IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

### Key Words Explained

Sovereign: India is free from external control. It can acquire or cede territory and make its own laws. The word affirms that the ultimate authority rests with the people of India.

Socialist: Added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976. It means the state will work towards reducing inequalities in income, status and opportunities and will strive for a mixed economy with a strong public sector role in welfare.

Secular: Also added by the 42nd Amendment. The state has no official religion. It treats all religions equally and maintains neutrality. Freedom of religion is guaranteed as a fundamental right.

Democratic: The government is based on the will of the people expressed through universal adult franchise. India follows a representative parliamentary democracy.

Republic: The head of the state is elected, not hereditary. The President of India is the elected constitutional head.

Justice: Social justice means equal treatment and absence of discrimination. Economic justice means reducing inequalities of wealth and income. Political justice means equal political rights including right to vote and contest elections.

Liberty: Freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship. These are essential for the development of individual personality.

Equality: Equality of status and opportunity. This includes equality before law, prohibition of discrimination, and equal access to public employment and education.

Fraternity: Sense of brotherhood. It assures the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation. The words “unity and integrity of the Nation” were added by the 42nd Amendment.

### Significance of the Preamble

The Preamble indicates the source of the Constitution – the people of India. It declares the nature of the Indian state. It states the objectives. It is a key to understanding the minds of the Constitution makers.

In the Berubari Union case (1960), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution. Later, in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), the Court held that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution and can be used to interpret ambiguous provisions. The Court also held that the basic structure of the Constitution cannot be amended, and the Preamble reflects that basic structure.

The Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but the basic features it embodies cannot be destroyed.

## Fundamental Rights (Part III)

Fundamental Rights are enshrined in Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12 to 35). They are justiciable, meaning a citizen can approach the courts for their enforcement.

Article 12 defines the State for the purpose of Fundamental Rights. It includes the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and Legislature of each State, and all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

Article 13 declares that all laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights shall be void. It also defines law to include ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages.

### Classification of Fundamental Rights

1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)
2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)
3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)
4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)
5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)
6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

The Right to Property was originally a Fundamental Right under Article 31. It was removed by the 44th Amendment in 1978 and made a legal right under Article 300A.

### Right to Equality

Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws. Equality before law is a negative concept (no special privilege). Equal protection of laws is a positive concept (equal treatment in equal circumstances and different treatment in different circumstances).

Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. The State can make special provisions for women and children, and for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Article 16: Equality of opportunity in matters of public employment. No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth or residence. The State can reserve posts for backward classes not adequately represented, and make provisions for SC/ST. There is also provision for reservation in promotion for SC/ST.

Article 17: Abolition of untouchability. Its practice in any form is forbidden and is an offence punishable by law. The Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 give effect to this.

Article 18: Abolition of titles. No title (except military or academic distinction) shall be conferred by the State. Citizens cannot accept titles from foreign states. The Bharat Ratna, Padma awards etc. are not titles under this Article as clarified by the Supreme Court.

### Right to Freedom

Article 19: Six freedoms – speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession. These are available only to citizens and can be restricted on specified grounds such as sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offence.

Article 20: Protection in respect of conviction for offences. No ex-post-facto law, no double jeopardy, and no self-incrimination.

Article 21: Protection of life and personal liberty. No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Through judicial interpretation this has expanded to include right to live with dignity, right to livelihood, right to privacy, right to health, right to education (until Article 21A was added), right to clean environment, and many other rights.

Article 21A: Right to education. The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years. Added by the 86th Amendment in 2002.

Article 22: Protection against arrest and detention. Rights of a person arrested under ordinary law and protections in case of preventive detention.

### Right against Exploitation

Article 23: Prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour. Begar and other similar forms of forced labour are prohibited.

Article 24: Prohibition of employment of children in factories, mines or other hazardous employment. No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment.

### Right to Freedom of Religion

Article 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of religion. Subject to public order, morality, health and other provisions of Part III. The State can regulate secular activities associated with religious practice and provide for social welfare and reform.

Article 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs. Every religious denomination has the right to establish and maintain institutions, manage its own affairs in matters of religion, own and acquire property, and administer such property.

Article 27: Freedom from payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion.

Article 28: Freedom as to attendance at religious instruction or worship in certain educational institutions. No religious instruction in institutions wholly maintained by State funds. In institutions administered by the State but established under any endowment or trust requiring religious instruction, such instruction may be imparted. No person attending any educational institution recognised by the State or receiving State aid shall be required to take part in religious instruction or attend religious worship without consent.

### Cultural and Educational Rights

Article 29: Protection of interests of minorities. Any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it. No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving State aid on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 30: Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. All minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The State shall not discriminate against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority.

### Right to Constitutional Remedies

Article 32: The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court can issue writs – habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto. Dr B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution. This right itself is a Fundamental Right and cannot be suspended except as provided in the Constitution (during Emergency under Article 359, certain rights can be suspended).

High Courts have similar power under Article 226, which is wider as it can be used for enforcement of any legal right, not only Fundamental Rights.

## Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)

Directive Principles are contained in Part IV (Articles 36 to 51). They are non-justiciable, meaning courts cannot enforce them. They are fundamental in the governance of the country and it is the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

They aim to establish a welfare state and social and economic democracy.

### Classification

Socialistic principles: Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47.

Gandhian principles: Articles 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48.

Liberal-intellectual principles: Articles 44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51.

Key Articles:

Article 38: State to secure a social order for the promotion of welfare of the people and to minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.

Article 39: Certain principles of policy – adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, protection of health and strength of workers and children, equal justice and free legal aid (39A).

Article 40: Organisation of village panchayats.

Article 41: Right to work, to education and to public assistance in certain cases.

Article 44: Uniform civil code for the citizens.

Article 45: Provision for early childhood care and education to children below the age of six years (after 86th Amendment; earlier it was free and compulsory education for children up to 14 years).

Article 48A: Protection and improvement of environment and safeguarding of forests and wildlife.

Article 50: Separation of judiciary from executive.

Article 51: Promotion of international peace and security.

The relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles has evolved through judicial decisions. Initially Fundamental Rights prevailed. After the 25th and 42nd Amendments and the Minerva Mills case, a harmonious construction is preferred. Both are complementary. Fundamental Rights represent political democracy; Directive Principles represent social and economic democracy.

## Fundamental Duties (Part IVA)

Added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Originally ten duties; the 86th Amendment added the eleventh duty regarding education of children.

Article 51A lists the duties. They are non-justiciable but serve as a reminder that citizens have responsibilities along with rights. Courts can use them to interpret laws and to uphold reasonable restrictions on Fundamental Rights.

The duties include abiding by the Constitution, cherishing noble ideals of the freedom struggle, upholding sovereignty and integrity, defending the country, promoting harmony, preserving heritage, protecting environment, developing scientific temper, safeguarding public property, striving for excellence, and providing education to children between 6 and 14 years.

## Key Takeaways for Memorisation

- Constitution adopted 26 November 1949, enforced 26 January 1950.
- Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati).
- Basic structure doctrine from Kesavananda Bharati case.
- Fundamental Rights are justiciable; Directive Principles are not.
- Article 32 is the heart and soul of the Constitution.
- Right to Property is no longer a Fundamental Right.
- Secular and Socialist added by 42nd Amendment.
- Fundamental Duties added by 42nd Amendment.

This foundation of the constitutional framework is essential for both Prelims and Mains. Understanding the philosophy behind the provisions helps in answering analytical questions.
