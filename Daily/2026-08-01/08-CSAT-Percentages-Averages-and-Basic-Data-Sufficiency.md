# CSAT: Percentages, Averages and Basic Data Sufficiency

## Percentages

Percentage means “per hundred.” It is a way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100. The symbol is %.

**Basic relations**
- x% of a number N = (x/100) × N
- To convert a fraction to a percentage: multiply by 100.
- To convert a percentage to a fraction: divide by 100.
- To convert a percentage to a decimal: move the decimal point two places to the left.

**Percentage change**
- Percentage increase = [(New value – Original value)/Original value] × 100
- Percentage decrease = [(Original value – New value)/Original value] × 100
- If a value increases by x% and then decreases by x%, there is a net decrease of (x²/100)%.
- Successive percentage changes: if a quantity increases by a% and then by b%, the net change is a + b + (ab/100).

**Applications commonly tested**
- Population growth or decline.
- Price increase or discount (marked price, selling price, cost price relationships).
- Examination marks and pass percentages.
- Mixture and allegation problems involving percentages.
- Data interpretation: percentage share, percentage growth over years, comparison of percentages.

**Useful shortcuts and observations**
- 10% of a number = number / 10; 5% = number / 20; 1% = number / 100.
- To find what percentage A is of B: (A/B) × 100.
- If A is x% more than B, then B is [x/(100+x)] × 100% less than A.
- If A is x% less than B, then B is [x/(100–x)] × 100% more than A.

**Practice orientation**
Focus on translating word statements into percentage equations quickly and accurately. Avoid calculation errors by estimating when options allow and by checking the reasonableness of the answer.

## Averages

The average (arithmetic mean) of a set of numbers is the sum of the numbers divided by the number of items.

Average = (Sum of observations) / (Number of observations)

**Important properties and techniques**
- If each observation is increased or decreased by a constant k, the average also increases or decreases by k.
- If each observation is multiplied or divided by a constant k, the average is also multiplied or divided by k.
- Combined average of two groups: if group 1 has n1 members with average a1 and group 2 has n2 members with average a2, the combined average is (n1·a1 + n2·a2) / (n1 + n2).
- When a new observation is added or an existing one is removed or replaced, the change in average can be used to find the value of the new or removed observation.
- Average of consecutive numbers or arithmetic progressions can often be found by taking the middle term(s).

**Weighted average**
When different groups have different sizes or different importance, the weighted average is used. It is the sum of (value × weight) divided by the sum of weights.

**Applications**
- Average marks, average age, average speed (note: average speed is total distance / total time, not the arithmetic mean of speeds unless times are equal).
- Average rainfall, temperature, production, etc., in data interpretation.
- Problems involving averages of different groups and the effect of adding or removing members.

**Common pitfalls**
- Confusing average speed with arithmetic mean of speeds.
- Forgetting that average is affected by extreme values (outliers).
- Errors in calculating combined averages when group sizes differ significantly.

## Basic Data Sufficiency

Data Sufficiency questions test the ability to determine whether the given information is adequate to answer a question, rather than necessarily finding the numerical answer itself.

**Typical structure**
A question is followed by two statements labelled (1) and (2). The options usually are:
- (a) Statement (1) alone is sufficient, but statement (2) alone is not.
- (b) Statement (2) alone is sufficient, but statement (1) alone is not.
- (c) Both statements together are sufficient, but neither alone is sufficient.
- (d) Each statement alone is sufficient.
- (e) Statements together are not sufficient.

**Approach**
1. Read the question carefully and understand exactly what is being asked.
2. Examine statement (1) alone. Decide whether it is sufficient to answer the question definitively.
3. Examine statement (2) alone in the same way.
4. If neither is sufficient alone, examine them together.
5. Avoid actually solving for the numerical value unless necessary; the goal is to judge sufficiency.

**Key principles**
- Sufficiency means that a unique answer can be determined.
- If a statement leads to more than one possible value or leaves the answer ambiguous, it is not sufficient.
- Do not import outside knowledge or assumptions not supported by the statements.
- In questions involving equations, check the number of independent equations versus unknowns.
- In percentage or average questions, ensure that the statements provide the necessary absolute or relative values.

**Common question types**
- Finding the value of a variable or an expression.
- Comparing two quantities.
- Determining whether a number is odd/even, positive/negative, prime, etc.
- Questions on ages, averages, percentages, ratios, and basic arithmetic.

**Practice focus**
Develop the habit of systematically checking each statement alone before combining them. Time management is important; do not spend excessive time calculating when the structure of the information already reveals sufficiency or insufficiency.

## Integrated Application

Many CSAT questions combine percentages and averages, or embed them inside data interpretation sets or data sufficiency formats. Regular practice with mixed problem sets builds both speed and accuracy. Emphasis should remain on conceptual clarity, careful reading of the question, and disciplined elimination of options.

Mastery of percentages and averages provides a strong foundation for a large proportion of quantitative aptitude questions in the CSAT paper, while data sufficiency trains analytical judgement under time pressure.
