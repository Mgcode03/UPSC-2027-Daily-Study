# The Revolt of 1857: Causes, Course, Nature and Consequences

## Introduction

The Revolt of 1857, also known as the First War of Independence, the Sepoy Mutiny, or the Great Rebellion, was a major, though ultimately unsuccessful, uprising against the British East India Company’s rule in India. It began as a mutiny of sepoys of the Company’s army on 10 May 1857 in the town of Meerut and soon escalated into other mutinies and civilian rebellions largely in the upper Gangetic plain and central India. The rebellion posed a considerable threat to British power and was suppressed only after prolonged fighting. It marked a turning point in the history of modern India and led to the end of Company rule and the beginning of direct Crown rule.

## Causes of the Revolt

The causes were multi-dimensional—political, economic, social, religious and military.

### Political Causes

- **Doctrine of Lapse**: Introduced by Lord Dalhousie, it denied the right of Hindu rulers to adopt an heir if they died without a natural one. States such as Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, Sambalpur and others were annexed. The Rani of Jhansi and Nana Sahib (adopted son of the last Peshwa) were directly affected and became leaders of the revolt.
- **Subsidiary Alliance and Annexation Policy**: Many Indian rulers had lost their independence and territories. The Nawab of Awadh was deposed in 1856 on the ground of misgovernance, even though Awadh had been a loyal ally. This caused widespread resentment among the taluqdars and people of Awadh.
- **Disrespect to Mughal Emperor**: The British decision to end the Mughal imperial title after Bahadur Shah Zafar and to move the royal family out of the Red Fort hurt Muslim sentiments.

### Economic Causes

- Heavy taxation and the ruin of traditional industries, especially textiles, due to the influx of British manufactured goods.
- Exploitation of peasants through high land revenue demands under the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems. Many peasants lost their lands to moneylenders and became landless labourers.
- The annexation of Awadh led to unemployment among the sepoys who came largely from that region, as well as among the retainers of the Nawab and taluqdars.

### Social and Religious Causes

- British social reforms such as the abolition of Sati (1829), the Widow Remarriage Act (1856), and the introduction of Western education were viewed by orthodox sections as interference in Indian social and religious customs.
- The activities of Christian missionaries and the fear of forced conversion created anxiety.
- The introduction of the new Enfield rifle in 1856 became the immediate trigger. The cartridges were rumoured to be greased with the fat of cows and pigs, offending both Hindu and Muslim sepoys. The requirement to bite the cartridge before loading was seen as a deliberate attempt to destroy their religion.

### Military Causes

- Indian sepoys outnumbered British soldiers roughly 5:1 or 6:1.
- Discrimination in pay, promotions and allowances. Indian soldiers could not rise above the rank of subedar.
- The General Service Enlistment Act of 1856 required new recruits to serve overseas if required, which was against the religious beliefs of high-caste Hindus who regarded crossing the sea as polluting.
- Withdrawal of the foreign service allowance (bhatta) when serving in newly annexed territories such as Sindh and Punjab.

## Course of the Revolt

### Outbreak and Spread

- 29 March 1857: Mangal Pandey of the 34th Native Infantry at Barrackpore attacked British officers and was executed.
- 10 May 1857: Sepoys at Meerut refused to use the new cartridges, were court-martialled and imprisoned. Their comrades released them, killed British officers, and marched to Delhi.
- 11–12 May: Delhi was captured. Bahadur Shah Zafar was proclaimed Emperor of Hindustan. The revolt now had a symbolic centre.
- The rebellion spread rapidly to Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Bareilly, Banaras, Allahabad, parts of Bihar, and central India.

### Major Centres and Leaders

- **Delhi**: Bahadur Shah Zafar (nominal), actual leadership under General Bakht Khan.
- **Kanpur**: Nana Sahib, assisted by Tantia Tope and Azimullah Khan.
- **Lucknow**: Begum Hazrat Mahal (wife of the deposed Nawab of Awadh).
- **Jhansi and Gwalior**: Rani Lakshmibai, later joined by Tantia Tope.
- **Bihar**: Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur, an elderly zamindar who fought with remarkable skill.
- **Bareilly and Rohilkhand**: Khan Bahadur Khan.
- **Faizabad**: Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah.

### British Response and Suppression

The British, initially caught by surprise, recovered with reinforcements from Britain and other parts of India. Key British commanders included John Lawrence, Henry Havelock, James Outram, Colin Campbell and Hugh Rose.

- Delhi was recaptured in September 1857 after fierce fighting. Bahadur Shah was captured and exiled to Rangoon; his sons were executed.
- Kanpur was retaken; Nana Sahib escaped (his subsequent fate remains uncertain).
- Lucknow was finally relieved and captured after prolonged operations.
- Rani Lakshmibai died fighting in June 1858 near Gwalior. Tantia Tope was captured and executed in 1859.
- Kunwar Singh died of wounds in 1858.

By mid-1858 the revolt had been largely suppressed, though sporadic resistance continued into 1859.

## Nature of the Revolt

Historians have debated whether it was a mere sepoy mutiny or a national war of independence.

- **Sepoy Mutiny view** (contemporary British writers): It was primarily a military rising caused by the cartridge issue, with limited civilian participation.
- **Nationalist view** (V.D. Savarkar and others): It was the First War of Independence, a conscious attempt by Indians to overthrow British rule.
- **Modern balanced view**: It was more than a mutiny but less than a fully coordinated national war. It involved sepoys, peasants, dispossessed zamindars, and some princes. There was a common anti-British sentiment, yet it lacked unified leadership, clear political programme, and pan-Indian participation. Large parts of India (Punjab, Bengal, Madras, Bombay Presidency, most of Rajputana and the Nizam’s territories) remained largely unaffected or even supported the British. Regional, caste and religious differences limited its scope.

## Consequences

### Administrative and Political

- The Government of India Act 1858 ended the rule of the East India Company. India came under the direct rule of the British Crown.
- A Secretary of State for India, assisted by a Council, was appointed in Britain. The Governor-General became the Viceroy.
- The policy of annexation was abandoned. Indian princes were assured that their territories would not be annexed and that the Doctrine of Lapse would be given up. They were to be treated as allies.
- The army was reorganised. The proportion of British to Indian soldiers was increased. Recruitment shifted away from Awadh, Bihar and central India towards Punjab, Nepal and the North-West. Artillery was placed largely under British control.

### Social and Economic

- The British became more cautious about social legislation. Interference in religious and social matters was reduced.
- Racial discrimination and the gulf between rulers and ruled widened.
- Economic exploitation continued, but the structure of governance became more bureaucratic and centralised.

### Impact on National Consciousness

Although the revolt failed, it left a deep imprint. It demonstrated that British rule was not invincible and that Indians could unite across regions and communities against a common enemy. The memory of 1857, the sacrifices of leaders such as Rani Lakshmibai, Nana Sahib and Kunwar Singh, later inspired generations of nationalists. It marked the end of one phase of resistance and the beginning of a longer, more organised struggle for independence.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Immediate cause: greased cartridges of the Enfield rifle.
- Political spark: Doctrine of Lapse and annexation of Awadh.
- Symbolic head: Bahadur Shah Zafar at Delhi.
- Important leaders: Nana Sahib (Kanpur), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi), Kunwar Singh (Bihar), Tantia Tope, Bakht Khan.
- End of Company rule: Government of India Act 1858.
- Nature: widespread but localised, multi-class, anti-British uprising with both traditional and modern elements.

The Revolt of 1857 remains a foundational event in modern Indian history, marking both the culmination of early resistance and the starting point of a new phase of colonial rule and nationalist awakening.
