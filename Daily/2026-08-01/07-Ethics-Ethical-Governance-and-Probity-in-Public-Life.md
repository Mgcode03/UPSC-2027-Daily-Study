# Ethical Governance and Probity in Public Life

## Introduction

Ethical governance refers to the conduct of public affairs in a manner that is consistent with principles of integrity, transparency, accountability, fairness and public interest. Probity in public life means uprightness, honesty and integrity in the discharge of public duties. In a democracy, the legitimacy of institutions and the trust of citizens depend heavily on the ethical standards observed by public functionaries. The Indian Constitution, various statutes, codes of conduct, and the recommendations of committees such as the Santhanam Committee and the Second Administrative Reforms Commission provide the normative framework for ethical governance.

## Core Values of Public Service

Public servants are expected to uphold certain foundational values:

- **Integrity**: Consistency between principles, words and actions; honesty in financial and non-financial matters; avoidance of conflicts of interest.
- **Impartiality and Non-partisanship**: Decisions based on merit and public interest rather than personal, political or sectional considerations.
- **Objectivity**: Decisions based on evidence and rational analysis.
- **Accountability**: Answerability for actions and decisions to the appropriate authorities and ultimately to the public.
- **Transparency**: Openness in decision-making processes and access to information, subject to legitimate confidentiality requirements.
- **Dedication to Public Service**: Priority to public interest over personal interest.
- **Compassion and Empathy**: Sensitivity to the needs of the vulnerable and the ability to understand the impact of decisions on citizens.
- **Tolerance and Responsiveness**: Respect for diversity and willingness to listen and respond to public concerns.

These values are interrelated. The absence of one often weakens the others.

## Probity in Governance

Probity goes beyond mere compliance with rules. It involves a commitment to ethical behaviour even in situations where rules may be silent or ambiguous. Key elements include:

- Avoidance of corruption in all its forms (bribery, nepotism, favouritism, misuse of official position).
- Proper management of conflicts of interest: disclosure, recusal, and divestment where necessary.
- Responsible use of public resources and assets.
- Maintenance of confidentiality where required and protection of whistle-blowers.
- Fairness in the exercise of discretionary powers.

Corruption undermines development, distorts public policy, erodes trust, and disproportionately harms the poor. Ethical governance is therefore both a moral imperative and a practical necessity for effective administration and inclusive growth.

## Institutional and Legal Framework for Probity

- **Constitutional provisions**: Oath of office for ministers and constitutional functionaries; provisions relating to the Comptroller and Auditor General, Election Commission, and independent judiciary.
- **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988** (as amended): Defines offences relating to public servants taking gratification, criminal misconduct, etc., and provides for investigation and prosecution.
- **Right to Information Act, 2005**: Empowers citizens to seek information and thereby promotes transparency and accountability.
- **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013**: Creates institutions for inquiring into allegations of corruption against public functionaries.
- **Central Vigilance Commission**: Apex vigilance institution for the central government.
- **Codes of Conduct**: For ministers, civil servants, and members of the judiciary and other bodies.
- **Whistle Blowers Protection Act**: Aims to protect persons who expose corruption or wilful misuse of power.
- Judicial pronouncements emphasising the need for integrity in public life and the concept of public office as a public trust.

## Challenges to Ethical Governance

- Political interference and the pressure to favour particular interests.
- Weak enforcement of existing laws and delays in investigation and prosecution.
- Complex and opaque procedures that create opportunities for rent-seeking.
- Inadequate protection and incentives for honest officials and whistle-blowers.
- Social tolerance or resignation towards corruption in certain contexts.
- Conflicts between personal loyalty, organisational loyalty and public interest.
- Emerging challenges related to technology, data privacy, and new forms of influence.

## Promoting Ethical Governance

- Strong leadership that sets an example of integrity (tone from the top).
- Simplification of rules and procedures and reduction of unnecessary discretion.
- Effective and independent vigilance and anti-corruption institutions.
- Transparency mechanisms, including proactive disclosure and open data.
- Training and sensitisation of public servants in ethics and values.
- Protection of honest decision-making and insulation from undue pressure.
- Citizen engagement, social audits, and grievance redressal systems.
- Ethical education from an early stage and reinforcement of values in society.

## Ethical Dilemmas in Public Life

Public servants frequently face situations in which values conflict. Examples include:

- Pressure from superiors or political executives to act in a manner that is legally permissible but ethically questionable.
- Conflict between rule-following and the need for compassionate or flexible responses in individual cases.
- Balancing transparency with the requirements of confidentiality and national security.
- Managing personal relationships and social expectations without compromising impartiality.

Resolving such dilemmas requires a clear understanding of the hierarchy of values, the ability to reason ethically, and the moral courage to act on one’s conclusions. Frameworks such as the “public interest” test, the “sunshine” test (would I be comfortable if this decision became public?), and consultation with codes and experienced colleagues can help.

## Conclusion

Ethical governance and probity are not optional extras; they are central to the quality of democracy and the effectiveness of the state. While laws and institutions provide the necessary architecture, the ultimate foundation is the personal integrity and ethical competence of those who hold public power. Cultivating these qualities is a continuous responsibility for individuals, institutions and society as a whole.
