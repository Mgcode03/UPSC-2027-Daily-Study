# Emergency Provisions in the Indian Constitution

## Introduction

The Indian Constitution provides for three types of emergencies to enable the Union Government to deal with extraordinary situations that threaten the security, financial stability, or constitutional machinery of the country. These provisions are contained in Part XVIII (Articles 352 to 360). The framers of the Constitution borrowed the concept largely from the Government of India Act, 1935, and the Weimar Constitution of Germany, while adapting it to Indian conditions. The idea is to temporarily alter the normal federal balance and expand the powers of the Centre so that the nation can respond effectively to crises.

Emergency provisions are extraordinary measures. They suspend or modify the normal functioning of the Constitution and can affect fundamental rights, the autonomy of states, and the distribution of financial resources. Because of their far-reaching consequences, they are surrounded by procedural safeguards and have been subject to significant judicial scrutiny and constitutional amendments over time.

## Types of Emergencies

### 1. National Emergency (Article 352)

A National Emergency can be proclaimed by the President when the security of India or any part of its territory is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. Originally the ground was “internal disturbance,” but the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 replaced it with “armed rebellion” to prevent misuse.

**Proclamation Procedure**
- The President can issue the proclamation only on the written recommendation of the Union Cabinet (Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister).
- The proclamation must be laid before both Houses of Parliament.
- It must be approved by resolutions of both Houses within one month; otherwise it ceases to operate.
- Once approved, it remains in force for six months and can be extended indefinitely by successive parliamentary approvals every six months.
- A special majority is required for approval and extension: a majority of the total membership of each House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.

**Effects of National Emergency**

- **Centre-State Relations**: The executive power of the Union extends to giving directions to any state on any matter. Parliament can make laws on subjects in the State List. The normal distribution of legislative powers is suspended in favour of the Centre.
- **Fundamental Rights**: Article 358 suspends the six freedoms under Article 19 automatically when the emergency is on the ground of war or external aggression. Article 359 allows the President to suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of other Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 and 21 after the 44th Amendment).
- **Parliament**: The life of the Lok Sabha can be extended by one year at a time beyond its normal five-year term, but not beyond six months after the emergency ends.
- **Financial Relations**: The President can modify the distribution of revenues between the Centre and the states.

**Historical Instances**
- 1962: External aggression by China.
- 1971: External aggression related to the Bangladesh War.
- 1975: Internal disturbance (later replaced by armed rebellion as ground). This emergency was imposed on the advice of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and remains the most controversial.

### 2. President’s Rule / State Emergency (Article 356)

This is also known as constitutional emergency or failure of constitutional machinery in a state. The President can issue a proclamation if, on receipt of a report from the Governor of a state or otherwise, he is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution.

**Key Features**
- The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months.
- Once approved, it remains in force for six months and can be extended for a maximum period of three years, subject to parliamentary approval every six months. Beyond one year, certain conditions must be met (National Emergency in operation or Election Commission certifying that elections cannot be held).
- During President’s Rule, the President assumes all or any of the functions of the state government and the powers of the Governor. The state legislature may be suspended or dissolved. Parliament can make laws for the state.
- The 44th Amendment and later judicial pronouncements (especially S.R. Bommai case, 1994) have imposed significant restrictions. The Supreme Court held that the proclamation is subject to judicial review. The Court can examine whether the material on which the President acted was relevant and whether the imposition was mala fide.

**Important Judicial Principles from S.R. Bommai Case**
- The satisfaction of the President is not beyond judicial review.
- Floor test is the proper method to test majority support in the Assembly.
- Secularism is a basic feature; a state government acting against secularism can be dismissed.
- The power under Article 356 is exceptional and should be used sparingly.

### 3. Financial Emergency (Article 360)

A Financial Emergency can be proclaimed if the President is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.

**Effects**
- The executive authority of the Union extends to giving directions to any state to observe such canons of financial propriety as may be specified.
- Directions may include reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the state, including judges of the High Court.
- The President may issue directions for the reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving the Union, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
- All Money Bills and other financial bills of states require the consideration of the President.

No Financial Emergency has ever been proclaimed in India so far.

## Safeguards Introduced by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978

The experience of the 1975 Emergency led to several important safeguards:
- Written recommendation of the Cabinet is mandatory for National Emergency.
- “Internal disturbance” was replaced by “armed rebellion.”
- Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even during National Emergency.
- The period for parliamentary approval of National Emergency was reduced from two months to one month.
- A proclamation of National Emergency can be revoked by a subsequent proclamation, and it must be laid before Parliament.
- The Lok Sabha can pass a resolution disapproving the continuation of the Emergency, after which the President must revoke it.

## Critical Evaluation

Emergency provisions have been both a strength and a source of controversy. They provide the necessary flexibility to deal with existential threats to the nation. At the same time, their misuse, particularly in 1975 and in numerous impositions of President’s Rule for political reasons, has raised concerns about the erosion of federalism and democratic values.

The judiciary has played a crucial role in checking excesses. The basic structure doctrine, the Bommai judgment, and subsequent cases have established that emergency powers are not absolute and must conform to constitutional limitations.

Key points to remember:
- Three types: National (352), State (356), Financial (360).
- National Emergency affects the entire country or parts; it alters federal structure and can suspend fundamental rights.
- President’s Rule is the most frequently used and the most litigated.
- Financial Emergency remains unused.
- 44th Amendment and judicial review are the principal safeguards.

Understanding emergency provisions is essential for grasping the flexibility and resilience built into the Indian Constitution, as well as the constant tension between security, federalism, and individual rights.
