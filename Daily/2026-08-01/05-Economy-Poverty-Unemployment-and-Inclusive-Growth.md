# Poverty, Unemployment and Inclusive Growth in India

## Introduction

Poverty and unemployment are among the most persistent challenges facing the Indian economy. Despite significant progress in economic growth since the 1980s and especially after the 1991 reforms, large sections of the population continue to experience deprivation in income, health, education and living standards. Inclusive growth refers to a process of economic growth that creates opportunities for all sections of society and distributes the benefits of prosperity more equitably. Understanding the concepts, measurement, causes, trends and policy responses related to poverty and unemployment is central to the study of the Indian economy.

## Concept and Measurement of Poverty

Poverty can be understood in absolute and relative terms. Absolute poverty refers to the inability to satisfy basic minimum needs of food, clothing and shelter. Relative poverty refers to inequality in the distribution of income and wealth.

In India, poverty has traditionally been measured using a poverty line based on calorie norms and later on consumption expenditure required to meet basic needs. The Planning Commission and later expert groups (Alagh, Lakdawala, Tendulkar, Rangarajan) estimated poverty lines and poverty ratios.

- Tendulkar Committee (2009) shifted from calorie-based to a broader basket including education, health and other expenditures, and used Mixed Reference Period.
- Rangarajan Committee (2014) suggested a higher poverty line.
- Official estimates have shown a substantial decline in the poverty ratio over the decades, though the absolute number of poor remained large for a long time.

Multidimensional poverty measures (such as the Global Multidimensional Poverty Index used by NITI Aayog) capture deprivations in health, education and living standards beyond income. These show significant reductions in recent years, reflecting improvements in access to electricity, sanitation, cooking fuel, banking, and other amenities.

## Causes of Poverty

- Low productivity in agriculture and dependence of a large workforce on it.
- Underemployment and seasonal unemployment in rural areas.
- Slow growth of employment in the organised manufacturing sector.
- Unequal distribution of land and other assets.
- Social factors: caste, gender and regional disparities that limit access to education, credit and opportunities.
- Population pressure and demographic factors.
- Historical factors related to colonial exploitation and the structure of the post-independence economy.

## Unemployment: Concepts and Types

Unemployment exists when people who are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage rate cannot find jobs. In a developing economy like India, open unemployment coexists with various forms of underemployment and disguised unemployment.

**Types relevant to India**
- **Disguised unemployment**: More people are engaged in an activity (especially agriculture) than required; the marginal productivity of labour is close to zero.
- **Seasonal unemployment**: Common in agriculture and agro-based industries; workers are employed only during certain seasons.
- **Open unemployment**: Visible lack of work.
- **Educated unemployment**: Graduates and other educated persons unable to find jobs matching their qualifications.
- **Structural unemployment**: Mismatch between the skills of the workforce and the requirements of the labour market.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: Related to business cycles (less prominent in India than in developed economies).

**Measurement**
The National Sample Survey Office (and now Periodic Labour Force Survey conducted by NSO) uses three main approaches:
- Usual Status (principal and subsidiary).
- Current Weekly Status.
- Current Daily Status.

The unemployment rate is generally higher under the Current Daily Status, which captures underemployment better. Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR), Worker Population Ratio (WPR) and Unemployment Rate are the key indicators. Female LFPR in India has been relatively low, though recent data show some improvement.

## Trends and Challenges

Economic growth in India has been described as “jobless” or “job-poor” in certain periods, particularly in organised manufacturing. The services sector has absorbed more labour, but much of it is in the informal segment with low productivity and limited social security. The informal sector employs the vast majority of the workforce.

Youth unemployment and the challenge of absorbing the demographic dividend are critical issues. The quality of employment (regular wage jobs versus casual labour, social security coverage, working conditions) is as important as the quantity of jobs.

## Inclusive Growth and Policy Responses

Inclusive growth aims at both faster growth and broader participation in and benefits from that growth. It requires expansion of opportunities in education, health, skills, credit, infrastructure and productive employment, along with social protection for the vulnerable.

**Major policy interventions**

- **Employment programmes**: Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) provides a legal guarantee of 100 days of wage employment in rural areas. It has served as a social safety net, created rural assets, and empowered women and marginalised groups to some extent.
- **Skill development**: National Skill Development Mission and related programmes aim to bridge the skill gap.
- **Social security and welfare**: Public Distribution System (and its reforms such as NFSA), Direct Benefit Transfers, pensions, insurance schemes (Ayushman Bharat, PM-JAY), housing (PMAY), and sanitation (Swachh Bharat).
- **Financial inclusion**: Jan Dhan accounts, digital payments, credit to priority sectors and microfinance.
- **Agricultural and rural development**: Irrigation, credit, marketing reforms, support for farmer producer organisations, and schemes for income support.
- **Education and health**: Expansion of schooling, mid-day meals, higher education and health infrastructure to improve human capital.
- **Industrial and MSME policy**: Support for micro, small and medium enterprises which are labour-intensive, along with Production Linked Incentive schemes for manufacturing.

## Critical Issues and Way Forward

Persistent challenges include the slow structural transformation of the workforce out of low-productivity agriculture, the dominance of informal employment, regional disparities, gender gaps in labour force participation, and the need for higher quality education and skills.

Sustainable reduction in poverty and unemployment requires:
- Higher and more labour-intensive growth, particularly in manufacturing and productive services.
- Investment in human capital and continuous skill upgradation.
- Strengthening of social protection systems that are portable and cover the informal workforce.
- Improvement in the ease of doing business and removal of rigidities that discourage formal job creation.
- Focus on lagging regions and disadvantaged social groups.

Poverty and unemployment are not merely economic statistics; they reflect the lived experience of millions and the effectiveness of the development process. Inclusive growth remains both an economic necessity and a social imperative for India.
