# Environmental Laws in India and Major International Conventions

## Introduction

Environmental protection in India is supported by a combination of constitutional provisions, statutory laws, regulatory institutions and judicial interventions. At the global level, India is a party to several multilateral environmental agreements that shape national policy and action. A clear understanding of the key laws, their objectives, institutional mechanisms and the major international conventions is essential for both Prelims and Mains.

## Constitutional Foundations

- Article 48A (Directive Principles): The State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard the forests and wildlife of the country.
- Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duties): It shall be the duty of every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
- Article 21: The right to life has been interpreted by the Supreme Court to include the right to a healthy environment.
- The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 explicitly introduced environmental protection into the Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties.

## Major Environmental Laws

### The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

Provides for the protection of wild animals, birds and plants. It establishes schedules of protected species, creates protected areas (National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, Conservation Reserves, Community Reserves), regulates hunting, trade and possession of wildlife, and provides for the constitution of the National Board for Wildlife and State Boards. It has been amended several times to strengthen penalties and expand the scope of protection.

### The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

Aims to prevent and control water pollution and maintain or restore the wholesomeness of water. It establishes the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). The Boards have powers to set standards, inspect, take samples, and take legal action against polluters. Consent of the Board is required for establishing industries that may discharge sewage or trade effluent.

### The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Similar framework for air pollution. Empowers CPCB and SPCBs to set emission standards, declare air pollution control areas, and regulate industrial and other sources of air pollution. The Act was prompted in part by the Bhopal gas tragedy and growing urban air quality concerns.

### The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

An umbrella legislation enacted in the wake of the Bhopal disaster. It empowers the Central Government to take all necessary measures for protecting and improving the quality of the environment and preventing, controlling and abating environmental pollution. It provides for the setting of standards for emissions and discharges, regulation of the location of industries, management of hazardous substances, and the creation of authorities for specific purposes. Many subsequent rules (Hazardous Waste Rules, EIA Notification, Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, Plastic Waste Rules, etc.) have been framed under this Act.

### The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980

Restricts the de-reservation of forests or use of forest land for non-forest purposes without the prior approval of the Central Government. It aims to check deforestation and diversion of forest land.

### The Biological Diversity Act, 2002

Enacted to give effect to the Convention on Biological Diversity. It provides for conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the use of biological resources and associated traditional knowledge. It establishes the National Biodiversity Authority, State Biodiversity Boards and Biodiversity Management Committees at the local level.

### Other Important Legislations and Rules

- Indian Forest Act, 1927 (colonial legacy, still relevant for forest administration).
- Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.
- National Green Tribunal Act, 2010: establishes the National Green Tribunal for effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection and conservation of forests and other natural resources.
- Various rules on solid waste, plastic waste, e-waste, construction and demolition waste, biomedical waste, and the Environmental Impact Assessment Notification.

## Institutional Framework

- Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).
- Central Pollution Control Board and State Pollution Control Boards.
- National Green Tribunal.
- Forest Departments and Wildlife authorities at Centre and State levels.
- National Biodiversity Authority and State Boards.
- Central Zoo Authority, Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, etc.

The judiciary, particularly the Supreme Court and High Courts through Public Interest Litigation, and the National Green Tribunal have played a proactive role in environmental governance.

## Major International Environmental Conventions and Protocols

### Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992

Objectives: conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits. India is a party and has enacted the Biological Diversity Act. Related protocols include the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety and the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing.

### United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 1992

The parent treaty for global climate action. The Kyoto Protocol (1997) set binding emission reduction targets for developed countries. The Paris Agreement (2015) requires all parties to submit Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and aims to limit global temperature rise to well below 2°C, preferably 1.5°C, above pre-industrial levels. India has submitted NDCs and updated them, emphasising renewable energy, non-fossil capacity, emissions intensity reduction, and creation of carbon sinks.

### Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer, 1987

Highly successful treaty for the phase-out of ozone-depleting substances (CFCs, halons, etc.). The Kigali Amendment (2016) adds hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) to the list of controlled substances. India is a party and has been implementing phase-out schedules.

### Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), 1973

Regulates international trade in endangered species through a system of permits and appendices. India is a party and implements CITES through the Wildlife (Protection) Act and related measures.

### Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, 1971

Provides for the conservation and wise use of wetlands. India has designated numerous Ramsar sites and is active in wetland conservation.

### Basel Convention, 1989

Controls transboundary movements of hazardous wastes and their disposal. India is a party.

### Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, 2001

Aims to eliminate or restrict the production and use of persistent organic pollutants. India is a party.

### Other Relevant Agreements

- UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD).
- Convention on Migratory Species (CMS or Bonn Convention).
- Minamata Convention on Mercury.
- Various regional and bilateral arrangements.

## Key Principles in Environmental Law and Policy

- Sustainable development.
- Precautionary principle.
- Polluter pays principle.
- Public trust doctrine.
- Inter-generational equity.
- Absolute liability (developed in Indian jurisprudence after Bhopal and Oleum gas leak cases).

These principles have been repeatedly invoked by Indian courts and inform both domestic legislation and international commitments.

## Conclusion

India possesses a comprehensive legal and institutional framework for environmental protection, reinforced by an active judiciary. At the same time, implementation gaps, capacity constraints, and the pressures of development remain significant challenges. International conventions provide both obligations and opportunities for cooperation, technology transfer and finance. A balanced understanding of domestic laws and global agreements is necessary to appreciate the full scope of environmental governance in India.
