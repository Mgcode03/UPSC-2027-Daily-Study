# Ancient Indian Philosophy and Schools of Thought

## Overview of Indian Philosophical Tradition

Ancient Indian philosophy developed over many centuries and is characterised by a rich diversity of schools that debated questions of reality, knowledge, ethics, liberation, and the nature of the self. These systems are traditionally classified into two broad categories: Astika (orthodox) schools that accept the authority of the Vedas, and Nastika (heterodox) schools that do not.

The primary goal in most systems is moksha or liberation from the cycle of birth and death (samsara). The methods and metaphysical frameworks differ significantly.

## The Six Orthodox (Astika) Schools

### 1. Nyaya

Nyaya is primarily a school of logic and epistemology. Founded by Gautama (Akshapada), it focuses on the means of valid knowledge (pramanas).

Four accepted pramanas:
- Perception (pratyaksha)
- Inference (anumana)
- Comparison (upamana)
- Testimony (shabda)

Nyaya developed sophisticated theories of inference, including the five-membered syllogism. It defends the existence of a personal God (Ishvara) as the efficient cause of the world and the author of the Vedas. Liberation is achieved through true knowledge that removes ignorance and the consequent cycle of rebirth.

### 2. Vaisheshika

Vaisheshika, associated with Kanada, is a school of atomism and particularism. It classifies reality into six (later seven) categories (padarthas):

- Substance (dravya)
- Quality (guna)
- Action (karma)
- Generality (samanya)
- Particularity (vishesha)
- Inherence (samavaya)
- Non-existence (abhava) – added later

The world is composed of eternal atoms of earth, water, fire, and air. Atoms combine under the guidance of an unseen force or later under divine will. Vaisheshika is closely allied with Nyaya; the combined Nyaya-Vaisheshika system became a major philosophical tradition.

### 3. Samkhya

Samkhya, attributed to Kapila, is a dualistic system. Reality consists of two independent principles:

- Purusha: pure consciousness, passive, plural
- Prakriti: primordial matter, active, consisting of three gunas – sattva (equilibrium, knowledge), rajas (activity, passion), and tamas (inertia, ignorance)

The evolution of the world occurs when Prakriti is disturbed in the presence of Purusha. The 24 (or 25) tattvas include mahat (intellect), ahamkara (ego), manas (mind), sense organs, and the five elements.

Liberation (kaivalya) is the isolation of Purusha from Prakriti through discriminative knowledge. Classical Samkhya is atheistic; later versions sometimes incorporate a theistic element.

### 4. Yoga

Yoga, systematised by Patanjali in the Yoga Sutras, accepts the metaphysics of Samkhya but adds a practical path and a concept of God (Ishvara) as a special Purusha unaffected by afflictions.

The eight limbs (ashtanga) of Yoga:
1. Yama – ethical restraints (non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy/restraint, non-possessiveness)
2. Niyama – observances (purity, contentment, austerity, self-study, surrender to God)
3. Asana – posture
4. Pranayama – breath control
5. Pratyahara – withdrawal of senses
6. Dharana – concentration
7. Dhyana – meditation
8. Samadhi – absorption or enlightenment

The goal is to still the fluctuations of the mind (chitta-vritti-nirodha) so that the pure consciousness of Purusha is realised.

### 5. Mimamsa (Purva Mimamsa)

Mimamsa, founded by Jaimini, focuses on the interpretation of Vedic ritual and the authority of the Vedas. It is primarily concerned with dharma as understood through ritual action.

Key ideas:
- The Vedas are eternal and authorless (apaurusheya).
- Dharma is known through Vedic injunctions.
- Ritual action produces an unseen potency (apurva) that leads to results, including heavenly rewards.
- Emphasis on orthopraxy (correct action) rather than orthodoxy of belief.

Later Mimamsa developed theories of language, meaning, and knowledge. Liberation is sometimes understood as the cessation of rebirth through the exhaustion of karma via correct performance of duties.

### 6. Vedanta (Uttara Mimamsa)

Vedanta bases itself on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras of Badarayana, and the Bhagavad Gita (the prasthanatrayi). It has several sub-schools:

- Advaita Vedanta (Shankara): Non-dualism. Brahman alone is real; the world is appearance (maya); the individual self (jiva) is identical with Brahman. Liberation is the realisation of this identity through knowledge (jnana).
- Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja): Qualified non-dualism. Brahman is qualified by souls and matter; souls are distinct yet inseparable attributes of God. Liberation involves eternal service and communion with God through devotion (bhakti).
- Dvaita (Madhva): Dualism. Strict distinction between God, souls, and matter. Liberation is the eternal proximity to God achieved through devotion and grace.
- Other schools include Dvaitadvaita, Shuddhadvaita, and Achintya Bhedabheda.

Vedanta became the most influential philosophical tradition in later Indian thought and continues to shape religious and intellectual life.

## Heterodox (Nastika) Schools

### Buddhism

Founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha). Core teachings:

- Four Noble Truths: suffering exists; it has a cause (craving); it can cease; the path to cessation is the Eightfold Path.
- Eightfold Path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration.
- Doctrine of dependent origination (pratityasamutpada).
- Anatta (no permanent self) and anicca (impermanence).
- Nirvana as the extinction of craving and liberation from rebirth.

Major schools: Theravada, Mahayana (including Madhyamaka and Yogacara), and later Vajrayana.

### Jainism

Attributed to Mahavira (though the tradition is older). Key doctrines:

- Extreme non-violence (ahimsa).
- Anekantavada (many-sidedness of reality) and syadvada (conditional predication).
- Theory of karma as subtle matter that binds the soul.
- Liberation through the three jewels: right faith, right knowledge, right conduct.
- Dualism of jiva (soul) and ajiva (non-soul).
- Ascetic practices and the ideal of the ford-makers (tirthankaras).

### Charvaka / Lokayata

A materialist and sceptical school. It rejected the authority of the Vedas, the existence of an afterlife, and supernatural entities. Perception was regarded as the only valid pramana. The goal of life was the pursuit of pleasure in this world. Few original texts survive; knowledge comes mainly from critiques by other schools.

## Common Themes and Debates

Indian philosophical schools engaged in sustained debates on:

- The nature of reality (monism, dualism, pluralism)
- Means of knowledge (pramanas)
- Existence and nature of the self
- Causation and change
- The problem of suffering and its solution
- Role of ritual, knowledge, and devotion
- Relationship between the individual and the ultimate

These debates refined concepts of logic, metaphysics, ethics, and soteriology that remain relevant for understanding Indian intellectual history and cultural traditions.

## Key Points for Retention

- Astika schools accept Vedic authority; Nastika do not.
- Nyaya-Vaisheshika: logic and atomism.
- Samkhya-Yoga: dualism of Purusha-Prakriti and practical path.
- Mimamsa: ritual and dharma.
- Vedanta: interpretation of Upanishadic teachings with major sub-schools.
- Buddhism and Jainism: independent paths to liberation emphasising ethics and insight.
- Charvaka: materialist counter-tradition.

A clear grasp of these systems provides the conceptual foundation for understanding later developments in Indian thought, religion, and culture.
