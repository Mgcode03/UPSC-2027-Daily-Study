# Biodiversity Conservation Strategies and Protected Area Network

## Understanding Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variability among living organisms from all sources, including terrestrial, marine, and other aquatic ecosystems, and the ecological complexes of which they are part. It includes diversity within species (genetic), between species, and of ecosystems.

India is one of the 17 megadiverse countries and contains four global biodiversity hotspots (Himalaya, Western Ghats, Indo-Burma, Sundaland – Nicobar). High endemism, varied ecosystems (from alpine to coastal and marine), and a long history of human interaction with nature characterise India’s biodiversity.

Threats to biodiversity include habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, invasive alien species, pollution, climate change, and underlying drivers such as population pressure and unsustainable consumption.

## Approaches to Conservation

Conservation strategies are broadly classified into in-situ and ex-situ approaches.

### In-situ Conservation

Conservation of species in their natural habitats. This is the preferred strategy because it maintains evolutionary processes and ecological interactions.

Main instruments:

- Protected Areas (PAs)
- Community conserved areas and sacred groves
- Biodiversity heritage sites
- Conservation reserves and community reserves
- Habitat restoration and corridor management

### Ex-situ Conservation

Conservation outside natural habitats. Used when in-situ measures are insufficient or for critically endangered species.

Instruments:

- Botanical gardens and zoological parks
- Seed banks, gene banks, pollen banks, tissue culture
- Captive breeding and reintroduction programmes
- Cryopreservation

Both approaches are complementary.

## Protected Area Network in India

India’s protected area network is established primarily under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, and subsequent amendments.

### Categories

1. National Parks
   - Highest level of protection.
   - No human activity except those permitted for conservation and research.
   - Boundary is relatively rigid.

2. Wildlife Sanctuaries
   - Protection of wildlife and habitat.
   - Certain regulated human activities may be allowed.
   - Greater flexibility than National Parks.

3. Conservation Reserves
   - Areas owned by the government adjacent to protected areas or connecting them.
   - Declared for protecting landscapes, seascapes, flora and fauna, and their habitat.
   - Community participation is encouraged.

4. Community Reserves
   - Areas where community or private land is voluntarily conserved.
   - Provides legal recognition to community initiatives.

5. Tiger Reserves
   - Notified under the Wildlife (Protection) Act following Project Tiger.
   - Consist of a core (critical tiger habitat) and a buffer zone.
   - Managed with a focus on tiger conservation while addressing livelihood issues in the buffer.

6. Biosphere Reserves
   - Larger areas recognised under UNESCO’s Man and the Biosphere Programme (though national designation also exists).
   - Zoned into core, buffer, and transition areas.
   - Aim to reconcile conservation with sustainable use and research.

Other categories include Elephant Reserves (under Project Elephant), Ramsar sites (wetlands of international importance), and Important Bird Areas.

## Project Tiger and Project Elephant

Project Tiger (launched 1973) is a centrally sponsored scheme aimed at tiger conservation through habitat protection, anti-poaching measures, and scientific management. Tiger Reserves form the backbone of the programme. Monitoring uses methods such as camera trapping and the All India Tiger Estimation.

Project Elephant focuses on elephant conservation, habitat protection, mitigation of human-elephant conflict, and welfare of captive elephants.

Both projects illustrate landscape-level approaches and the need to address ecological and social dimensions together.

## Legal and Institutional Framework

- Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (amended multiple times): Schedules listing protected species, provisions for protected areas, penalties, and the National and State Boards for Wildlife.
- Biological Diversity Act, 2002: Provides for conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use, and equitable sharing of benefits arising from use of biological resources and traditional knowledge. Establishes the National Biodiversity Authority, State Biodiversity Boards, and Biodiversity Management Committees.
- Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980: Regulates diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes.
- Environment (Protection) Act, 1986: Umbrella legislation enabling various rules and notifications.

Institutions include the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, National Tiger Conservation Authority, Wildlife Institute of India, Botanical Survey of India, Zoological Survey of India, and State Forest Departments.

## International Commitments

India is party to several conventions relevant to biodiversity:

- Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)
- Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)
- Convention on Wetlands (Ramsar)
- Convention on Migratory Species
- World Heritage Convention (natural sites)

National Biodiversity Targets and the National Biodiversity Action Plan align domestic efforts with global goals, including the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework.

## Challenges and Emerging Approaches

Challenges include:

- Habitat fragmentation and degradation
- Human-wildlife conflict
- Poaching and illegal wildlife trade
- Inadequate connectivity between protected areas
- Resource constraints and management effectiveness
- Balancing conservation with development and livelihood needs of local communities

Emerging approaches emphasise:

- Landscape and seascape level planning
- Ecological corridors and connectivity
- Community-based conservation and benefit sharing
- Integration of traditional knowledge
- Use of technology (camera traps, drones, DNA forensics, satellite monitoring)
- Climate change adaptation in conservation planning
- Mainstreaming biodiversity into sectoral policies

## Key Concepts for Retention

- Biodiversity has genetic, species, and ecosystem levels.
- In-situ conservation is primary; ex-situ is supportive.
- Protected Area categories under the Wildlife (Protection) Act provide graded protection.
- Project Tiger and Project Elephant are flagship programmes.
- Legal framework includes the Wildlife (Protection) Act and Biological Diversity Act.
- Effective conservation requires addressing both ecological and social dimensions and maintaining connectivity.

A thorough understanding of conservation strategies and the protected area network is essential for environmental questions in both Prelims and Mains.
