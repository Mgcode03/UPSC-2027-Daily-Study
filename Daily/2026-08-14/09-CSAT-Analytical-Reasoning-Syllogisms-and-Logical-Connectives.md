# Analytical Reasoning – Syllogisms and Logical Connectives

## Introduction to Analytical Reasoning in CSAT

Analytical reasoning tests the ability to understand structure, draw valid conclusions, and evaluate arguments. Two frequently tested areas are syllogisms (categorical reasoning) and logical connectives (propositional reasoning). Mastery requires understanding the underlying logical forms rather than relying solely on intuition or Venn diagram drawing under time pressure.

## Categorical Syllogisms

A categorical syllogism consists of two premises and a conclusion, each of which is a categorical proposition. Categorical propositions relate two classes or categories.

### Standard Forms of Categorical Propositions

- A: All S are P (universal affirmative)
- E: No S are P (universal negative)
- I: Some S are P (particular affirmative)
- O: Some S are not P (particular negative)

### Structure of a Syllogism

- Major premise: contains the major term (predicate of the conclusion)
- Minor premise: contains the minor term (subject of the conclusion)
- Conclusion: relates minor term to major term
- Middle term: appears in both premises but not in the conclusion

### Validity Rules (Traditional)

A syllogism is valid if the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises. Key rules include:

1. The middle term must be distributed at least once.
2. Any term distributed in the conclusion must be distributed in the premise.
3. From two negative premises, no conclusion follows.
4. If one premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative.
5. From two particular premises, no conclusion follows (in classical rules).
6. If one premise is particular, the conclusion must be particular.

Distribution:
- A distributes subject only
- E distributes both subject and predicate
- I distributes neither
- O distributes predicate only

### Immediate Inferences

From a single proposition one can draw:

- Conversion (with limitations for A and O)
- Obversion (always valid)
- Contraposition (with limitations)

Understanding these helps in checking equivalence and validity.

### Common Question Patterns

- Given two statements, decide which conclusions follow
- Statements followed by conclusions; choose the option that must be true
- Possibility-based questions (what can follow)
- Questions involving “all,” “some,” “no,” and “some not”

Venn diagrams can be used for verification, but speed comes from internalising valid moods and figures or applying distribution rules systematically.

## Logical Connectives and Compound Statements

Propositional logic deals with statements that can be true or false and with connectives that form compound statements.

### Basic Connectives

- Negation (~ or “not”): reverses truth value
- Conjunction (∧ or “and”): true only when both components are true
- Disjunction (∨ or “or”): true when at least one component is true (inclusive or in standard logic)
- Conditional (→ or “if … then”): false only when antecedent is true and consequent is false
- Biconditional (↔ or “if and only if”): true when both components have the same truth value

### Truth Tables

Truth tables systematically list all possible truth-value combinations and the resulting value of the compound statement. They are the foundation for determining tautologies, contradictions, and equivalences.

### Important Equivalences and Implications

- Implication: p → q is equivalent to ~p ∨ q
- Contrapositive: p → q is equivalent to ~q → ~p (always valid)
- Converse (q → p) and inverse (~p → ~q) are not equivalent to the original
- De Morgan’s laws: ~(p ∧ q) ≡ ~p ∨ ~q; ~(p ∨ q) ≡ ~p ∧ ~q
- Double negation: ~~p ≡ p

### Common CSAT Patterns with Connectives

- “If A then B” type statements and conclusions
- Statements involving “only if,” “unless,” “either … or”
- Determining what must be true or what cannot be true given certain conditionals
- Chains of implications
- Identifying necessary and sufficient conditions

Necessary condition: B is necessary for A if A → B (A cannot occur without B).
Sufficient condition: A is sufficient for B if A → B (A guarantees B).

“Unless” is typically translated as “if not … then” or as a disjunction.

## Strategies for Accuracy and Speed

1. Translate natural language carefully into logical form. Pay attention to quantifiers (“all,” “some,” “only,” “none”) and connectives.
2. For syllogisms, check distribution and the negative/particular rules before drawing conclusions.
3. For conditionals, always consider the contrapositive; avoid affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent (common fallacies).
4. When multiple conclusions are offered, test each against the premises rather than assuming everyday plausibility.
5. Possibility questions require checking whether a situation is consistent with the premises, not whether it must occur.
6. Practice recognising equivalent formulations to save time.

## Common Fallacies to Avoid

- Affirming the consequent: from p → q and q, concluding p
- Denying the antecedent: from p → q and ~p, concluding ~q
- Illicit major or minor (term distributed in conclusion but not in premise)
- Undistributed middle
- Exclusive or when inclusive is intended, or vice versa
- Confusing “some” with “all” or with “only some”

## Key Concepts Summary

- Categorical syllogisms relate classes; validity depends on distribution and quality/quantity rules.
- Standard forms A, E, I, O have definite distribution patterns.
- Propositional connectives form compound statements whose truth conditions are defined by truth tables.
- Conditional statements are central; the contrapositive is equivalent, converse and inverse are not.
- Necessary vs sufficient conditions must be distinguished clearly.
- Accurate translation from language to logic is the first and most important step.

Consistent practice with varied statement sets builds the pattern recognition needed for reliable performance in the CSAT analytical reasoning section.
