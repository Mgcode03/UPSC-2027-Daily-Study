# India and the Global South – Concepts and Engagement

## Understanding the Global South

The term “Global South” refers broadly to countries in Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, Asia (excluding Japan and sometimes others), and Oceania that share historical experiences of colonialism, economic underdevelopment relative to the industrialised North, and ongoing challenges of development, inequality, and representation in global institutions. It is a political and analytical category rather than a strictly geographical one.

The concept evolved from earlier terms such as “Third World” and “developing countries.” It emphasises shared interests in reforming global economic governance, addressing climate justice, ensuring technology access, and enhancing voice in international decision-making.

India has historically identified with the concerns of the Global South while simultaneously pursuing its own developmental trajectory and strategic autonomy.

## Historical Foundations of India’s Engagement

India’s engagement with what is now called the Global South has deep roots:

- Anti-colonial solidarity and support for independence movements
- Leadership in the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)
- Advocacy for a New International Economic Order in the 1970s
- Participation in the G77 and other developing-country coalitions
- South-South cooperation initiatives in technical assistance, training, and capacity building

These traditions continue to inform contemporary policy even as India’s economic weight and strategic interests have grown.

## Contemporary Pillars of Engagement

### Voice and Representation

India advocates reform of global institutions (United Nations Security Council, international financial institutions, WTO) to better reflect contemporary realities and the interests of developing countries. It emphasises that legitimacy and effectiveness of global governance require inclusive decision-making.

### Development Partnership

India’s development cooperation has expanded significantly. Features include:

- Lines of credit for infrastructure and other projects
- Grant assistance and capacity-building programmes
- Training and educational opportunities (e.g., ITEC – Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation)
- Humanitarian assistance and disaster relief
- Focus on demand-driven, non-conditional partnership distinct from traditional aid models

Cooperation spans Africa, neighbouring countries, and other regions of the Global South.

### Climate and Sustainability

India stresses the principles of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC) in climate negotiations. It highlights the developmental needs of the Global South, the historical responsibility of industrialised countries, and the importance of finance, technology transfer, and capacity building. Domestic efforts in renewable energy, adaptation, and lifestyle-based approaches (e.g., Mission LiFE) are presented as contributions that can be relevant for other developing countries.

### Food and Energy Security

India has positioned itself as a partner on issues of food security (including through humanitarian grain shipments and advocacy in international forums) and energy access. It supports the concerns of developing countries regarding energy transition pathways that do not compromise development.

### Digital and Technological Cooperation

Sharing of digital public infrastructure experiences, capacity building in digital skills, and cooperation on emerging technologies form growing areas of engagement. India emphasises affordable and inclusive technology solutions.

## Platforms and Mechanisms

India engages the Global South through multiple platforms:

- Bilateral partnerships
- Regional forums and organisations
- Multilateral coalitions such as G77 + China, NAM (where relevant), and issue-specific groupings
- BRICS and its outreach
- Voice of the Global South Summits (hosted by India)
- Engagement in the G20 with a focus on amplifying Global South priorities (especially during India’s presidency)

These platforms allow agenda-setting, consensus building, and practical cooperation.

## Continuities and Evolving Interests

While solidarity remains important, India’s approach also reflects its evolving capabilities and interests:

- Growing economic and technological strengths enable more substantial partnerships.
- Strategic competition and the need for diversified partnerships shape engagement.
- Domestic developmental priorities (growth, employment, technology) influence the content of cooperation.
- Balancing relations with major powers while maintaining credibility among developing countries requires careful diplomacy.

India often presents itself as a bridge between the North and the South and as a country that understands both the aspirations and the constraints of developing economies.

## Challenges and Critiques

- Managing expectations given resource constraints relative to needs
- Ensuring that cooperation delivers tangible and sustainable outcomes
- Navigating differences of interest within the heterogeneous Global South
- Addressing perceptions related to trade, investment, or specific bilateral issues
- Maintaining consistency between domestic policies and international advocacy on certain issues

Effective engagement requires continuous delivery, transparent processes, and sensitivity to partner priorities.

## Key Concepts for Retention

- Global South is a political-analytical category rooted in shared historical and developmental experiences.
- India’s engagement builds on anti-colonial and non-aligned traditions while adapting to contemporary capabilities.
- Main pillars include voice and representation, development partnership, climate justice, food and energy security, and digital cooperation.
- Multiple bilateral, regional, and multilateral platforms are used.
- Approach combines solidarity with pragmatic pursuit of mutual interests and strategic autonomy.
- Credibility depends on consistent delivery and respect for partner agency.

A conceptual understanding of India and the Global South is useful for analysing foreign policy questions, multilateral diplomacy, and India’s role in shaping global governance debates.
