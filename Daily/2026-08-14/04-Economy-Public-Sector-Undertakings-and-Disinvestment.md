# Public Sector Undertakings and Disinvestment in India

## Concept and Evolution of Public Sector Undertakings

Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) are enterprises owned and controlled by the government. They were created to achieve multiple objectives: rapid industrialisation, development of infrastructure and basic industries, reduction of regional disparities, generation of employment, and occupation of the “commanding heights” of the economy.

After Independence, the Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956 assigned a leading role to the public sector. The 1956 Resolution classified industries into three schedules, with Schedule A reserved exclusively for the public sector. Large investments were made in steel, heavy engineering, coal, power, petroleum, and transport.

Categories of PSUs:

- Departmental undertakings (e.g., Railways, Posts)
- Statutory corporations (created by special Acts of Parliament)
- Government companies registered under the Companies Act (majority shareholding by government)

Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) are those owned by the Central Government. State-level public enterprises also exist in large numbers.

## Objectives and Achievements

Intended objectives included:

- Building industrial base and self-reliance
- Promoting balanced regional development
- Preventing concentration of economic power
- Generating resources for further investment
- Providing employment and acting as model employers
- Supporting strategic and defence-related industries

Achievements:

- Creation of a diversified industrial structure
- Development of heavy industries and infrastructure
- Contribution to technological capability in certain areas
- Significant role in employment and social infrastructure
- Support to private sector through backward and forward linkages

## Problems and Criticisms

Over time, many PSUs faced challenges:

- Low profitability and returns on investment
- Overstaffing and rigid labour practices
- Bureaucratic control and lack of managerial autonomy
- Political interference in pricing, location, and appointments
- Inadequate technological upgradation
- Accumulation of losses in certain enterprises
- Soft budget constraints and repeated bailouts

These issues led to fiscal burden and questions about the efficiency of public ownership in competitive sectors.

## Policy Shift and Liberalisation

The economic reforms of 1991 marked a major change. The New Industrial Policy reduced the number of industries reserved for the public sector, opened most areas to private and foreign investment, and introduced the policy of disinvestment.

Objectives of disinvestment and privatisation policies have included:

- Raising resources for the government
- Improving efficiency through private ownership and market discipline
- Reducing the fiscal burden of loss-making enterprises
- Broadening ownership and developing capital markets
- Allowing government to focus on core functions and strategic areas

## Forms of Disinvestment and Privatisation

- Minority stake sale (government retains majority control)
- Majority stake sale / strategic sale (transfer of management control)
- Complete privatisation
- Offer for sale (OFS) and other market mechanisms
- Listing of PSUs on stock exchanges to improve transparency and governance

The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) is the nodal agency for disinvestment of Central Government equity.

## Classification of CPSEs

CPSEs are classified on the basis of performance and status:

- Maharatna: highest category with significant autonomy (e.g., large companies with high turnover and net worth)
- Navratna: substantial autonomy
- Miniratna Category I and II: operational and financial autonomy within limits

These classifications grant greater freedom in capital expenditure, joint ventures, and other decisions.

## Strategic and Non-Strategic Sectors

Recent policy has distinguished between strategic and non-strategic sectors. In strategic sectors (atomic energy, space, defence, certain transport and financial services, etc.), the government retains a presence, often with a minimum public shareholding. In non-strategic sectors, the policy has moved towards privatisation or closure of loss-making enterprises, subject to conditions.

## Issues and Debates

Key debates surrounding disinvestment include:

- Valuation of assets and prevention of under-selling
- Impact on employment and labour rights
- Strategic concerns in sensitive sectors
- Use of proceeds (whether for fiscal deficit reduction, capital expenditure, or social sector)
- Corporate governance post-disinvestment
- Role of public sector in ensuring availability of essential goods and services
- Balancing commercial viability with social obligations

Empirical evidence on performance after privatisation is mixed and depends on sector, regulatory environment, and the specific enterprise.

## Current Framework and Considerations

The policy framework continues to evolve. Emphasis is placed on:

- Transparent processes
- Maximising value through competitive bidding or market mechanisms
- Protection of employee interests through appropriate mechanisms
- Retention of government presence where strategic considerations or market failures exist
- Strengthening remaining PSUs through professional management, board-level autonomy, and performance contracts

Public sector enterprises remain important in several domains. The policy challenge is to ensure that ownership structures—public, private, or mixed—deliver efficiency, accountability, and alignment with broader developmental goals.

## Key Points to Retain

- PSUs were central to the post-Independence development strategy.
- Achievements in industrial base and infrastructure coexisted with efficiency problems.
- 1991 reforms initiated a shift towards disinvestment and reduced reservation.
- Forms range from minority sale to strategic sale and full privatisation.
- Classification into Maharatna, Navratna, Miniratna confers graded autonomy.
- Distinction between strategic and non-strategic sectors guides current policy.
- Disinvestment involves trade-offs among fiscal, efficiency, employment, and strategic objectives.

A clear understanding of the evolution, rationale, mechanisms, and debates surrounding PSUs and disinvestment is essential for analysing India’s economic policy landscape.
