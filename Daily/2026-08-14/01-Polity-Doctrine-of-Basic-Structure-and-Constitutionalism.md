# Doctrine of Basic Structure and Constitutionalism

## Introduction to Constitutionalism

Constitutionalism is the principle that government authority is derived from and limited by a body of fundamental law. It stands in contrast to arbitrary rule or absolute power. In the Indian context, constitutionalism means that every organ of the state—Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary—must act within the bounds of the Constitution. The Constitution is not merely a legal document but the supreme law that structures power, protects rights, and provides mechanisms for accountability.

Key features of Indian constitutionalism include:

- Written and detailed Constitution
- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Separation of powers with checks and balances
- Independent judiciary
- Fundamental Rights as justiciable guarantees
- Rule of law
- Democratic form of government with periodic elections
- Federal structure with unitary features

Constitutionalism requires that power be exercised according to established procedures and that those who wield power remain accountable. It also implies that the Constitution itself can be amended, but only within certain limits so that its core identity is preserved.

## Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine emerged from a series of judicial decisions that examined the scope of Parliament’s amending power under Article 368.

### Early Position

Initially, the Supreme Court in Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) held that Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution included the power to amend Fundamental Rights. The Court treated constitutional amendments as ordinary legislative acts for the purpose of Article 13, which prohibits laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

### Golaknath Case (1967)

In Golaknath v. State of Punjab, a larger bench reversed the earlier view. The Court held that Fundamental Rights occupy a transcendental position and that Parliament cannot amend them. Amendments were treated as “law” under Article 13(2). This decision created a rigid barrier against any alteration of Fundamental Rights and raised concerns about the adaptability of the Constitution.

### Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)

The landmark judgment in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala settled the conflict. A 13-judge bench held by a narrow majority that while Parliament has wide power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter or destroy the “basic structure” or “basic features” of the Constitution. The Court did not provide an exhaustive list of basic features but indicated several elements through various opinions.

The doctrine struck a balance: it preserved the amendability of the Constitution while protecting its foundational identity from majoritarian excess.

### Subsequent Affirmations

- Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): The Court applied the doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment that sought to place the election of the Prime Minister beyond judicial scrutiny.
- Minerva Mills (1980): The Court invalidated clauses that gave unlimited amending power and subordinated Fundamental Rights to Directive Principles in an absolute manner. It reaffirmed that harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure.
- Waman Rao (1981): The Court clarified that the basic structure doctrine applies to amendments made after 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda judgment).
- Later cases continued to refine and apply the doctrine in contexts such as judicial independence, secularism, federalism, and the rule of law.

## Identified Elements of Basic Structure

Although no single judgment provides a closed list, the following features have been repeatedly recognised as part of the basic structure:

1. Supremacy of the Constitution
2. Republican and democratic form of government
3. Secular character of the Constitution
4. Separation of powers among the legislature, executive, and judiciary
5. Federal character of the Constitution
6. Unity and integrity of the nation
7. Sovereignty of India
8. Individual freedom and dignity (including Fundamental Rights in their essential aspects)
9. Parliamentary system of government
10. Rule of law
11. Independence of the judiciary
12. Judicial review
13. Free and fair elections
14. Limited amending power of Parliament itself
15. Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
16. Welfare state / social justice orientation in certain formulations

The list is illustrative. Courts examine each amendment in its specific context to determine whether it damages or destroys any essential feature.

## Significance of the Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine serves several constitutional purposes:

- It prevents the conversion of a limited government into an unlimited one through the formal process of amendment.
- It protects minority rights and institutional checks against temporary majorities.
- It preserves the identity of the Constitution as a framework for ordered liberty and democratic governance.
- It allows necessary reforms while maintaining continuity of core principles.
- It reinforces the role of the judiciary as the guardian of the Constitution without making the Constitution completely rigid.

Critics argue that the doctrine gives unelected judges excessive power to invalidate democratic decisions and that the content of “basic structure” remains somewhat open-ended. Supporters reply that without such a doctrine, the Constitution could be reduced to a malleable instrument of the ruling majority.

## Constitutionalism in Practice

Constitutionalism is not only a theoretical concept but a living practice visible in:

- Judicial enforcement of Fundamental Rights through writs under Articles 32 and 226.
- Scrutiny of legislation and executive action for constitutionality.
- Protection of institutional independence (judiciary, Election Commission, CAG, etc.).
- Requirements of fair procedure, natural justice, and non-arbitrariness under Article 14.
- Horizontal application of certain rights and the expansion of Article 21 to include dignity, privacy, livelihood, and environmental protection.

The doctrine of basic structure is the most powerful judicial tool for maintaining constitutionalism against potential legislative overreach. It ensures that while the Constitution can evolve, its foundational commitments remain intact.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- Constitutionalism = limited government under higher law.
- Article 368 confers wide but not unlimited amending power.
- Basic structure is the core identity that cannot be destroyed.
- The doctrine is a judicial innovation that has become an established part of Indian constitutional law.
- Elements are identified case by case; the list is not exhaustive.
- Harmony between rights and Directive Principles, judicial independence, and democratic form are recurring themes.

Understanding the Basic Structure Doctrine is essential for grasping how the Indian Constitution balances flexibility with permanence and democracy with constitutional limits.
