# International Relations: India’s Neighbourhood and SAARC – Foundational Concepts

## Significance of the Neighbourhood

India’s foreign policy has always attached special importance to its immediate neighbourhood. The countries that share land or maritime boundaries with India—Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and the island nations of the Indian Ocean region—form a critical circle of engagement. Stability, connectivity, and cooperation in the neighbourhood directly affect India’s security, economic development, and strategic autonomy.

The neighbourhood is characterised by shared history, cultural linkages, economic complementarities, and, in several cases, unresolved disputes and asymmetric power relations. Managing this complex set of relationships is a continuous challenge and opportunity for Indian diplomacy.

## Core Principles Guiding Neighbourhood Policy

Several principles have shaped India’s approach:

- **Sovereign equality**: Respect for the sovereignty and territorial integrity of all neighbours.
- **Non-interference**: Avoidance of interference in the internal affairs of neighbouring countries, while remaining engaged on issues of mutual concern.
- **Mutual benefit**: Emphasis on economic cooperation, connectivity, and people-to-people contacts that benefit both sides.
- **Strategic autonomy**: Maintaining freedom of action and avoiding entanglement in the rivalries of extra-regional powers to the extent possible.
- **Regional cooperation**: Support for multilateral and regional mechanisms that promote stability and development.

In recent years, the approach has been articulated through frameworks such as “Neighbourhood First,” which prioritises relations with immediate neighbours, and the broader vision of a free, open, and inclusive Indo-Pacific.

## SAARC – Origin and Objectives

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) was established in 1985 with the signing of the SAARC Charter in Dhaka. The founding members were Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. Afghanistan joined as the eighth member in 2007.

The primary objectives of SAARC, as stated in its Charter, include:
- Promoting the welfare of the peoples of South Asia and improving their quality of life
- Accelerating economic growth, social progress, and cultural development
- Promoting and strengthening collective self-reliance
- Contributing to mutual trust, understanding, and appreciation of one another’s problems
- Promoting active collaboration and mutual assistance in the economic, social, cultural, technical, and scientific fields
- Strengthening cooperation among the member states in international forums on matters of common interest

SAARC was conceived as a platform for regional cooperation that would work on the basis of unanimity and would not discuss bilateral and contentious issues. This design reflected the political realities of the region, particularly the difficult relationship between India and Pakistan.

## Institutional Structure of SAARC

- **Summit**: The highest decision-making body, consisting of the heads of state or government. Summits are supposed to be held regularly, though there have been long gaps.
- **Council of Ministers**: Meets regularly to review progress and prepare for summits.
- **Standing Committee**: Consists of Foreign Secretaries and monitors implementation.
- **Secretariat**: Located in Kathmandu, Nepal. It coordinates and monitors the implementation of SAARC activities.
- **Regional Centres**: Specialised centres dealing with agriculture, meteorology, tuberculosis, documentation, and other areas.

## Areas of Cooperation

SAARC has identified several areas of cooperation, including agriculture and rural development, education, culture, health, population, environment, meteorology, science and technology, transport, telecommunications, and people-to-people contacts. The SAARC Preferential Trading Arrangement (SAPTA) and the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA) were steps towards economic integration. A SAARC Regional Convention on Suppression of Terrorism and other agreements on social issues have also been adopted.

## Challenges and Limitations of SAARC

Despite its long existence, SAARC has not achieved the level of regional integration seen in some other parts of the world. The main challenges include:

- **Political differences and bilateral disputes**: The India–Pakistan relationship has repeatedly constrained the functioning of SAARC. Summits have been postponed or cancelled due to political tensions.
- **Unanimity principle**: The requirement of unanimity for decisions means that any one member can block progress.
- **Asymmetry**: India’s size, population, and economic weight create both opportunities and sensitivities. Smaller countries sometimes fear domination; India sometimes feels that its interests are not adequately accommodated.
- **Limited economic integration**: Intra-SAARC trade remains low as a percentage of the total trade of the member countries. Non-tariff barriers, connectivity gaps, and political mistrust impede deeper economic cooperation.
- **Implementation gaps**: Many agreements and declarations have not been fully implemented.
- **Extra-regional influences**: The involvement of external powers in the region adds another layer of complexity.

## Alternative and Complementary Frameworks

Given the difficulties in SAARC, India has also pursued cooperation through other mechanisms:

- **BIMSTEC** (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation): Includes countries from South Asia and Southeast Asia and focuses on practical cooperation in areas such as security, connectivity, and economic development.
- **BBIN** (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal): A sub-regional initiative focused on connectivity and economic cooperation among the four countries.
- **Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA)** and other maritime frameworks.
- Bilateral and trilateral initiatives with individual neighbours.

These frameworks allow progress on specific issues even when the larger SAARC process is stalled.

## India’s Relations with Key Neighbours – Conceptual Overview

- **Pakistan**: Complex relationship marked by historical disputes, cross-border terrorism concerns, and limited people-to-people and economic engagement. Dialogue has been intermittent.
- **China**: Relationship combines elements of cooperation (trade, multilateral forums) and competition (border issues, strategic influence in the neighbourhood). Boundary questions remain unresolved.
- **Bangladesh**: Generally positive trajectory with cooperation on connectivity, energy, trade, and security. Settlement of the land boundary and maritime boundary issues were important milestones.
- **Nepal and Bhutan**: Relations shaped by geography, treaties, and extensive people-to-people ties. Hydropower, trade, and transit are major areas of engagement. Sensitivities regarding autonomy and external influence exist.
- **Sri Lanka and Maldives**: Maritime neighbours with cultural and economic links. Issues of ethnic reconciliation, economic assistance, and strategic presence of extra-regional powers figure in the relationship.
- **Myanmar**: Land and maritime neighbour; cooperation on security, connectivity (including the India–Myanmar–Thailand Trilateral Highway), and managing the border areas.

## Connectivity and Development Partnership

A major thrust of India’s neighbourhood policy has been the improvement of physical, digital, and people-to-people connectivity. Projects related to roads, railways, inland waterways, ports, power transmission, and digital links are intended to reduce the costs of trade and interaction and to create mutual stakes in stability. Development assistance, lines of credit, and capacity-building programmes form part of this engagement.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Neighbourhood First is a priority framework in Indian foreign policy.
- SAARC was established in 1985; Afghanistan joined in 2007; Secretariat is in Kathmandu.
- SAARC works on the principle of unanimity and does not discuss bilateral contentious issues.
- Intra-SAARC trade remains low; political differences have limited the organisation’s effectiveness.
- BIMSTEC, BBIN, and bilateral initiatives serve as complementary or alternative platforms.
- Connectivity, economic cooperation, and mutual respect for sovereignty are central themes in neighbourhood engagement.
- Relations with each neighbour have unique historical, geographic, and strategic characteristics.
- Stability and cooperation in the neighbourhood are directly linked to India’s security and development goals.

A clear grasp of the geography, history, institutional frameworks, and political dynamics of South Asia is essential for understanding India’s foreign policy choices and the opportunities and constraints it faces in its immediate periphery.
