# CSAT: Critical Reasoning – Statements and Assumptions

## Introduction to Critical Reasoning in CSAT

The CSAT paper tests, among other things, the ability to understand arguments, identify hidden assumptions, draw valid conclusions, and evaluate the strength of reasoning. Questions based on statements and assumptions are a standard component of logical reasoning. These questions assess whether a candidate can distinguish between what is explicitly stated and what is taken for granted for the statement to make sense.

## What is an Assumption?

An assumption is an unstated premise that must be true for the given statement or argument to hold. It is something that the author of the statement takes for granted. Identifying assumptions requires careful reading and the ability to see what is necessary for the logic to work.

Assumptions are different from:
- **Conclusions**: What the argument is trying to prove.
- **Inferences**: What can be logically drawn from the given information.
- **Facts**: Information that is explicitly given or known to be true.

## Structure of Statement–Assumption Questions

A typical question presents a statement followed by two or more assumptions. The candidate is asked to decide which of the assumptions is implicit in the statement. The standard options usually are:
- Only assumption 1 is implicit
- Only assumption 2 is implicit
- Either 1 or 2 is implicit
- Neither 1 nor 2 is implicit
- Both 1 and 2 are implicit

## How to Approach These Questions

### Step 1: Read the Statement Carefully
Understand exactly what is being said. Do not add your own knowledge or opinions. Stick to the given words.

### Step 2: Identify the Core Idea
What is the main claim or suggestion in the statement? What action is being recommended or what judgment is being made?

### Step 3: Ask “What Must Be True?”
For the statement to make sense or for the recommended action to be reasonable, what unstated belief must the speaker hold? That belief is the assumption.

### Step 4: Test Each Assumption
- If the assumption is true, does the statement still make sense? (It should.)
- If the assumption is false, does the statement fall apart or become unreasonable? (If yes, then the assumption is implicit.)

### Step 5: Avoid Common Traps
- Do not treat a possible consequence as an assumption.
- Do not bring in outside information.
- Do not assume that something is implicit merely because it is desirable or commonly believed.
- Extreme or absolute assumptions are less likely to be implicit unless the statement itself is absolute.

## Types of Assumptions

### 1. Assumptions about Cause and Effect
When a statement recommends an action to solve a problem, it assumes that the action will have the desired effect and that the problem exists.

Example (conceptual):
Statement: “The government should ban the sale of junk food in school canteens to improve students’ health.”
Possible implicit assumptions:
- Junk food is available in school canteens.
- Consumption of junk food is harmful to students’ health.
- Banning sale in canteens will reduce consumption by students.

### 2. Assumptions about Existence or Prevalence
A statement that talks about solving a problem assumes that the problem exists or is significant.

### 3. Assumptions about Desirability or Value
When a statement recommends a policy, it often assumes that the goal of the policy is desirable.

### 4. Assumptions about Feasibility or Capability
Recommendations often assume that the suggested course of action is possible or that the relevant actors are capable of implementing it.

### 5. Assumptions about Comparison or Preference
Statements that prefer one option over another assume that the preferred option is better on the relevant criteria.

## Practice Approach with Sample Logic

Consider a statement: “To reduce traffic congestion in the city, the municipal corporation should increase the frequency of public buses.”

Assumption A: “Traffic congestion is caused at least partly by insufficient public bus services.”
Assumption B: “People will shift from private vehicles to buses if bus frequency is increased.”

Both assumptions are necessary for the recommendation to be logical. If congestion has nothing to do with bus frequency, or if people will not switch to buses regardless of frequency, the recommendation loses its force. Hence both are implicit.

Another statement: “The company should introduce flexible working hours to improve employee productivity.”

Assumption A: “Employees currently have fixed working hours.”
Assumption B: “Flexible working hours lead to higher productivity.”

Both are reasonable implicit assumptions. The statement presupposes the current system and the effectiveness of the proposed change.

## Negative Assumptions and “Not Implicit”

Sometimes an assumption is not implicit because:
- It is too extreme (uses words like “only”, “never”, “all”).
- It introduces a new idea that is not necessary for the statement.
- It contradicts the statement.
- It is merely a restatement of the given information rather than an unstated premise.

## Relationship with Other Reasoning Topics

Statement–assumption questions are related to:
- **Statement–conclusion** questions (what follows from the statement)
- **Statement–argument** questions (which arguments strengthen or weaken)
- **Course of action** questions (what should be done)
- **Cause–effect** questions

Mastering the identification of assumptions improves performance across these related question types.

## Tips for Accuracy and Speed

- Read the statement twice if needed; precision matters.
- Treat the statement as true for the purpose of the question.
- Focus on necessity, not on possibility or desirability alone.
- Eliminate options systematically.
- Do not spend excessive time on a single question; flag and return if needed.
- Practice with a variety of statements—policy recommendations, explanations, predictions, and value judgments.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- An assumption is an unstated premise necessary for the statement to hold.
- The key test: if the assumption is false, the statement becomes weak or meaningless.
- Do not import outside knowledge; stay within the given statement.
- Extreme assumptions are usually not implicit.
- Recommendations typically assume that the problem exists and that the solution will work.
- Both “existence” and “effectiveness” assumptions are common in policy-related statements.
- Practice is essential because the skill is pattern-based and improves with exposure to varied examples.

Critical reasoning questions on statements and assumptions test the ability to think clearly and to separate what is said from what is presupposed. Developing this skill is valuable not only for CSAT but also for analysing arguments in essays, ethics case studies, and current affairs discussions.
