# Environment: Pollution Control Mechanisms in India

## Nature and Types of Pollution

Pollution refers to the introduction of harmful substances or energy into the environment, resulting in adverse effects on living organisms and ecosystems. The major types relevant to India are air pollution, water pollution, soil pollution, noise pollution, and plastic pollution. Each type has distinct sources, impacts, and control strategies.

## Air Pollution – Sources and Impacts

Major sources of air pollution in India include:
- Vehicular emissions (carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, hydrocarbons)
- Industrial emissions (sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, heavy metals)
- Thermal power plants (especially coal-based)
- Biomass burning for cooking and heating in rural areas
- Agricultural residue burning, particularly in northern India during October–November
- Construction dust and road dust
- Waste burning

The impacts include respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, reduced visibility, damage to crops and forests, acid rain, and contribution to climate change through black carbon and other short-lived climate pollutants. Cities such as Delhi, several cities in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, and some industrial towns frequently record high levels of PM2.5 and PM10.

## Legal and Institutional Framework for Air Pollution Control

### Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981
This is the principal legislation for the control of air pollution. It provides for the prevention, control, and abatement of air pollution. Key features include:
- Declaration of air pollution control areas
- Restriction on the establishment of industrial plants in these areas without consent
- Power to prescribe emission standards
- Establishment of Central and State Pollution Control Boards with powers of inspection, sampling, and direction

### Environment (Protection) Act, 1986
An umbrella legislation that empowers the Central Government to take all necessary measures for the protection and improvement of the environment. It has been used to issue various rules and notifications related to air quality, including the National Ambient Air Quality Standards.

### National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS)
Notified by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), these standards specify the maximum permissible concentrations of various pollutants (SO2, NO2, PM10, PM2.5, Ozone, Lead, Carbon Monoxide, Ammonia, etc.) for industrial, residential, and sensitive areas.

### National Clean Air Programme (NCAP)
Launched in 2019, NCAP is a comprehensive, time-bound strategy to reduce particulate pollution. It aims at a significant reduction in PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations in targeted cities. The programme involves city-specific action plans, strengthening of monitoring networks, and coordination among various agencies.

### Other Measures
- Bharat Stage emission standards for vehicles (currently BS-VI nationwide)
- Promotion of electric vehicles and cleaner fuels (CNG, ethanol blending)
- Ban or restriction on stubble burning, with promotion of alternative uses of crop residue
- Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) for Delhi-NCR, which imposes restrictions based on air quality levels
- Expansion of continuous ambient air quality monitoring stations

## Water Pollution – Sources and Impacts

Sources of water pollution include:
- Untreated or partially treated municipal sewage
- Industrial effluents containing heavy metals, chemicals, and organic pollutants
- Agricultural runoff containing fertilisers and pesticides
- Religious and social practices leading to immersion of idols and disposal of waste in rivers
- Oil spills and leakage from ships and pipelines
- Open defecation and poor sanitation in some areas

Polluted water affects human health (water-borne diseases), aquatic ecosystems, agricultural productivity (when used for irrigation), and the availability of safe drinking water.

## Legal and Institutional Framework for Water Pollution Control

### Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974
The first major legislation specifically targeting water pollution. It established the Central and State Pollution Control Boards and gave them powers to set standards, issue consents, and take action against polluting units. The Act prohibits the discharge of pollutants into water bodies beyond prescribed standards.

### Water Cess Act, 1977
Provided for the levy of a cess on water consumed by certain industries and local authorities. The proceeds were meant to augment the resources of the Pollution Control Boards. (Note: The cess has undergone changes with the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax framework.)

### Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and Related Rules
Used to notify standards for effluent discharge and to regulate specific industries. The Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules and the Solid Waste Management Rules also have implications for water quality.

### National Water Quality Monitoring Programme
The CPCB and State Pollution Control Boards monitor water quality at numerous locations across rivers, lakes, and groundwater. The data is used to identify polluted stretches and to prioritise action.

### Namami Gange and Other River Rejuvenation Programmes
Namami Gange is an integrated mission for the cleaning and rejuvenation of the Ganga. It includes sewage treatment infrastructure, industrial effluent control, riverfront development, biodiversity conservation, and public awareness. Similar efforts have been initiated for other major rivers.

### Other Measures
- Common Effluent Treatment Plants (CETPs) for clusters of small and medium industries
- Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) requirements for certain highly polluting industries
- Promotion of sewage treatment plants (STPs) and faecal sludge management
- Regulation of groundwater extraction in over-exploited areas

## Central and State Pollution Control Boards

The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) was constituted under the Water Act, 1974, and later given additional responsibilities under the Air Act, 1981, and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. Its main functions include:
- Advising the Central Government on pollution control
- Coordinating the activities of State Boards
- Providing technical assistance and guidance
- Setting standards for air and water quality and for emissions and effluents
- Collecting, compiling, and publishing technical and statistical data
- Organising training and awareness programmes

State Pollution Control Boards perform similar functions at the state level and are responsible for the enforcement of consent mechanisms and monitoring within their jurisdictions.

## Noise Pollution

Noise pollution is regulated under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, through the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000. These rules prescribe ambient noise standards for different areas (industrial, commercial, residential, silence zones) and restrict the use of loudspeakers and public address systems during certain hours. Noise from vehicles, construction, and industrial activity is also addressed through various standards and guidelines.

## Plastic and Solid Waste Pollution

The Plastic Waste Management Rules and the Solid Waste Management Rules provide the framework for the management of plastic and municipal solid waste. Key elements include extended producer responsibility (EPR), segregation at source, ban on certain single-use plastic items, and the promotion of recycling and waste-to-energy technologies. Improper disposal of plastic contributes to both land and water pollution and poses risks to wildlife.

## Challenges in Pollution Control

- Rapid urbanisation and industrialisation outpacing the growth of treatment infrastructure
- Inadequate sewage treatment capacity in many cities
- Enforcement gaps and capacity constraints in Pollution Control Boards
- Fragmented institutional responsibilities across multiple agencies
- Difficulty in controlling non-point sources of pollution (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater)
- Seasonal factors such as stubble burning and winter inversion conditions that aggravate air pollution
- Need for greater public awareness and behavioural change
- Balancing economic growth and environmental protection

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, and Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, are the principal legislations.
- Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, is the umbrella law.
- CPCB and State Pollution Control Boards are the main regulatory agencies.
- NAAQS set the ambient air quality standards.
- NCAP is the national programme for reducing particulate pollution in cities.
- Namami Gange is the flagship river rejuvenation programme for the Ganga.
- BS-VI emission standards apply to vehicles across the country.
- GRAP is the graded response mechanism for Delhi-NCR air quality.
- Common Effluent Treatment Plants and Zero Liquid Discharge are important industrial pollution control measures.
- Noise is regulated under the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000.

Effective pollution control requires a combination of strong legislation, adequate institutional capacity, technological solutions, economic instruments, and active public participation. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for analysing environmental governance in India.
