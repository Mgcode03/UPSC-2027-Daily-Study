# Society: Gender Issues and Women Empowerment in India

## Understanding Gender and Sex

Sex refers to the biological differences between males and females. Gender refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviours, expectations, and identities that a society considers appropriate for men and women. Gender is not fixed; it varies across cultures and changes over time. Gender inequality arises when these socially constructed roles lead to unequal access to resources, opportunities, power, and dignity.

## Manifestations of Gender Inequality in India

### 1. Demographic Indicators
- Sex ratio: The overall sex ratio and especially the child sex ratio reflect deep-rooted son preference and gender-biased sex selection in some regions.
- Female literacy and education levels lag behind male literacy in several states, though the gap has been narrowing.
- Maternal mortality ratio, while declining, remains a concern in certain areas and indicates issues related to healthcare access and nutrition.

### 2. Economic Indicators
- Female labour force participation rate in India is relatively low compared to many other countries and has shown periods of decline or stagnation.
- Women are over-represented in the informal sector and in unpaid care work.
- Gender wage gap persists across many occupations.
- Women’s ownership of land and productive assets remains limited despite legal provisions for equal inheritance.

### 3. Political Indicators
- Representation of women in the Lok Sabha and state legislatures has increased but remains below the proportion of women in the population.
- The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments reserved one-third of seats for women in Panchayati Raj Institutions and urban local bodies, leading to the entry of large numbers of women into local governance. The proposed higher reservation in Parliament and state legislatures has been a subject of long debate.

### 4. Social and Cultural Indicators
- Preference for male children leading to sex-selective practices
- Early marriage in some communities
- Restrictions on women’s mobility and decision-making within the household in certain social settings
- Gender-based violence, including domestic violence, sexual harassment, and trafficking
- Stereotyping of gender roles in education, media, and the family

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

The Constitution of India guarantees equality before the law (Article 14), prohibits discrimination on grounds of sex (Article 15), and allows the state to make special provisions for women and children (Article 15(3)). The Directive Principles call for equal pay for equal work and for maternity relief. Fundamental Duties include the duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.

Important laws include:
- Hindu Succession Act (as amended) providing equal inheritance rights
- Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005
- Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013
- Dowry Prohibition Act
- Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act
- Various provisions in the Indian Penal Code dealing with crimes against women
- Maternity Benefit Act and related labour laws

## Concept of Women Empowerment

Women empowerment refers to the process by which women gain control over their lives, acquire the ability to make strategic life choices, and access resources and opportunities on an equal basis with men. It has several dimensions:

- **Economic empowerment**: Access to income, assets, credit, employment, and control over economic resources.
- **Social empowerment**: Access to education, health, nutrition, and freedom from violence and discrimination.
- **Political empowerment**: Participation in decision-making processes at all levels, from the household to national politics.
- **Psychological empowerment**: Sense of self-worth, confidence, and the ability to challenge existing power relations.

Empowerment is both a process and an outcome. It requires changes in formal institutions (laws, policies) as well as informal institutions (norms, customs, family practices).

## Government Initiatives and Schemes (Conceptual Overview)

Over the decades, successive governments have launched programmes aimed at improving the status of women. These include measures for education (scholarships, residential schools, campaigns for girls’ education), health and nutrition (maternal health programmes, adolescent health), economic support (self-help groups, skill development, credit facilities, support for women entrepreneurs), political participation (reservations in local bodies), and protection from violence (one-stop centres, helplines, special laws).

The approach has evolved from a welfare-oriented approach to a development and empowerment-oriented approach, recognising women as agents of change rather than only as beneficiaries.

## Role of Self-Help Groups and Microfinance

Self-Help Groups (SHGs) have emerged as an important vehicle for women’s economic and social empowerment, especially in rural areas. By pooling savings, accessing credit, and engaging in collective activities, women in SHGs gain financial independence, social solidarity, and experience in collective decision-making. Many SHGs have also become platforms for awareness on health, education, and rights.

## Challenges Remaining

- Deep-rooted patriarchal norms that limit women’s agency
- Uneven implementation of laws and schemes across states and social groups
- Intersectionality: women from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, minority communities, and economically weaker sections face compounded disadvantages
- Balancing unpaid care work with opportunities for paid employment
- Safety and mobility concerns that restrict access to education and work
- Under-representation in higher education in certain STEM fields and in leadership positions in the private sector and academia
- Need for greater male engagement in the process of gender equality

## Gender Mainstreaming

Gender mainstreaming is the process of assessing the implications for women and men of any planned action—legislation, policy, or programme—in all areas and at all levels. It is a strategy for making women’s as well as men’s concerns and experiences an integral dimension of the design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation of policies so that women and men benefit equally and inequality is not perpetuated.

## International Commitments

India is a signatory to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) and has committed to the Sustainable Development Goals, including Goal 5 on gender equality. These international frameworks reinforce the domestic constitutional and legal commitments.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Sex is biological; gender is socially constructed.
- Gender inequality appears in demographic, economic, political, and social indicators.
- Constitution guarantees equality and permits special provisions for women (Article 15(3)).
- 73rd and 74th Amendments provided one-third reservation for women in local bodies.
- Women empowerment has economic, social, political, and psychological dimensions.
- Self-Help Groups have been an important instrument of empowerment at the grassroots.
- Patriarchy, uneven implementation, and intersectional disadvantages remain major challenges.
- Gender mainstreaming aims to integrate gender perspectives into all policies and programmes.
- CEDAW and SDG 5 provide the international normative framework.

Gender equality and women empowerment are not only matters of social justice; they are also essential for economic development, demographic balance, and the overall quality of democracy. Progress requires simultaneous action on legal, policy, economic, and socio-cultural fronts.
