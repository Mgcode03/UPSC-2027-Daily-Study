# Economy: Capital Markets and Stock Exchanges in India

## Concept of Capital Market

The capital market is the market for long-term funds. It channels savings from households, institutions, and foreign investors into productive investments by companies and governments. Unlike the money market, which deals with short-term instruments (usually less than one year), the capital market deals with instruments that have a maturity of more than one year or are perpetual (such as equity shares).

A well-functioning capital market performs several important functions:
- Mobilisation of savings
- Allocation of capital to the most productive uses
- Provision of liquidity to investors
- Risk diversification
- Price discovery of financial assets
- Corporate governance through the discipline of the market

## Structure of the Indian Capital Market

The Indian capital market consists of two main segments:

### 1. Primary Market
The primary market is the market for new issues of securities. When a company issues fresh shares or debentures to raise capital, the transaction takes place in the primary market. The main methods of raising capital in the primary market are:

- **Public Issue**: Offer of securities to the general public. It may be an Initial Public Offering (IPO) when a company comes to the market for the first time, or a Follow-on Public Offer (FPO) when an already listed company issues additional shares.
- **Rights Issue**: Offer of shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holding.
- **Private Placement**: Sale of securities to a select group of investors (Qualified Institutional Buyers, high-net-worth individuals, etc.). When the placement is made by a listed company, it is called a Preferential Allotment or Qualified Institutions Placement (QIP).
- **Offer for Sale**: Existing shareholders (often promoters or the government) sell their shares to the public.

### 2. Secondary Market
The secondary market is the market where existing securities are traded among investors. It provides liquidity and enables price discovery. The secondary market in India is dominated by the two major stock exchanges—the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) and the National Stock Exchange (NSE).

## Major Stock Exchanges

### Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE)
The BSE is Asia’s oldest stock exchange, established in 1875. It was originally called the Native Share and Stock Brokers’ Association. The Sensex (Sensitive Index) is the flagship index of the BSE. It is a free-float market-capitalisation-weighted index of 30 large and financially sound companies listed on the BSE. The Sensex is widely used as a barometer of the Indian stock market.

### National Stock Exchange (NSE)
The NSE was established in 1992 and began operations in 1994. It introduced electronic screen-based trading, which revolutionised the Indian capital market. The Nifty 50 is the flagship index of the NSE. It comprises 50 large companies and is also a free-float market-capitalisation-weighted index. The NSE is the larger of the two exchanges in terms of trading volume and number of listed companies in the equity derivatives segment.

Both exchanges are regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI).

## Key Market Indices

- **Sensex**: 30 stocks, BSE
- **Nifty 50**: 50 stocks, NSE
- **Nifty Next 50**: Next 50 companies after Nifty 50
- **Bank Nifty**: Banking sector index
- **Sectoral indices**: Indices tracking specific sectors such as IT, Pharma, FMCG, Auto, Metal, etc.

These indices help investors track market performance and serve as benchmarks for mutual funds and portfolio managers.

## Market Participants

- **Retail Investors**: Individual investors who invest their own money.
- **Institutional Investors**: Mutual funds, insurance companies, banks, pension funds, and foreign portfolio investors (FPIs).
- **Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs)**: Foreign entities that invest in Indian securities. Their flows have a significant impact on market movements.
- **Domestic Institutional Investors (DIIs)**: Mutual funds, insurance companies, and other domestic institutions. In recent years, DIIs have played a stabilising role when FPI flows are volatile.
- **Brokers and Trading Members**: Intermediaries who execute buy and sell orders on behalf of clients.
- **Depositories**: NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited) and CDSL (Central Depository Services Limited) hold securities in dematerialised (electronic) form.
- **Clearing Corporations**: Ensure settlement of trades and manage counterparty risk.

## Regulatory Framework

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) is the primary regulator of the capital market. Established as a non-statutory body in 1988 and given statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992, SEBI’s main objectives are:
- Protection of the interests of investors
- Promotion of the development of the securities market
- Regulation of the securities market

SEBI regulates stock exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, portfolio managers, credit rating agencies, and other intermediaries. It has the power to conduct investigations, issue guidelines, impose penalties, and frame regulations.

Other important laws and institutions include:
- Companies Act, 2013 (governs issue of securities and corporate governance)
- Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956
- Depositories Act, 1996
- Reserve Bank of India (for certain debt instruments and foreign exchange aspects)

## Important Concepts and Instruments

### Equity Shares
Represent ownership in a company. Equity shareholders have voting rights and residual claim on profits and assets. They bear the highest risk but also have the potential for highest returns through capital appreciation and dividends.

### Debentures and Bonds
Debt instruments. Debenture holders are creditors of the company and receive fixed interest. Bonds issued by the government or public sector entities are considered relatively safer.

### Mutual Funds
Pool money from many investors and invest in a diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other instruments. They provide professional management and diversification to small investors. Mutual funds in India are regulated by SEBI.

### Derivatives
Instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset (stocks, indices, commodities, currencies). Futures and options are the main types traded on Indian exchanges. Derivatives are used for hedging risk as well as for speculation.

### Initial Public Offering (IPO)
The process by which a private company offers its shares to the public for the first time and becomes a listed company. The IPO process involves preparation of a prospectus, grading (optional), book building or fixed price method, and listing on the exchange.

## Reforms and Development of the Capital Market

Since the early 1990s, the Indian capital market has undergone major reforms:
- Establishment of SEBI as a statutory regulator
- Introduction of electronic trading by the NSE
- Dematerialisation of shares (elimination of physical certificates)
- Introduction of rolling settlement (T+1 settlement cycle in recent years)
- Development of the derivatives market
- Strengthening of corporate governance norms (Clause 49, now LODR regulations)
- Opening of the market to foreign portfolio investors
- Growth of mutual funds and systematic investment plans (SIPs)
- Introduction of new products such as Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs)

These reforms have increased transparency, reduced transaction costs, improved liquidity, and broadened the investor base.

## Role in Economic Development

A deep and liquid capital market helps companies raise long-term funds for expansion without depending solely on bank credit. It allows the government to raise resources through bonds. It provides an exit route for early investors (including venture capital and private equity) and enables efficient allocation of capital across sectors. The growth of the capital market is therefore closely linked to the overall growth and modernisation of the Indian economy.

## Risks and Challenges

- Market volatility and the possibility of sharp corrections
- Information asymmetry between promoters and small investors
- Risk of fraud, insider trading, and market manipulation (SEBI continuously works to curb these)
- Over-dependence on foreign portfolio flows, which can be volatile
- Need for greater financial literacy among retail investors
- Balancing the interests of promoters, institutional investors, and retail investors

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Capital market deals with long-term funds; money market with short-term funds.
- Primary market is for new issues; secondary market for trading existing securities.
- BSE (Sensex – 30 stocks) and NSE (Nifty 50) are the two main exchanges.
- SEBI is the statutory regulator of the securities market.
- Dematerialisation is done through NSDL and CDSL.
- FPIs and DIIs are major institutional participants.
- IPOs, FPOs, rights issues, and private placements are methods of raising capital in the primary market.
- Derivatives (futures and options) are used for hedging and speculation.
- Major reforms since the 1990s have transformed the Indian capital market into a modern, electronic, and relatively transparent system.

Understanding the structure, instruments, and regulation of the capital market is essential for analysing issues related to investment, corporate finance, financial stability, and inclusive growth.
