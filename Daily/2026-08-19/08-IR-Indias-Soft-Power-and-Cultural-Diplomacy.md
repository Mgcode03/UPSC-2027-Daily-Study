# India’s Soft Power and Cultural Diplomacy

## Concept of Soft Power

The term “soft power” was coined by Joseph Nye to describe the ability of a country to shape the preferences of others through attraction rather than coercion or payment. Soft power arises from the attractiveness of a country’s culture, political values, and foreign policies. In contrast to hard power (military and economic might), soft power works through persuasion, agenda-setting, and the generation of soft attraction.

For India, soft power has deep historical roots. The spread of Buddhism, the influence of Indian epics and philosophy across Asia, and the legacy of non-violence associated with Mahatma Gandhi are early examples of civilisational influence. In the contemporary period, India consciously employs cultural diplomacy, educational exchanges, development partnerships, and the global appeal of its diaspora, yoga, cinema, and democratic values as instruments of soft power.

## Sources of India’s Soft Power

### 1. Civilisational and Cultural Heritage

India’s ancient civilisation, philosophical traditions (Vedanta, Buddhism, Jainism), classical arts, literature, and architectural heritage have long exercised influence beyond its borders. The transmission of Buddhism to East and Southeast Asia, the popularity of the Ramayana and Mahabharata in various cultural forms across the region, and the historical role of Indian traders and scholars are part of this legacy.

### 2. Yoga and Ayurveda

Yoga has become a global phenomenon. The declaration of 21 June as the International Day of Yoga by the United Nations (following an Indian initiative) is a landmark in India’s cultural diplomacy. Ayurveda and other traditional systems of medicine have also gained international attention, contributing to India’s image as a source of holistic well-being.

### 3. Indian Cinema and Popular Culture

Bollywood and regional cinema have large audiences in South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, Central Asia, and among diaspora communities worldwide. Indian music, television serials, and increasingly digital content contribute to cultural familiarity and positive perceptions of India.

### 4. Democratic Values and Pluralism

India’s identity as the world’s largest democracy, with a functioning constitutional system, free press, independent judiciary, and experience of managing deep diversity, is a significant soft power asset. The narrative of democratic resilience and inclusive development resonates in many parts of the Global South.

### 5. Diaspora

The Indian diaspora (estimated at over 30 million) is an important bridge. Successful professionals, entrepreneurs, and political leaders of Indian origin in various countries enhance India’s image and create networks of influence. Diaspora engagement is a structured component of India’s foreign policy.

### 6. Development Cooperation and Capacity Building

India’s development partnership programmes — including lines of credit, grants, technical assistance, and training under the Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation (ITEC) programme — build goodwill, especially in Africa, South Asia, and other developing regions. The emphasis on demand-driven, consultative cooperation differentiates India’s approach.

### 7. Education and Knowledge

Indian universities, institutes of technology and management, and the growing number of international students in India (and Indian students abroad) create long-term people-to-people ties. Scholarship programmes and educational exchanges are instruments of soft power.

### 8. Spiritual and Religious Soft Power

India is the birthplace of major religions and spiritual traditions. Buddhist diplomacy (engagement with East and Southeast Asian countries through Buddhist heritage), as well as the appeal of spiritual tourism and ashrams, contribute to soft influence.

## Instruments of Cultural Diplomacy

### Indian Council for Cultural Relations (ICCR)

The primary institutional vehicle for India’s cultural diplomacy. ICCR establishes cultural centres abroad, organises festivals, supports chairs of Indian studies in foreign universities, offers scholarships, and facilitates exchange of artists and intellectuals.

### Festivals and Cultural Events

India organises and participates in cultural festivals, film festivals, food festivals, and year-long country-focused cultural programmes. These events showcase diversity and create direct experiential engagement.

### Public Diplomacy Division and Digital Outreach

The Ministry of External Affairs uses traditional and digital media to communicate India’s perspectives, values, and achievements. Social media engagement, webinars, and digital content have expanded the reach of public diplomacy.

### Yoga Diplomacy and Wellness

Beyond the International Day of Yoga, India promotes yoga education, training of teachers, and collaboration with foreign institutions. Wellness tourism is an emerging dimension.

### Archaeological and Heritage Cooperation

Conservation projects, joint archaeological work, and the restoration of heritage sites in partner countries (especially in Asia) strengthen cultural ties and project India’s expertise.

### Sports and People-to-People Exchanges

Cricket diplomacy, training of athletes, and youth exchanges contribute to familiarity and goodwill.

## Soft Power in India’s Neighbourhood and Beyond

In South Asia and the extended neighbourhood, cultural affinity, shared historical experiences, and people-to-people ties provide a foundation for engagement. However, soft power is not automatic; it must be cultivated carefully and is affected by political relations, economic asymmetries, and perceptions of India’s regional role.

Beyond the neighbourhood, India’s soft power is visible in Africa (through capacity building, education, and the appeal of democratic development), in the Indo-Pacific (through shared democratic values and cultural links), and among global diaspora communities.

## Limitations and Challenges

- Soft power is diffuse and difficult to measure. Attraction does not always translate into policy influence.
- Domestic challenges (social tensions, governance deficits, economic inequalities) can undermine the attractiveness of India’s model.
- Competition from other cultural industries and narratives is intense.
- Institutional capacity for cultural diplomacy, though improved, still faces resource and coordination constraints.
- Soft power cannot substitute for hard power capabilities or coherent economic and strategic policies; it is most effective when aligned with overall foreign policy goals.

## Soft Power and Foreign Policy Strategy

India increasingly integrates soft power into its broader foreign policy. The emphasis on “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam” (the world is one family), the projection of India as a democratic and pluralistic partner, and the use of cultural and developmental diplomacy to build long-term relationships are part of this approach. Soft power complements strategic autonomy, multi-alignment, and the pursuit of national interests in a complex international environment.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Soft power (Joseph Nye): attraction through culture, values, and policies.
- Major sources for India: civilisation, yoga, cinema, democracy, diaspora, development cooperation, education, spirituality.
- ICCR is the main institutional vehicle for cultural diplomacy.
- International Day of Yoga (21 June) is a landmark achievement.
- Diaspora is both a soft power asset and a foreign policy priority.
- Soft power is complementary to, not a substitute for, hard power and coherent strategy.
- Domestic performance affects external attractiveness.

India’s soft power is rooted in its civilisational depth and contemporary achievements. When combined with consistent domestic progress and skilful diplomacy, it enhances India’s ability to shape its external environment through attraction and partnership rather than coercion alone.
