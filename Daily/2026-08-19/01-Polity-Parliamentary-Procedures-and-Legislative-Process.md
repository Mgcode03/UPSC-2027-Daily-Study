# Parliamentary Procedures and Legislative Process in India

## Introduction to Parliamentary Functioning

The Indian Parliament operates under a detailed set of procedures derived from the Constitution, Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha, and established parliamentary conventions. These procedures ensure orderly debate, accountability of the executive, and the passage of legislation in a democratic manner. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for grasping how laws are made, how the government is held accountable, and how the two Houses interact.

Parliament consists of the President and the two Houses — Lok Sabha (House of the People) and Rajya Sabha (Council of States). The President summons, prorogues, and dissolves the Lok Sabha. Each House has its own Rules of Procedure, though many features are similar.

## Sessions of Parliament

Parliament meets in three regular sessions each year:

- Budget Session (February–May): Longest session, includes presentation of the Union Budget, discussion on the Economic Survey, and passage of the Finance Bill and Appropriation Bills.
- Monsoon Session (July–September): Focuses on legislative business and discussion of important national issues.
- Winter Session (November–December): Shorter session for remaining legislative work and policy discussions.

A session is the period between the first sitting and the prorogation of the House. The President summons Parliament under Article 85. The gap between two sessions cannot exceed six months. Prorogation ends a session, while adjournment is a temporary break within a sitting. Dissolution applies only to Lok Sabha and ends its life, necessitating fresh elections.

## Summoning, Adjournment, Prorogation and Dissolution

- **Summoning**: The President summons each House to meet. The maximum gap between two sessions is six months.
- **Adjournment**: Suspension of the sitting of the House for a specified time. The Speaker/Chairman decides the time of reassembly.
- **Adjournment sine die**: Suspension without fixing a date for the next sitting. The House is later summoned by the President.
- **Prorogation**: Formal end of a session by the President. All pending business (except certain bills and motions) lapses.
- **Dissolution**: Applies only to Lok Sabha. It can occur automatically after five years or earlier by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha; all pending business lapses (except bills pending in Rajya Sabha that originated there).

## Types of Motions in Parliament

Motions are formal proposals moved by members for the consideration of the House. Key types include:

### Substantive Motions
These are self-contained independent proposals dealing with important matters. Examples include motions of thanks on the President’s Address, motions of confidence or no-confidence, and motions for the impeachment of the President or removal of judges.

### Substitute Motions
Moved in substitution of an original motion. If adopted, it supersedes the original motion.

### Subsidiary Motions
These depend upon or relate to other motions or proceedings. They are further classified as:

- **Ancillary Motions**: Used for the orderly transaction of business (e.g., “That the Bill be taken into consideration”).
- **Superseding Motions**: Seek to supersede the original motion (e.g., motion for adjournment of debate).
- **Amendments**: Seek to modify the original motion.

### Closure Motions
Used to cut short the debate. Types include:

- Simple Closure: “That the question be now put.”
- Closure by Compartments: Debate is closed on specific clauses or groups of clauses.
- Kangaroo Closure: Only important clauses are discussed; intervening clauses are skipped.
- Guillotine: Remaining undiscussed clauses are put to vote at a fixed time without further debate (commonly used for budget demands).

### Privilege Motions
Moved when a member feels that a minister or any member has committed a breach of privilege of the House or its members. It is a serious motion and requires the Speaker’s consent.

### Calling Attention Motion
A member calls the attention of a minister to a matter of urgent public importance. The minister makes a brief statement. No debate follows, though clarifications may be sought. It is an Indian innovation.

### Adjournment Motion
Moved to adjourn the normal business of the House to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance. It requires the support of 50 members in Lok Sabha. If admitted, it leads to a discussion and is treated as a censure against the government if passed. It is not available in Rajya Sabha.

### No-Confidence Motion
Under Article 75, the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. A no-confidence motion can be moved only in the Lok Sabha. It requires the support of 50 members. If passed, the government must resign. There is no provision for a confidence motion in the Constitution, but it is a parliamentary practice.

### Censure Motion
Moved to censure the government or an individual minister for specific policies or actions. It can be moved in both Houses. Unlike a no-confidence motion, it need not lead to the resignation of the government unless the government treats it as such.

### Motion of Thanks
Moved after the President’s Address at the beginning of the first session each year. The motion is “That the Members of the Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha assembled in this session are deeply grateful to the President for the Address which he has been pleased to deliver to both Houses of Parliament assembled together.” Amendments can be moved. Defeat of the Motion of Thanks is treated as a loss of confidence.

## Legislative Process: How a Bill Becomes an Act

A Bill is a legislative proposal. It becomes an Act after being passed by both Houses and receiving the President’s assent.

### Classification of Bills

1. **Ordinary Bills**: Concern any matter other than financial subjects.
2. **Money Bills**: Defined under Article 110. Deal exclusively with taxation, borrowing, Consolidated Fund, etc. Certified by the Speaker. Rajya Sabha has limited powers — can only recommend amendments within 14 days; Lok Sabha may accept or reject them.
3. **Financial Bills**: 
   - Financial Bill Category I (Article 117(1)): Contains provisions of a Money Bill but also other matters. Requires President’s recommendation for introduction. Same procedure as Ordinary Bills after introduction.
   - Financial Bill Category II (Article 117(3)): Involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund but does not include Money Bill provisions. Ordinary procedure applies, but President’s recommendation is needed for consideration.
4. **Constitution Amendment Bills**: Under Article 368. Special majorities required. Some require ratification by half the state legislatures.
5. **Private Members’ Bills**: Introduced by members who are not ministers. Rarely passed.
6. **Government Bills**: Introduced by ministers.

### Stages of an Ordinary Bill

1. **First Reading**: Introduction of the Bill. In Lok Sabha, a minister or private member seeks leave. If opposed, a brief statement is made and the House decides. No discussion on merits at this stage. The Bill is published in the Gazette.

2. **Second Reading**: Most important stage. Consists of two stages:
   - General discussion on the principles of the Bill. The House may refer it to a Select Committee or Joint Committee, circulate it for public opinion, or take it into consideration immediately.
   - Clause-by-clause consideration. Amendments are moved, discussed, and voted upon. Each clause is voted.

3. **Third Reading**: Debate is restricted to acceptance or rejection of the Bill as a whole. No amendments (except minor formal ones) are allowed. Voting takes place.

4. **Transmission to the Other House**: The Bill goes through similar stages in the second House. The second House may:
   - Pass it without amendments.
   - Pass it with amendments (returned to the originating House).
   - Reject it.
   - Take no action (in which case, after six months, a joint sitting may be called for ordinary bills).

5. **Joint Sitting (Article 108)**: Convened by the President if there is disagreement between the two Houses on an ordinary bill. Presided over by the Speaker of Lok Sabha. Decision is by simple majority of members present and voting. Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills cannot be referred to a joint sitting.

6. **President’s Assent (Article 111)**: The President may:
   - Give assent (Bill becomes Act).
   - Withhold assent (absolute veto — rarely used).
   - Return the Bill for reconsideration (suspensive veto). If passed again, the President must give assent.
   - For Money Bills, the President cannot return the Bill; must give or withhold assent.

### Special Procedure for Money Bills (Article 110 & 109)

- Can be introduced only in Lok Sabha with the President’s recommendation.
- Speaker certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill; the decision is final.
- Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend a Money Bill. It can only make recommendations within 14 days.
- Lok Sabha may accept or reject the recommendations. The Bill is deemed passed in the form preferred by Lok Sabha.
- If Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed passed by both Houses.
- President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration.

## Parliamentary Committees

Committees perform detailed scrutiny that the full House cannot undertake due to time constraints. They are of two types: Standing Committees and Ad hoc Committees.

### Important Standing Committees

- **Financial Committees**:
  - Public Accounts Committee (PAC): Examines the annual audit reports of the CAG. Ensures that public money is spent in accordance with Parliament’s decisions. Has 22 members (15 from Lok Sabha, 7 from Rajya Sabha). Chairperson is traditionally from the Opposition.
  - Estimates Committee: Suggests alternative policies to bring efficiency and economy in administration. 30 members, all from Lok Sabha.
  - Committee on Public Undertakings: Examines the reports and accounts of public sector undertakings. 22 members (15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha).

- **Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)**: 24 committees covering all ministries. Examine Demands for Grants, Bills, and long-term policy documents. Each has 31 members (21 Lok Sabha + 10 Rajya Sabha).

- **Other Important Committees**:
  - Business Advisory Committee: Recommends allocation of time for discussion of government and other business.
  - Committee on Privileges: Examines questions of privilege.
  - Committee on Government Assurances: Ensures that assurances given by ministers on the floor of the House are fulfilled.
  - Committee on Subordinate Legislation: Scrutinises rules, regulations, and bye-laws made by the executive under delegated legislation.
  - Committee on Petitions: Considers petitions submitted by the public.
  - Ethics Committee: Oversees the moral and ethical conduct of members.

## Question Hour and Zero Hour

- **Question Hour**: The first hour of every sitting is reserved for questions. Questions are of three types:
  - Starred Questions: Require oral answers; supplementary questions allowed.
  - Unstarred Questions: Require written answers; no supplementaries.
  - Short Notice Questions: Relate to matters of urgent public importance; can be asked with shorter notice.

- **Zero Hour**: An Indian innovation. Starts immediately after Question Hour. Members raise matters of urgent public importance without prior notice. Not mentioned in the Rules of Procedure; it is a convention.

## Budgetary Process in Parliament

The annual financial statement (Budget) is presented under Article 112. The process involves:

1. Presentation of the Budget by the Finance Minister.
2. General discussion on the Budget.
3. Scrutiny by Departmentally Related Standing Committees.
4. Voting on Demands for Grants (only in Lok Sabha). Guillotine is applied to remaining demands.
5. Passing of the Appropriation Bill (authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund).
6. Passing of the Finance Bill (gives effect to the taxation proposals).

Vote on Account may be passed if the full budget cannot be passed before the start of the financial year (especially in election years).

## Key Constitutional Provisions

- Article 85: Sessions of Parliament, prorogation and dissolution.
- Article 107: Provisions as to introduction and passing of Bills.
- Article 108: Joint sitting of both Houses.
- Article 109: Special procedure for Money Bills.
- Article 110: Definition of Money Bills.
- Article 111: Assent to Bills.
- Article 112: Annual financial statement.
- Article 113: Procedure in Parliament with respect to estimates.
- Article 114: Appropriation Bills.
- Article 117: Special provisions for Financial Bills.

## Important Conventions and Practices

- Collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha.
- The Speaker’s certification of a Money Bill is final and cannot be questioned in any court.
- The Leader of the Opposition is recognised in both Houses (with certain numerical requirements).
- Pairing system allows members of opposite parties to be absent together without affecting voting strength.
- Crossing the floor is regulated by the anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule).

## Significance of Parliamentary Procedures

These procedures balance efficiency with deliberation. They protect the rights of the minority while allowing the majority to govern. Devices such as Question Hour, Zero Hour, adjournment motions, and parliamentary committees ensure continuous accountability of the executive. The detailed legislative process provides multiple opportunities for scrutiny, amendment, and public input. Understanding these procedures is fundamental to appreciating the working of India’s parliamentary democracy.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Maximum gap between two sessions: 6 months.
- Money Bill: Article 110; Speaker’s certificate final; Rajya Sabha has only 14 days.
- Joint sitting: Only for ordinary bills; Speaker presides.
- No-confidence motion: Only in Lok Sabha; requires 50 members’ support.
- PAC Chairperson: Traditionally from Opposition.
- Zero Hour: Indian innovation; after Question Hour.
- Guillotine: Applied to remaining Demands for Grants.
- President’s options on a Bill: Assent, withhold, return (except Money Bill).

This detailed understanding of parliamentary procedures forms the foundation for analysing legislative developments, government accountability, and the dynamics of Indian democracy.
