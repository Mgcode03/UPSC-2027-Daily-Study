# Environmental Governance and Institutions in India

## Concept of Environmental Governance

Environmental governance refers to the set of regulatory processes, mechanisms, and organisations through which political actors influence environmental actions and outcomes. It involves the state, civil society, markets, and international institutions. In India, environmental governance has evolved from a relatively weak regulatory framework in the early decades after independence to a more comprehensive system driven by legislation, judicial intervention, and growing public awareness.

The Constitution of India originally did not contain specific provisions on the environment. The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, inserted Article 48A (Directive Principle) directing the State to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife, and Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty) imposing a duty on every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment.

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

### Constitutional Provisions

- Article 48A: Protection and improvement of environment and safeguarding of forests and wildlife.
- Article 51A(g): Fundamental duty to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife.
- Article 21: The right to life has been interpreted by the Supreme Court to include the right to a healthy environment.
- Article 253: Empowers Parliament to make laws for implementing international treaties (used for environmental legislation).

### Key Environmental Legislations

- **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972**: Provides for the protection of wild animals, birds, and plants. Creates schedules of protected species and provides for the establishment of protected areas (National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, Conservation Reserves, Community Reserves).
- **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974**: The first major environmental law. Establishes Central and State Pollution Control Boards to prevent and control water pollution.
- **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980**: Restricts the de-reservation of forests and use of forest land for non-forest purposes without prior approval of the Central Government.
- **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981**: Provides for the prevention, control, and abatement of air pollution. Expanded the mandate of Pollution Control Boards.
- **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**: An umbrella legislation enacted in the wake of the Bhopal Gas Tragedy. Empowers the Central Government to take all necessary measures to protect and improve the environment. Enables the framing of rules on various aspects (EIA, hazardous substances, coastal regulation, etc.).
- **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**: Enacted to give effect to the Convention on Biological Diversity. Provides for conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits.
- **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010**: Establishes the National Green Tribunal for effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection and conservation of forests and other natural resources.

## Institutional Framework

### Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC)

The nodal ministry for environmental policy, planning, promotion, and coordination. Its responsibilities include:

- Formulation of environmental policy and legislation
- Conservation and survey of flora, fauna, forests, and wildlife
- Prevention and control of pollution
- Afforestation and regeneration of degraded areas
- Protection of the environment
- Welfare of animals

### Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)

Established under the Water Act, 1974. Functions include:

- Advising the Central Government on pollution-related matters
- Coordinating the activities of State Pollution Control Boards
- Providing technical assistance and guidance
- Setting standards for air and water quality
- Monitoring pollution levels
- Preparing manuals, codes, and guidelines

### State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) / Pollution Control Committees (PCCs)

Operate at the state/UT level. Responsible for implementing pollution control laws, granting consents to establish and operate, monitoring compliance, and taking enforcement action.

### National Green Tribunal (NGT)

Established in 2010. It is a specialised body equipped with the necessary expertise to handle environmental disputes involving multi-disciplinary issues. Key features:

- Jurisdiction over civil cases relating to environmental protection and conservation.
- Power to order relief and compensation to victims of pollution and other environmental damage.
- Guided by principles of natural justice, sustainable development, precautionary principle, and polluter pays principle.
- Decisions can be challenged before the Supreme Court.

### National Board for Wildlife (NBWL)

Chaired by the Prime Minister. Advises the Central Government on wildlife policy and the establishment of protected areas. Approval of the NBWL (or its Standing Committee) is required for certain activities inside protected areas and eco-sensitive zones.

### Forest Survey of India (FSI)

Assesses forest cover and publishes the India State of Forest Report periodically. Provides data crucial for policy and planning.

### Botanical Survey of India (BSI) and Zoological Survey of India (ZSI)

Responsible for the survey and documentation of plant and animal resources respectively.

### National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)

Established under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002. Regulates access to biological resources and associated traditional knowledge, and ensures benefit sharing.

## Important Principles of Environmental Governance

### Sustainable Development

Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The concept was popularised by the Brundtland Commission (1987) and has been repeatedly affirmed by Indian courts.

### Precautionary Principle

Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. Accepted by the Supreme Court of India as part of the environmental law of the land.

### Polluter Pays Principle

The polluter is liable to pay the cost of pollution abatement, environmental restoration, and compensation to victims. This principle has been applied in several landmark judgments.

### Public Trust Doctrine

Natural resources such as air, water, and forests are held by the government in trust for the public. The state cannot transfer these resources for private ownership if it affects public interest.

### Inter-generational Equity

The present generation holds the earth in trust for future generations. Resource use must not compromise the rights of those yet to be born.

## Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)

EIA is a key tool of environmental governance. It is a process of evaluating the likely environmental impacts of a proposed project or development, taking into account inter-related socio-economic, cultural, and human-health impacts. In India, EIA is mandated under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and detailed notifications have been issued (notably the EIA Notification of 2006, as amended).

The process generally involves screening, scoping, public consultation, appraisal, and decision-making. Categories of projects (A and B) determine whether clearance is granted at the Central or State level.

## Role of the Judiciary

The Indian judiciary, particularly the Supreme Court and High Courts, has played a transformative role in environmental governance through Public Interest Litigation (PIL). Landmark contributions include:

- Expanding Article 21 to include the right to a healthy environment.
- Applying the principles of sustainable development, precautionary principle, polluter pays, and public trust.
- Issuing continuing mandamus in cases such as the cleaning of the Ganga, protection of the Taj Mahal, and control of air pollution in Delhi.
- Ordering the creation of authorities and monitoring committees.

The National Green Tribunal has further specialised and expedited environmental adjudication.

## Challenges in Environmental Governance

- Weak enforcement capacity and understaffing of regulatory agencies.
- Conflicts between development and conservation objectives.
- Overlapping jurisdictions and coordination problems among institutions.
- Inadequate monitoring and data systems.
- Limited public participation in decision-making in some cases.
- Emerging challenges such as climate change, plastic pollution, and electronic waste that require new regulatory responses.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- 42nd Amendment introduced Articles 48A and 51A(g).
- Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is the umbrella legislation.
- NGT established in 2010; applies precautionary and polluter pays principles.
- CPCB and SPCBs are the main pollution control agencies.
- NBWL is chaired by the Prime Minister.
- EIA is a major preventive tool under the Environment (Protection) Act.
- Public Trust Doctrine and Inter-generational Equity are recognised by courts.
- Article 21 has been interpreted to include the right to a clean environment.

Effective environmental governance requires strong institutions, clear laws, scientific capacity, public participation, and the political will to balance development with ecological sustainability. India’s framework has evolved significantly, but implementation remains the critical challenge.
