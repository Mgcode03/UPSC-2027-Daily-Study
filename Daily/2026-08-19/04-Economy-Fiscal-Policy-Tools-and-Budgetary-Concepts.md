# Fiscal Policy Tools and Budgetary Concepts in India

## Meaning and Objectives of Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy refers to the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. It is the counterpart of monetary policy (which is managed by the Reserve Bank of India). In India, fiscal policy is formulated by the Ministry of Finance and is expressed primarily through the annual Union Budget.

The main objectives of fiscal policy in India include:

- Promoting economic growth and development
- Maintaining price stability
- Achieving full employment and reducing unemployment
- Reducing income and regional inequalities
- Ensuring external balance
- Allocating resources according to national priorities
- Stabilising the economy against cyclical fluctuations

## Components of the Government Budget

The Union Budget is divided into two main parts: the Revenue Budget and the Capital Budget.

### Revenue Budget

Consists of revenue receipts and revenue expenditure.

**Revenue Receipts**:
- Tax Revenue: Corporation tax, income tax, goods and services tax (GST), customs duties, excise duties, etc.
- Non-Tax Revenue: Interest receipts, dividends and profits from public sector enterprises, fees, fines, external grants, etc.

**Revenue Expenditure**: Expenditure that neither creates assets nor reduces liabilities. Includes interest payments, subsidies, salaries, pensions, defence revenue expenditure, and grants to states.

### Capital Budget

Consists of capital receipts and capital expenditure.

**Capital Receipts**:
- Debt receipts: Market borrowings, external loans, short-term borrowings
- Non-debt receipts: Recoveries of loans, proceeds from disinvestment, small savings (in some classifications)

**Capital Expenditure**: Expenditure that creates assets or reduces liabilities. Includes expenditure on creation of infrastructure, loans to states, repayment of loans, and defence capital expenditure.

## Key Budgetary Concepts and Deficits

Understanding deficits is crucial for analysing the health of public finances.

### Revenue Deficit

Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Receipts

It indicates the extent to which the government is borrowing to finance its current consumption. A high revenue deficit is considered undesirable because it means the government is living beyond its means and borrowing for consumption rather than investment.

### Effective Revenue Deficit

Effective Revenue Deficit = Revenue Deficit – Grants for creation of capital assets

This concept was introduced to distinguish between pure consumption expenditure and grants that lead to asset creation by other entities (mainly state governments).

### Fiscal Deficit

Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure – (Revenue Receipts + Non-debt Capital Receipts)

It represents the total borrowing requirement of the government. Fiscal deficit is the most comprehensive measure of the government’s deficit. It is usually expressed as a percentage of GDP.

### Primary Deficit

Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit – Interest Payments

It shows the fiscal deficit excluding the interest burden of past borrowings. A zero primary deficit means the government is borrowing only to pay interest; any positive primary deficit means fresh borrowing for current expenditure.

### Budget Deficit (older concept)

Budget Deficit = Total Expenditure – Total Receipts (including borrowings). This concept is no longer in official use because it does not clearly show the borrowing requirement.

## Fiscal Policy Tools

### 1. Taxation

Taxation is the primary source of revenue and an important tool for redistribution and resource allocation.

- **Direct Taxes**: Levied on income and wealth (personal income tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax). These are progressive in nature and help reduce inequality.
- **Indirect Taxes**: Levied on goods and services (GST, customs, excise). These can be regressive unless carefully designed with exemptions and lower rates on essential items.

Changes in tax rates, tax base, exemptions, and compliance mechanisms are used to influence disposable income, investment, and consumption.

### 2. Public Expenditure

Government spending on infrastructure, education, health, defence, subsidies, and social welfare directly affects aggregate demand and long-term productive capacity. Capital expenditure is particularly important for growth because it creates productive assets.

### 3. Public Debt and Borrowing

The government borrows from domestic markets (through dated securities, treasury bills) and external sources. The management of public debt (maturity profile, interest rates, and composition) has implications for interest rates in the economy and the sustainability of finances.

### 4. Subsidies

Subsidies on food, fertilisers, petroleum, and other items are used to protect vulnerable sections and achieve specific policy goals. However, poorly targeted subsidies can create fiscal stress and distort markets.

### 5. Disinvestment and Privatisation

Proceeds from the sale of public sector equity are treated as non-debt capital receipts and help finance the fiscal deficit without additional borrowing.

## Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act

Enacted in 2003 (and subsequently amended), the FRBM Act aims to ensure fiscal discipline, reduce the fiscal deficit, and improve inter-generational equity. Key features include:

- Setting targets for fiscal deficit and revenue deficit (or effective revenue deficit).
- Elimination of revenue deficit and reduction of fiscal deficit to a specified percentage of GDP.
- Introduction of the Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement, Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement, and Macro-Economic Framework Statement along with the Budget.
- Escape clauses allowing deviation from targets under exceptional circumstances (national security, natural calamities, severe economic downturn, etc.).

The Act has undergone revisions; targets have been adjusted in response to economic conditions (including the COVID-19 pandemic). The emphasis remains on transparency, medium-term planning, and sustainability of public debt.

## Counter-Cyclical vs Pro-Cyclical Fiscal Policy

- **Counter-cyclical policy**: During a recession, the government increases spending or cuts taxes to stimulate demand (expansionary fiscal policy). During a boom, it reduces spending or raises taxes to prevent overheating (contractionary fiscal policy).
- **Pro-cyclical policy**: Government spending rises during booms and falls during recessions, amplifying economic fluctuations. Developing countries often find it difficult to follow purely counter-cyclical policies because of revenue constraints during downturns.

India has attempted to move towards a more counter-cyclical and rules-based fiscal framework through the FRBM Act and successive Finance Commissions.

## Fiscal Federalism and Vertical/Horizontal Imbalances

India is a federal country. The Union government collects a large share of taxes, while state governments have major expenditure responsibilities (health, education, law and order). This creates a vertical imbalance. Horizontal imbalances arise because states have different revenue capacities and expenditure needs.

The Finance Commission (constituted every five years under Article 280) recommends the distribution of the net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the states (vertical devolution) and among the states (horizontal distribution). It also recommends grants-in-aid. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) has further changed the landscape of fiscal federalism by creating a shared tax base and a GST Council for cooperative decision-making.

## Important Concepts Related to the Budget

- **Consolidated Fund of India (Article 266)**: All revenues received, loans raised, and recoveries of loans go into this fund. No money can be spent without parliamentary appropriation.
- **Public Account of India**: Contains receipts such as provident funds, small savings, and deposits, which do not belong to the government but must be paid back. Expenditure from the Public Account does not require parliamentary appropriation.
- **Contingency Fund of India (Article 267)**: Placed at the disposal of the President to meet unforeseen expenditure. Parliamentary approval is obtained later.
- **Demand for Grants**: The estimates of expenditure from the Consolidated Fund are presented in the form of Demands for Grants. Voting on Demands is an exclusive privilege of the Lok Sabha.
- **Appropriation Bill**: Authorises the government to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund.
- **Finance Bill**: Gives effect to the taxation proposals of the government.
- **Vote on Account**: Advance grant to meet expenses for a short period when the full budget cannot be passed before the start of the financial year.
- **Guillotine**: The process by which all outstanding Demands for Grants are put to vote at a predetermined time without further discussion.

## Quality of Fiscal Deficit and Crowding Out

Not all fiscal deficits are equal. A fiscal deficit used to finance capital expenditure (which creates assets) is considered better than one used to finance revenue expenditure (consumption). Persistent high fiscal deficits can lead to:

- Higher interest rates (crowding out of private investment)
- Inflationary pressures if financed by monetisation
- Rising public debt and interest burden
- Reduced fiscal space for future priorities

Hence, the composition of the deficit and the sustainability of the debt-GDP ratio are closely monitored.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure – (Revenue Receipts + Non-debt Capital Receipts)
- Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit – Interest Payments
- Revenue Deficit indicates borrowing for consumption.
- FRBM Act aims at fiscal discipline and inter-generational equity.
- Finance Commission deals with vertical and horizontal distribution of resources.
- Consolidated Fund requires parliamentary appropriation; Public Account does not.
- Capital expenditure is growth-enhancing; revenue expenditure is largely consumption.
- GST is a major milestone in cooperative fiscal federalism.

A sound understanding of fiscal policy tools and budgetary concepts enables analysis of government priorities, macroeconomic stability, and the sustainability of public finances — all of which are central to India’s economic development.
