# Indian Physiographic Divisions – A Detailed Study

## Introduction

India’s physical landscape is the result of complex geological processes operating over millions of years. The present physiography reflects the collision of the Indian plate with the Eurasian plate, the deposition of sediments in the Tethys Sea, volcanic activity, and prolonged erosion and deposition by rivers. Understanding the major physiographic divisions is fundamental to grasping climate, drainage, soils, natural vegetation, agriculture, and settlement patterns.

India can be divided into six major physiographic divisions:

1. The Northern and North-Eastern Mountains
2. The Northern Plain
3. The Peninsular Plateau
4. The Indian Desert
5. The Coastal Plains
6. The Islands

## 1. The Northern and North-Eastern Mountains

This is the youngest and most dynamic physiographic division, formed by the collision of the Indian plate with the Eurasian plate. The Himalayas are still rising.

### Longitudinal Divisions of the Himalayas

- **Punjab Himalayas** (Indus to Satluj): Includes the Kashmir and Himachal ranges. Famous valleys — Kashmir, Kangra, Kullu.
- **Kumaon Himalayas** (Satluj to Kali): Includes Nanda Devi, Kamet, and the famous hill stations of Nainital, Mussoorie, and Ranikhet.
- **Nepal Himalayas** (Kali to Tista): Contains the highest peaks — Mount Everest, Kanchenjunga, Makalu, Dhaulagiri, Annapurna.
- **Assam Himalayas** (Tista to Dihang): Lower elevation compared to Nepal Himalayas; includes the Bhutan and Arunachal ranges.

### Transverse Divisions (North-South)

- **Trans-Himalayas** (or Tibetan Himalayas): North of the Great Himalayas. Includes the Karakoram, Ladakh, Zaskar, and Kailash ranges. The Karakoram contains K2 (Godwin Austen), the second highest peak in the world. The region is characterised by cold desert conditions and extensive glaciers (Siachen, Baltoro, Biafo).

- **Great Himalayas (Himadri)**: The highest and most continuous range. Average elevation 6,000 m. Contains all the loftiest peaks. The snowline is around 6,000 m in the west and lower in the east. Important passes — Zoji La, Shipki La, Nathu La, Bomdi La.

- **Lesser Himalayas (Himachal)**: Average elevation 3,700–4,500 m. Composed of highly compressed and altered rocks. Famous ranges — Pir Panjal, Dhaula Dhar, Mussoorie, and Nag Tibba. The Kashmir Valley, Kangra Valley, and Kullu Valley lie in this zone. Hill stations such as Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital, and Darjeeling are located here.

- **Shiwaliks (Outer Himalayas)**: Southernmost range. Average elevation 900–1,200 m. Formed of unconsolidated sediments brought down by rivers. The longitudinal valleys between the Lesser Himalayas and Shiwaliks are called Duns (e.g., Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun). The region is prone to earthquakes and landslides.

### North-Eastern Hills and Mountains

Beyond the Dihang gorge, the Himalayas bend sharply to the south and form the Eastern Hills or Purvanchal. These include the Patkai Bum, Naga Hills, Manipur Hills, Mizo Hills (Lushai Hills), and the Garo-Khasi-Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya. The Meghalaya Plateau is a northward extension of the Peninsular Plateau and is separated from the main Peninsular block by the Garo-Rajmahal Gap.

## 2. The Northern Plain

Formed by the alluvial deposits of the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra river systems. It is a depositional plain of great fertility and supports a large population.

### Divisions of the Northern Plain

- **Punjab Plain**: Western part, formed mainly by the Indus and its tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Satluj). Characterised by doabs (land between two rivers). The plain is intensively irrigated and is a major wheat-producing region.

- **Ganga Plain**: Extends from the Yamuna River in the west to the Bangladesh border in the east. Further subdivided into:
  - Upper Ganga Plain (west of the Gandak)
  - Middle Ganga Plain
  - Lower Ganga Plain (including the deltaic region)

- **Brahmaputra Plain**: In Assam. Characterised by a steep slope, thick alluvial cover, and numerous river islands (the largest is Majuli). The plain is prone to flooding and bank erosion.

### Longitudinal Belts of the Northern Plain

- **Bhabar**: A narrow belt (8–16 km wide) running along the foot of the Shiwaliks. Consists of pebbles and boulders. Streams disappear underground here.
- **Terai**: South of Bhabar. A wet, marshy, and densely forested belt where underground streams re-emerge. Much of the Terai has been reclaimed for agriculture.
- **Bhangar**: Older alluvium. Forms the upland alluvial terraces. Contains calcareous concretions called kankar.
- **Khadar**: Newer alluvium. Deposited by floods almost every year. Extremely fertile.

## 3. The Peninsular Plateau

One of the oldest and most stable landmasses of India. It is a tableland composed of old crystalline, igneous, and metamorphic rocks. The plateau is broadly triangular, with its base along the Satpura-Vindhya ranges and apex pointing southward.

### Major Divisions

- **Central Highlands**: Lie north of the Narmada River. Include the Malwa Plateau, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand, and the Chotanagpur Plateau. The Chotanagpur Plateau is rich in minerals (coal, iron ore, mica, bauxite). The Aravali Range, one of the oldest fold mountains, runs across the western part in a north-east to south-west direction.

- **Deccan Plateau**: South of the Narmada. Bounded by the Western Ghats (Sahyadris) in the west, Eastern Ghats in the east, and the Satpura-Mahadeo-Maikal ranges in the north. The general slope is from west to east, which is why major rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi) flow eastward into the Bay of Bengal. The western part is covered by the Deccan Trap — a thick layer of basaltic lava flows that gives rise to black cotton soil (regur).

### Western Ghats (Sahyadris)

- Run parallel to the western coast from the Tapi River to Kanyakumari.
- Average elevation 900–1,600 m; highest peak Anaimudi (2,695 m) in the Anaimalai Hills.
- Continuous and higher than the Eastern Ghats; hence act as an orographic barrier causing heavy rainfall on the windward side.
- Important passes: Thal Ghat, Bhor Ghat, Pal Ghat (Palghat Gap).
- Biodiversity hotspot; source of many rivers.

### Eastern Ghats

- Run parallel to the eastern coast, discontinuous and lower than the Western Ghats.
- Highest peak: Mahendragiri (1,501 m).
- Breached by major rivers — Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri.
- Important hills: Javadi, Shevaroy, Nallamala, Palkonda, and the Nilgiri Hills (where Eastern and Western Ghats meet).

### Other Features of the Peninsular Plateau

- The Satpura Range lies between the Narmada and Tapti rivers.
- The Vindhyan Range forms the northern boundary of the Deccan Plateau proper.
- The plateau is rich in minerals, especially in the north-eastern part (Chotanagpur) and the central parts.

## 4. The Indian Desert (Thar Desert)

Located in the north-western part of Rajasthan, extending into Gujarat, Haryana, and Punjab. It is a rain-shadow area lying on the leeward side of the Aravalis.

### Characteristics

- Low rainfall (less than 150 mm in the western part).
- Sandy plain with sand dunes (barchans and longitudinal dunes).
- Presence of playas (saline lakes) such as Sambhar, Kuchaman, and Didwana.
- The Luni is the only significant river; it is seasonal and ends in the Rann of Kachchh.
- Vegetation is xerophytic; agriculture is dependent on irrigation (Indira Gandhi Canal has transformed parts of the desert).

## 5. The Coastal Plains

Narrow strips of plain land lying between the mountains and the sea.

### Western Coastal Plain

- Extends from the Gujarat coast to Kerala.
- Narrower than the eastern coastal plain.
- Subdivided into:
  - Konkan Coast (Mumbai to Goa)
  - Karnataka (Canara) Coast
  - Malabar Coast (Kerala)
- Characterised by estuaries, creeks, and backwaters (kayals) in Kerala. The backwaters are a unique feature used for navigation, fishing, and tourism.
- Important ports: Mumbai, Marmagao, Cochin, Mangalore.

### Eastern Coastal Plain

- Extends from the West Bengal coast to Kanyakumari.
- Wider and more level than the western coastal plain.
- Subdivided into:
  - Northern Circars (north of the Godavari delta)
  - Coromandel Coast (south of the Godavari, especially Tamil Nadu)
- Characterised by well-developed deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri.
- Presence of lagoons and lakes (Chilika, Pulicat, Kolleru).
- Important ports: Kolkata, Paradip, Visakhapatnam, Chennai, Tuticorin.

## 6. The Islands

### Andaman and Nicobar Islands

- Located in the Bay of Bengal.
- Andaman group in the north; Nicobar group in the south.
- Separated by the Ten Degree Channel.
- These are elevated portions of submarine mountains.
- Barren Island is the only active volcano in India.
- Strategic importance; Indira Point is the southernmost point of India.

### Lakshadweep Islands

- Located in the Arabian Sea.
- Coral origin (atolls).
- Closest to Kerala.
- Minicoy is the largest and southernmost island.
- The Eight Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the Maldives.

## Geological Significance and Human Interaction

- The Himalayas are the source of perennial rivers and influence the monsoon.
- The Northern Plain is the most densely populated and agriculturally productive region.
- The Peninsular Plateau is the storehouse of minerals and has black soil ideal for cotton.
- The Western Ghats determine the distribution of rainfall on the west coast.
- The desert region requires special water management and has unique cultural adaptations.
- Coastal plains support ports, fishing, and dense settlement.
- Islands have strategic, ecological, and tourism significance.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Youngest landform: Himalayas.
- Oldest landform: Peninsular Plateau (and Aravalis).
- Highest peak in India: Kanchenjunga (if only Indian territory is considered); otherwise K2 is in the Karakoram.
- Highest peak in South India: Anaimudi.
- Largest river island: Majuli.
- Only active volcano: Barren Island.
- Passes in Western Ghats: Thal, Bhor, Palghat.
- Doabs: Characteristic of Punjab Plain.
- Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar: Belts of the Northern Plain.
- Deccan Trap: Source of black cotton soil.

A clear grasp of these physiographic divisions enables better understanding of India’s climate, drainage, soils, natural resources, and patterns of human settlement and economic activity.
