# Gandhian Era: Early Movements and Mass Nationalism

## Arrival of Gandhi and the New Phase

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in January 1915. By then he had already developed the technique of satyagraha through his struggles against racial discrimination in South Africa. His methods combined non-violence (ahimsa), truth (satya), and active resistance to injustice. The period from 1915 to 1922 marks the transition of the Indian national movement from elite constitutional agitation to genuine mass politics.

Gandhi spent the first year travelling across India, observing social and political conditions. He accepted the political mentorship of Gopal Krishna Gokhale and initially remained within the moderate framework of the Indian National Congress. However, his first major interventions were local and issue-based, which allowed him to test and refine his methods in the Indian context.

## Champaran Satyagraha, 1917

The Champaran district in Bihar was under the permanent settlement system. European planters forced cultivators to grow indigo on a portion of their land (tinkathia system) and sell it at prices fixed by the planters. When synthetic dyes made indigo less profitable, planters extracted heavy rents and illegal dues from the peasants.

Gandhi was invited by local leaders such as Rajkumar Shukla. He visited Champaran in April 1917, conducted detailed inquiries, and organised the peasants. The British administration initially ordered him to leave the district. Gandhi refused and offered civil disobedience. The government eventually appointed an inquiry committee with Gandhi as a member. The committee’s recommendations led to the abolition of the tinkathia system and the refund of a portion of the illegal dues.

Champaran demonstrated three important features of Gandhian politics: reliance on local grievances, meticulous collection of facts, and readiness for civil disobedience combined with willingness to negotiate. It also brought into the national movement a large number of peasants and local leaders who had previously remained outside Congress politics.

## Ahmedabad Mill Strike, 1918

In Ahmedabad, a dispute arose between mill owners and workers over the discontinuation of plague bonus and the demand for a wage increase. Gandhi was approached by Anasuya Sarabhai. He advised the workers to go on strike and simultaneously undertook a fast unto death to strengthen their resolve and put moral pressure on the mill owners.

The strike lasted about three weeks. Eventually a settlement was reached through arbitration, granting a 35 per cent wage increase. The Ahmedabad struggle introduced the element of the fast as a political weapon and showed Gandhi’s ability to intervene in industrial disputes while maintaining non-violence and discipline among workers.

## Kheda Satyagraha, 1918

In Kheda district of Gujarat, crops failed due to drought. The Gujarat Revenue Code permitted suspension of land revenue if the yield fell below a certain level. The government refused to grant widespread suspensions. Gandhi, supported by local leaders including Vallabhbhai Patel, organised the peasants to withhold payment of revenue.

The government initially adopted a hard line, attaching property and livestock. However, as the movement spread and public opinion turned against the administration, the authorities announced that revenue would be recovered only from those who could pay. Although not a complete victory, the Kheda satyagraha strengthened the organisation of the peasantry and brought Vallabhbhai Patel into national prominence.

## Rowlatt Satyagraha, 1919

The Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919) was enacted on the recommendations of the Rowlatt Committee. It empowered the government to imprison any person without trial and to try political cases without juries. The Act was widely seen as a betrayal of the promises of constitutional reform made during the First World War.

Gandhi called for a nationwide satyagraha. The programme included observance of a day of hartal, fasting, prayer, and civil disobedience against specific laws. The movement generated unprecedented popular participation across India. However, it also witnessed outbreaks of violence in some places, particularly in Punjab and Delhi.

The British response in Punjab was extremely repressive. The Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 13 April 1919, in which troops under General Dyer fired on an unarmed crowd in Amritsar, killing hundreds, became a turning point. The subsequent imposition of martial law and public humiliations further alienated Indian opinion. The Hunter Committee inquiry and the British government’s relatively mild treatment of Dyer deepened the sense of injustice.

The Rowlatt satyagraha marked the first all-India mass agitation under Gandhi’s leadership. It also revealed the difficulties of maintaining non-violence on a national scale and the readiness of the colonial state to use extreme force.

## Khilafat and Non-Cooperation

The end of the First World War brought the question of the future of the Ottoman Caliphate to the centre of Indian Muslim politics. The Treaty of Sevres and subsequent arrangements were seen as harsh towards Turkey. Indian Muslim leaders launched the Khilafat movement to pressurise the British government to protect the Caliph’s temporal powers.

Gandhi saw in the Khilafat issue an opportunity to unite Hindus and Muslims in a common struggle against British rule. He linked the Khilafat demand with the Punjab wrongs and the demand for swaraj. In 1920 the Congress, under Gandhi’s influence, adopted the programme of non-cooperation.

### Programme of Non-Cooperation

The non-cooperation programme included:

- Surrender of titles and honorary offices.
- Boycott of government schools, colleges and law courts.
- Boycott of foreign goods and promotion of swadeshi and khadi.
- Boycott of elections to the new legislatures under the Government of India Act, 1919.
- Resignation from government service where possible.
- Eventually, refusal to pay taxes if the movement progressed sufficiently.

The movement received massive popular response. Students left government institutions, lawyers boycotted courts, and the boycott of foreign cloth became widespread. National schools and colleges were established. The Congress organisation expanded rapidly into rural areas. The period also saw the growth of the volunteer movement and the constructive programme of spinning, Hindu-Muslim unity, and removal of untouchability.

### Chauri Chaura and Withdrawal

On 5 February 1922, a crowd of peasants and volunteers in Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur district, United Provinces) clashed with the police. The police fired on the crowd; in retaliation the crowd set fire to the police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi, who was preparing for a no-tax campaign in Bardoli, regarded the incident as a serious violation of the principle of non-violence. He insisted on the immediate suspension of the non-cooperation movement.

The decision to withdraw was controversial. Many leaders, including Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das and the younger generation, felt that the movement still had momentum and that isolated incidents of violence should not lead to a complete halt. Gandhi, however, remained firm. He was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced to six years’ imprisonment.

## Significance of the Early Gandhian Movements

The early movements transformed the character of Indian nationalism. Politics moved from the restricted circles of educated elites to the wider population of peasants, workers, students and women. New methods of struggle — satyagraha, hartal, boycott, constructive work — entered the political vocabulary. The Congress was reorganised on a mass basis with provincial and district committees and a large volunteer force.

At the same time, the movements revealed the limits of non-violent mass action under colonial conditions. Maintaining discipline over a vast and diverse population proved difficult. The colonial state retained the capacity for severe repression. The withdrawal after Chauri Chaura also showed Gandhi’s absolute commitment to non-violence as a moral and political principle, even at the cost of short-term political gains.

The period 1917–1922 thus laid the foundation for the subsequent phases of the national movement. It established Gandhi as the pre-eminent leader of Indian nationalism and made mass mobilisation the central feature of the struggle for independence.

## Key Facts and Points

- Champaran (1917): Abolition of tinkathia system; first application of satyagraha in India.
- Ahmedabad (1918): Mill strike; introduction of the political fast.
- Kheda (1918): Revenue suspension struggle; emergence of Vallabhbhai Patel.
- Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919): First all-India mass agitation; Jallianwala Bagh massacre.
- Non-Cooperation (1920–22): Linked with Khilafat; programme of boycott and constructive work; withdrawn after Chauri Chaura (5 February 1922).
- Gandhi’s methods combined moral pressure, mass organisation, and readiness for sacrifice while insisting on non-violence.

These early experiments shaped the strategy and ethics of the Indian national movement for the next two and a half decades.
