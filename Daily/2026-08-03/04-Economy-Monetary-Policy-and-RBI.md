# Monetary Policy and the Reserve Bank of India

## Meaning and Objectives of Monetary Policy

Monetary policy refers to the use of monetary instruments by the central bank to regulate the cost, availability and supply of money and credit in the economy. The primary objective of monetary policy in India is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. Price stability is considered a necessary precondition for sustainable growth.

In a modern economy, monetary policy influences aggregate demand through its effect on interest rates, exchange rates, asset prices and credit conditions. It also affects expectations about future inflation and growth.

## The Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934. It was nationalised in 1949. The RBI is the central bank and the monetary authority of the country. Its main functions include:

- Formulation and implementation of monetary policy.
- Regulation and supervision of banks and non-bank financial institutions.
- Management of foreign exchange reserves and the exchange rate (within the framework of a market-determined exchange rate with intervention).
- Issuer of currency.
- Banker to the government and banker to banks.
- Developmental and promotional functions, especially in the earlier decades.

The RBI is governed by a Central Board of Directors. The Governor is the chief executive. Monetary policy decisions are taken by the Monetary Policy Committee.

## Monetary Policy Framework

India formally adopted a Flexible Inflation Targeting framework in 2016. Under this framework, the RBI is mandated to maintain consumer price index (CPI) inflation at 4 per cent with a tolerance band of plus or minus 2 per cent. The target is reviewed periodically by the government in consultation with the RBI.

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) consists of six members: three from the RBI (including the Governor as Chairperson) and three external members appointed by the government. Each member has one vote, and the Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie. The MPC meets at least four times a year and announces the policy repo rate and other decisions.

## Instruments of Monetary Policy

### Quantitative or General Instruments

These instruments affect the overall supply of money and credit.

1. Repo Rate: The rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds to commercial banks against government securities. An increase in the repo rate makes borrowing from the RBI more expensive, reducing liquidity and raising interest rates in the system. A decrease has the opposite effect.

2. Reverse Repo Rate: The rate at which the RBI absorbs liquidity from banks. It provides a floor for short-term interest rates.

3. Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF): The mechanism through which the RBI conducts repo and reverse repo operations to manage day-to-day liquidity.

4. Marginal Standing Facility (MSF): A window through which banks can borrow overnight funds from the RBI at a rate higher than the repo rate, against the collateral of government securities.

5. Bank Rate: The longer-term rate at which the RBI rediscounts bills or provides advances. It is now aligned with the MSF rate and is used mainly for penal purposes.

6. Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): The proportion of net demand and time liabilities that banks must hold as cash reserves with the RBI. An increase in CRR reduces the lendable resources of banks.

7. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): The proportion of net demand and time liabilities that banks must maintain in the form of liquid assets such as government securities, cash and gold. SLR primarily serves prudential and government-borrowing purposes but also influences liquidity.

8. Open Market Operations (OMO): Purchase and sale of government securities by the RBI in the open market to inject or absorb liquidity.

### Qualitative or Selective Instruments

These instruments influence the direction of credit rather than its overall volume.

- Moral suasion: Informal persuasion by the RBI.
- Margin requirements: Prescribing the margin against which loans can be given for specific purposes.
- Credit rationing or ceilings on certain types of advances.
- Direct action against banks that violate directives.

In recent decades the RBI has relied primarily on market-based quantitative instruments, especially the repo rate and liquidity operations.

## Transmission of Monetary Policy

Monetary policy transmission refers to the process by which changes in the policy rate affect bank lending rates, deposit rates, bond yields, exchange rates and ultimately aggregate demand and inflation. Transmission in India has improved with the introduction of external benchmark-based lending rates for certain categories of loans, but it remains imperfect because of structural factors in the banking system, the large informal sector, and fiscal dominance at times.

## Challenges and Issues

- Balancing the dual objectives of price stability and growth, especially when they appear to conflict.
- Managing volatile capital flows and their impact on the exchange rate and domestic liquidity.
- Dealing with supply-side inflation (food and fuel) which is less responsive to monetary policy.
- Ensuring adequate credit flow to productive sectors while containing inflation.
- Coordination with fiscal policy; large government borrowing can put upward pressure on interest rates.
- Maintaining financial stability alongside price stability.

## Recent Evolution

The shift from a multiple-indicator approach to a formal inflation-targeting framework has increased transparency and accountability. The RBI publishes a Monetary Policy Report and provides detailed reasoning for its decisions. The framework has helped anchor inflation expectations, although episodes of high food inflation and global commodity shocks continue to pose challenges.

## Key Concepts for Retention

- Primary objective: Price stability while keeping growth in mind.
- Flexible Inflation Targeting: 4 per cent CPI inflation ± 2 per cent.
- Monetary Policy Committee: Six members, majority decision.
- Repo rate is the main policy rate.
- CRR and SLR are reserve requirements; OMO and LAF manage liquidity.
- Transmission is the link between policy rate and real economy.
- RBI is both monetary authority and banking regulator.

A sound understanding of monetary policy is essential for analysing macroeconomic conditions, inflation trends, interest rate movements and the overall policy environment in which the Indian economy operates.
