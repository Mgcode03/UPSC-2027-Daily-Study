# Parliamentary Privileges and Anti-Defection Law

## Concept of Parliamentary Privileges

Parliamentary privileges are special rights, immunities and exemptions enjoyed by the two Houses of Parliament, their committees and their members. These privileges are necessary for the Houses to discharge their functions effectively without interference from outside bodies, including the courts and the executive. Privileges protect the dignity, independence and authority of the legislature.

Privileges in India are derived from Article 105 of the Constitution for Parliament and Article 194 for State Legislatures. These articles state that the powers, privileges and immunities of each House, its members and committees shall be such as may from time to time be defined by Parliament by law, and until so defined, shall be those of the House of Commons of the United Kingdom and of its members and committees at the commencement of the Constitution.

Until Parliament defines them by law, the privileges remain those that existed in the House of Commons in 1950. In practice, Indian legislatures have not comprehensively codified privileges, leading to occasional friction with the judiciary and the media.

### Classification of Privileges

Privileges can be classified into two broad categories:

1. Privileges that belong to each House of Parliament collectively.
2. Privileges that belong to the members of each House individually.

#### Collective Privileges of the Houses

- The right to publish reports, debates and proceedings, and the right to restrain others from publishing the same. The House can prohibit the publication of its proceedings if it so decides.
- The right to exclude strangers from its proceedings and hold secret sittings when necessary.
- The right to make rules to regulate its own procedure and the conduct of its business, and to adjudicate upon such matters.
- The right to punish members as well as outsiders for breach of privilege or contempt of the House. Punishment can include admonition, reprimand, imprisonment, or suspension/expulsion of members.
- The right to receive immediate information of the arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment and release of a member.
- The right to institute inquiries and order the attendance of witnesses, and the production of papers and records.
- The right to regulate the publication of its reports and proceedings.

#### Individual Privileges of Members

- Freedom of speech in Parliament. No member is liable to any proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given by him in Parliament or any committee thereof. This is absolute and is protected by Article 105(2).
- Freedom from arrest in civil cases during the session of Parliament, and 40 days before and after each session. This does not extend to criminal cases or preventive detention.
- Exemption from jury service and appearance as a witness when Parliament is in session.
- Freedom from liability for any publication under the authority of either House of any report, paper, votes or proceedings.

### Breach of Privilege and Contempt

A breach of privilege occurs when any of the privileges of either House or its members are disregarded or attacked by an individual or authority. Contempt of the House is a broader concept. It includes any act or omission which obstructs or impedes either House in the performance of its functions, or which obstructs or impedes any member or officer of the House in the discharge of his duty, or which has a tendency, directly or indirectly, to produce such results.

Examples of contempt include:

- Speeches or writings reflecting on the character or proceedings of the House.
- Reflections on the character or impartiality of the Speaker.
- Publication of false or distorted reports of the proceedings.
- Assault on or molestation of a member within the precincts of the House.
- Bribery or attempts to influence members in their parliamentary conduct.
- Obstruction of officers of the House in the discharge of their duties.

The House itself is the sole judge of whether a particular act constitutes a breach or contempt. The courts generally do not interfere in the internal proceedings of the legislature under the doctrine of exclusive cognisance.

### Codification Debate

There has been a long-standing debate on whether parliamentary privileges should be codified. Arguments in favour of codification include greater clarity, reduced friction with the judiciary and media, and better protection of individual rights. Arguments against codification emphasise that privileges evolve with practice and that rigid statutory definition may limit the flexibility needed by the legislature. The Supreme Court has observed that privileges are subject to fundamental rights, particularly Articles 19 and 21, but the exact boundary remains a matter of constitutional interpretation.

## Anti-Defection Law

The anti-defection law was introduced by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985, which added the Tenth Schedule to the Constitution. The law was enacted to curb the evil of political defections which had become rampant after the 1967 general elections and were undermining the stability of governments and the integrity of the party system.

### Provisions of the Tenth Schedule

A member of a House belonging to any political party becomes disqualified for being a member of the House:

1. If he voluntarily gives up his membership of such political party; or
2. If he votes or abstains from voting in such House contrary to any direction issued by the political party to which he belongs or by any person or authority authorised by it in this behalf, without obtaining prior permission of such party, person or authority, and such voting or abstention has not been condoned by such party, person or authority within fifteen days from the date of such voting or abstention.

An independent member becomes disqualified if he joins any political party after the election. A nominated member becomes disqualified if he joins any political party after the expiry of six months from the date on which he takes his seat.

### Exceptions to Disqualification

The law originally provided two exceptions:

1. If a member goes out of his party as a result of a split in the party, provided that the group consists of at least one-third of the members of the legislature party. This provision was deleted by the 91st Amendment Act, 2003.
2. If a member goes out of his party as a result of a merger of the party with another party, provided that the merger is supported by at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party. This exception continues.

The 91st Amendment also added that a member who is disqualified under the Tenth Schedule shall also be disqualified from holding any remunerative political post.

### Decision-Making Authority

The question of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule is decided by the Chairman or the Speaker of the House, and his decision is final. However, the Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) held that the decision of the Speaker/Chairman is subject to judicial review. The Court also upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule while striking down the provision that made the Speaker’s decision final and non-justiciable.

### Criticisms and Issues

Several issues have arisen in the working of the anti-defection law:

- The law has been criticised for reducing the freedom of speech and vote of legislators. Members are forced to follow the party whip even on matters of conscience or public interest.
- The Speaker’s role has often been questioned on grounds of partisanship. Delays in deciding disqualification petitions have allowed defectors to enjoy the fruits of office.
- The distinction between “voluntarily giving up membership” and mere dissent is not always clear. Courts have held that the conduct of a member outside the House can also amount to voluntarily giving up membership.
- The merger provision has sometimes been used creatively to engineer mass defections while technically complying with the two-thirds requirement.
- The law does not adequately address the problem of “horse-trading” and the use of money and office to induce defections.

### Key Judicial Pronouncements

- Kihoto Hollohan case (1992): Upheld the Tenth Schedule, made Speaker’s decision subject to judicial review, and held that the law does not violate the basic structure.
- Ravi S. Naik case (1994): Held that “voluntarily giving up membership” is not synonymous with resignation and can be inferred from conduct.
- Rajendra Singh Rana case (2007): Clarified the procedure and timelines for Speaker’s decisions.
- Keisham Meghachandra Singh case (2020): Directed that Speakers should decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable period, preferably within three months.

### Significance

The anti-defection law remains an important instrument for ensuring political stability and party discipline. At the same time, it raises fundamental questions about the balance between party control and the representative character of legislators. Continuous judicial oversight and periodic legislative refinement are necessary to keep the law aligned with democratic values.

## Key Points for Retention

- Article 105 and Article 194 are the constitutional sources of privileges.
- Privileges are of two types: collective and individual.
- Freedom of speech inside the House is absolute; courts cannot question it.
- Anti-defection law is contained in the Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment.
- Disqualification arises from voluntary resignation of party membership or voting against party whip without permission.
- Merger exception requires support of two-thirds of the legislature party.
- Speaker’s decision is subject to judicial review.
- 91st Amendment deleted the split provision and added the bar on holding office after disqualification.

Understanding these concepts is essential for analysing the functioning of the Indian parliamentary system and the continuous tension between party discipline and legislative independence.
