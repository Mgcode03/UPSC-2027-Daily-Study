# Inflation Targeting and the Monetary Policy Framework in India

## What is Inflation?

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time. It erodes the purchasing power of money.

### Types of Inflation by Rate

- Creeping inflation: 1–3% per annum (generally considered healthy)
- Walking inflation: 3–10%
- Galloping inflation: 10–50% or more
- Hyperinflation: Extremely high and accelerating (e.g., Zimbabwe, Weimar Germany)

### Types by Cause

- Demand-pull inflation: Aggregate demand exceeds aggregate supply
- Cost-push inflation: Rise in costs of production (wages, raw materials, oil)
- Structural inflation: Due to structural rigidities in developing economies (supply bottlenecks, agricultural constraints)

## Measurement of Inflation in India

Two main indices:

1. **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** – Measures changes in prices of a basket of goods and services consumed by households. CPI-Combined is the headline inflation measure used for policy.
2. **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)** – Measures changes in prices at the wholesale level. Used more for industrial and wholesale transactions.

CPI has greater weight for food and fuel, making it more relevant for the common citizen. Since 2016, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) targets CPI inflation.

## Inflation Targeting Framework

In 2016, India formally adopted a Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework through an amendment to the RBI Act, 1934.

### Key Features

- The primary objective of monetary policy is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth.
- The inflation target is set by the Government of India in consultation with the RBI, once every five years.
- Current target (as notified): 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of ±2% (i.e., 2% to 6%).
- The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) is responsible for setting the policy repo rate to achieve the target.

### Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

- Constituted under the RBI Act.
- Six members: three from RBI (including the Governor as Chairperson) and three external members appointed by the Government.
- Decisions by majority; Governor has a casting vote in case of tie.
- Meets at least four times a year (usually six times).
- Publishes a Monetary Policy Report every six months.

If inflation remains outside the target band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI has to send a report to the Government explaining the reasons, remedial actions and the time within which the target would be achieved.

## Instruments of Monetary Policy

### Quantitative (General) Instruments

1. **Repo Rate** – Rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks against government securities. The main policy rate.
2. **Reverse Repo Rate** – Rate at which RBI borrows from banks.
3. **Bank Rate** – Long-term lending rate of RBI (now aligned more closely with policy rates).
4. **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** – Percentage of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that banks must keep with RBI as cash.
5. **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** – Percentage of NDTL that banks must maintain in liquid assets (gold, cash, government securities).
6. **Open Market Operations (OMO)** – Buying and selling of government securities by RBI to inject or absorb liquidity.
7. **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF)** – Repo and reverse repo operations for daily liquidity management.
8. **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** – Banks can borrow overnight from RBI at a rate higher than repo by dipping into SLR.

### Qualitative (Selective) Instruments

- Credit rationing
- Moral suasion
- Margin requirements
- Direct action

## Transmission of Monetary Policy

Changes in the policy repo rate are expected to influence lending and deposit rates of banks, which in turn affect consumption, investment and aggregate demand, thereby influencing inflation and growth.

Factors affecting transmission:
- Liquidity conditions in the banking system
- Competition among banks
- Asset quality of banks (NPAs)
- Interest rate structure (administered rates on small savings etc.)

## Challenges to Inflation Targeting in India

1. High weight of food in CPI (food inflation is often supply-driven and less responsive to monetary policy).
2. Supply-side shocks (monsoon failure, global oil prices, geopolitical events).
3. Structural issues in agriculture and logistics.
4. Fiscal dominance – large government borrowing can complicate monetary management.
5. Global financial conditions and capital flows affecting exchange rate and imported inflation.

## Other Related Concepts

- **Core Inflation** – CPI excluding food and fuel; reflects underlying demand pressures.
- **Headline Inflation** – Overall CPI.
- **Stagflation** – High inflation with stagnant growth and high unemployment.
- **Deflation** – Sustained fall in general price level (dangerous as it increases real debt burden and discourages spending).
- **Disinflation** – Slowing down of the rate of inflation.

## Historical Context

Before 2016, RBI followed a multiple-indicator approach. The Urjit Patel Committee (2014) recommended the shift to inflation targeting. The framework has broadly succeeded in anchoring inflation expectations, though food and fuel shocks continue to pose challenges.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Inflation target: 4% ± 2% (CPI)
- MPC: 6 members, majority decision, Governor casting vote
- Repo rate is the main policy rate
- Flexible Inflation Targeting adopted in 2016
- Failure to meet target for 3 consecutive quarters requires explanation to Government
- CPI is the target index, not WPI
