# Plate Tectonics and Earth Movements

## Structure of the Earth

The Earth is composed of three main layers:

1. **Crust** – Outermost solid layer. Continental crust is thicker (30–50 km) and less dense (granitic). Oceanic crust is thinner (5–10 km) and denser (basaltic).
2. **Mantle** – Extends to about 2,900 km depth. Upper mantle includes the asthenosphere, a partially molten, plastic layer on which tectonic plates move.
3. **Core** – Outer core is liquid (mainly iron-nickel); inner core is solid. Generates the Earth’s magnetic field through dynamo action.

The lithosphere (crust + uppermost rigid mantle) is broken into tectonic plates that float on the asthenosphere.

## Theory of Plate Tectonics

Plate Tectonics is the unifying theory of modern geology. It explains the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges and ocean basins.

Proposed in the 1960s, building on Alfred Wegener’s Continental Drift theory (1912) and Harry Hess’s Seafloor Spreading hypothesis (1960).

### Major Tectonic Plates

There are seven major plates and several minor ones:

- Pacific Plate (largest, mostly oceanic)
- North American Plate
- South American Plate
- Eurasian Plate
- African Plate
- Indo-Australian Plate (sometimes considered two separate plates)
- Antarctic Plate

India lies on the Indian Plate (part of Indo-Australian), which is moving northward at about 5 cm per year and colliding with the Eurasian Plate.

## Types of Plate Boundaries

### 1. Divergent Boundaries

Plates move apart. New crust is formed by magma rising from the mantle.

- Mid-ocean ridges (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge)
- Rift valleys on continents (e.g., East African Rift)

Characteristics: shallow earthquakes, volcanic activity, hydrothermal vents.

### 2. Convergent Boundaries

Plates move towards each other. Three subtypes:

**Oceanic–Continental Convergence**
- Denser oceanic plate subducts under continental plate.
- Forms oceanic trenches, volcanic arcs on the continent (e.g., Andes, Cascades).
- Examples: Nazca Plate under South American Plate.

**Oceanic–Oceanic Convergence**
- One oceanic plate subducts under another.
- Forms deep trenches and volcanic island arcs (e.g., Mariana Trench and Islands, Japanese Islands).

**Continental–Continental Convergence**
- Neither plate subducts easily because both are buoyant.
- Results in massive folding and uplift – mountain building (orogeny).
- Example: Indian Plate colliding with Eurasian Plate → Himalayas and Tibetan Plateau.

### 3. Transform Boundaries

Plates slide past each other horizontally.

- San Andreas Fault (California)
- Earthquake activity is common; little volcanic activity.

## Evidence Supporting Plate Tectonics

1. Fit of the continents (especially South America and Africa).
2. Fossil distribution (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris) across continents now separated by oceans.
3. Paleomagnetism and magnetic striping on either side of mid-ocean ridges.
4. Age of ocean floor – youngest at ridges, older farther away.
5. Distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes along plate boundaries (Ring of Fire).
6. GPS measurements showing current plate movements.

## Earth Movements

### Endogenic Forces (Internal)

Originate from within the Earth due to heat and pressure.

**Sudden Movements**
- Earthquakes
- Volcanic eruptions

**Diastrophic Movements** (slow)
- Orogenic (mountain-building) – folding and faulting
- Epeirogenic (continent-building) – uplift or subsidence of large areas

### Exogenic Forces (External)

Wear down the Earth’s surface:
- Weathering (physical, chemical, biological)
- Erosion and deposition by rivers, wind, glaciers, waves

## Folding and Faulting

**Folds** – Bends in rock layers due to compressional forces.
- Anticline (upfold)
- Syncline (downfold)
- Examples: Himalayas, Appalachians, Alps

**Faults** – Fractures along which movement has occurred.
- Normal fault (tensional forces – hanging wall moves down)
- Reverse/Thrust fault (compressional – hanging wall moves up)
- Strike-slip fault (horizontal movement)

## Indian Context

- The Himalayas are young fold mountains formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. The process is still ongoing; the Himalayas continue to rise.
- The Indo-Gangetic Plain is a foreland basin formed by sediments eroded from the rising Himalayas.
- The Deccan Plateau is a stable shield area with volcanic history (Deccan Traps – massive basalt flows around 66 million years ago, linked to the extinction of dinosaurs).
- India experiences earthquakes mainly in the Himalayan belt (Zone V and IV) due to ongoing collision, and in the peninsular region due to reactivation of old faults.

## Seismic Zones of India

Bureau of Indian Standards divides India into four seismic zones:

- Zone II – Low damage risk
- Zone III – Moderate
- Zone IV – High
- Zone V – Very high (entire Northeast, parts of Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Rann of Kutch, Andaman & Nicobar)

## Key Terms to Remember

- Lithosphere, Asthenosphere
- Subduction zone, Trench, Island arc
- Mid-ocean ridge, Rift valley
- Orogeny, Epeirogeny
- Anticline, Syncline
- Normal, Reverse, Strike-slip faults
- Ring of Fire
- Continental drift → Seafloor spreading → Plate tectonics

## Significance for UPSC

Understanding plate tectonics helps explain the location of the Himalayas, the distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes, the formation of the Indo-Gangetic plain, and the geological evolution of the Indian subcontinent. It also underpins disaster management strategies for earthquakes and related hazards.
