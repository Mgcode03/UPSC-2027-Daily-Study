# Judicial Review and Judicial Activism in the Indian Constitution

## Meaning of Judicial Review

Judicial Review is the power of the judiciary to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of both the Central and State governments. If a law or order is found to violate any provision of the Constitution, the judiciary can declare it unconstitutional and void.

This power is derived from the Constitution itself. Articles 13, 32, 131-136, 143, 226 and 246 form the basis of judicial review. Article 13 declares that all laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights shall be void. Article 32 provides the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Article 226 confers similar powers on High Courts.

The concept was borrowed from the United States Constitution, where Chief Justice John Marshall established it in Marbury v. Madison (1803). In India, the framers deliberately included provisions to ensure the supremacy of the Constitution.

## Scope of Judicial Review

Judicial Review covers three main areas:

1. Legislative actions – Acts of Parliament and State Legislatures.
2. Executive actions – Orders, notifications, rules and ordinances issued by the executive.
3. Constitutional amendments – Subject to the Basic Structure doctrine established in Kesavananda Bharati case (1973).

The judiciary examines whether the law is within the legislative competence of the enacting body (federal distribution of powers under Seventh Schedule) and whether it violates Fundamental Rights or other constitutional provisions.

## Judicial Review of Constitutional Amendments

Initially, under Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965), the Supreme Court held that constitutional amendments under Article 368 were not subject to judicial review on the ground of violation of Fundamental Rights. Golaknath (1967) reversed this and held that Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights. The 24th Amendment restored Parliament’s power. Finally, Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held that while Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, it cannot alter its Basic Structure. Subsequent cases like Minerva Mills (1980) and Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) reinforced this doctrine.

## Judicial Activism

Judicial Activism refers to the proactive role played by the judiciary in protecting the rights of citizens and promoting justice when the other two organs of government fail to do so. It involves the court going beyond the traditional role of adjudication and entering into the domain of policy-making and administration.

The term was coined by Arthur Schlesinger Jr. in 1947 in the American context. In India, it gained prominence from the late 1970s and 1980s through Public Interest Litigation (PIL).

### Evolution of PIL

PIL was pioneered by Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer. Traditional locus standi was relaxed so that any public-spirited person could approach the court on behalf of the underprivileged. Letters and even postcards were treated as petitions (epistolary jurisdiction).

Landmark PIL cases include:
- Hussainara Khatoon (1979) – Right to speedy trial
- Sunil Batra (1978-80) – Prisoners’ rights
- Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) – Bonded labour
- MC Mehta cases – Environmental protection (Ganga pollution, Oleum gas leak, Taj Trapezium)
- Vishaka (1997) – Guidelines against sexual harassment at workplace
- Vineet Narain (1998) – CBI independence and investigation of high-profile corruption

### Features of Judicial Activism

- Expansion of Fundamental Rights through interpretation (Right to Life under Article 21 includes right to livelihood, clean environment, education, health, privacy etc.)
- Issuance of guidelines and directions to fill legislative vacuum (Vishaka guidelines later became POSH Act)
- Monitoring of executive performance through continuing mandamus
- Suo motu cognisance of issues
- Use of amicus curiae and expert committees

## Judicial Restraint vs Judicial Activism

Judicial Restraint is the philosophy that courts should decide only the cases that come before them strictly according to law and should not venture into policy domains. Proponents argue that activism violates separation of powers and makes judges unelected legislators.

Critics of activism point to:
- Overreach into executive and legislative domains
- Delay in disposal of regular cases due to focus on PILs
- Inconsistency and subjectivity in some decisions
- Difficulty in implementing complex policy directions

Supporters argue that activism is necessary when other institutions fail, especially for protecting the rights of the poor, marginalised and environment.

## Important Doctrines Related to Judicial Review

1. **Doctrine of Severability** – If a part of a statute is unconstitutional, only that part is struck down if it can be separated from the rest.
2. **Doctrine of Eclipse** – A pre-constitutional law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is not void ab initio but remains eclipsed and becomes operative if the inconsistency is removed.
3. **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling** – Used in Golaknath; the new interpretation applies only to future cases.
4. **Doctrine of Colourable Legislation** – What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.
5. **Doctrine of Pith and Substance** – To determine the true nature of legislation when there is overlapping of subjects in the three lists.
6. **Doctrine of Harmonious Construction** – Conflicting provisions should be interpreted to give effect to both as far as possible.

## Key Cases on Judicial Review and Activism

- A.K. Gopalan (1950) – Narrow interpretation of Article 21
- Maneka Gandhi (1978) – Expanded Article 21; procedure must be just, fair and reasonable
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973) – Basic Structure
- Minerva Mills (1980) – Harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs; limited amending power
- S.R. Bommai (1994) – Judicial review of President’s Rule under Article 356
- I.R. Coelho (2007) – Laws under Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 open to Basic Structure review
- Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) – Right to Privacy as Fundamental Right
- Navtej Singh Johar (2018) – Decriminalisation of Section 377
- Joseph Shine (2018) – Decriminalisation of adultery

## Limitations on Judicial Review

- Political questions doctrine (though diluted in India)
- Self-imposed restraints by judiciary
- Difficulty in enforcement of certain orders
- Need for judicial accountability

## Conclusion

Judicial Review is the cornerstone of constitutionalism in India. It ensures that the Constitution remains the supreme law. Judicial Activism has played a vital role in expanding rights and ensuring accountability, but it must be exercised with caution to maintain institutional balance. The ideal is a judiciary that is neither passive nor overreaching – one that protects the Constitution while respecting the domains of the other organs.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Judicial Review based on Articles 13, 32, 226
- Basic Structure doctrine – Kesavananda Bharati 1973
- PIL pioneered by Justices Bhagwati and Krishna Iyer
- Maneka Gandhi expanded Article 21
- Doctrine of Pith and Substance for legislative competence
- Continuing mandamus in PIL cases
- Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 reviewable (I.R. Coelho)
