# Partition of India and the Achievement of Independence

## Background to Partition

The demand for Pakistan grew from the Two-Nation Theory articulated by Muslim League leaders, particularly Muhammad Ali Jinnah after 1940. The Lahore Resolution of March 1940 called for independent states in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-western and eastern zones of India.

Several factors contributed to the hardening of communal positions:

- Separate electorates introduced by the Morley-Minto Reforms (1909) and continued thereafter created political separation.
- The failure of the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) to secure agreement between Congress and League.
- Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) called by the Muslim League led to large-scale communal violence in Calcutta, followed by Noakhali and Bihar riots.
- The Interim Government formed in September 1946 faced continuous obstruction from League members.
- British desire for a quick transfer of power after World War II due to economic exhaustion and changing global opinion.

## The Mountbatten Plan

Lord Mountbatten arrived as the last Viceroy in March 1947 with a mandate to transfer power by June 1948. Communal violence and administrative breakdown forced an earlier date.

On 3 June 1947, the Mountbatten Plan (also called the 3 June Plan) was announced. Its main features were:

1. British India would be partitioned into two dominions – India and Pakistan.
2. The princely states would be free to join either dominion or remain independent (in practice, independence was not viable).
3. A Boundary Commission would demarcate the borders in Punjab and Bengal.
4. Power would be transferred on 15 August 1947.
5. The existing Constituent Assembly would continue for India; a separate one would be set up for Pakistan.
6. The Plan was accepted by Congress, Muslim League and the Sikh leadership (with reservations).

## Boundary Commission and Radcliffe Award

Sir Cyril Radcliffe headed the Boundary Commissions for Punjab and Bengal. The awards were announced on 17 August 1947, two days after independence, to avoid immediate controversy during the transfer of power.

The Radcliffe Line divided:

- Punjab into East Punjab (India) and West Punjab (Pakistan)
- Bengal into West Bengal (India) and East Bengal (Pakistan, later Bangladesh)

Controversial areas included Gurdaspur (awarded to India, providing a land link to Kashmir), Ferozepur and the Chittagong Hill Tracts. The award left large minorities on both sides and contributed to massive population transfers and violence.

## Independence of India Act, 1947

The British Parliament passed the Indian Independence Act on 18 July 1947. Key provisions:

- Two independent dominions of India and Pakistan from 15 August 1947.
- Each dominion to have a Governor-General appointed by the British Crown (Mountbatten continued for India; Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan).
- The suzerainty of the British Crown over the princely states lapsed.
- The Act provided for the division of the Indian Civil Service, army, and other assets.
- The Constituent Assemblies of both dominions were given full powers to frame their constitutions and act as legislatures.

## Integration of Princely States

At the time of independence there were 562 princely states. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Minister of States, with V.P. Menon, achieved integration through a combination of persuasion, diplomacy and, where necessary, force.

- Most states signed the Instrument of Accession by 15 August 1947, ceding defence, external affairs and communications to the Indian Union.
- Junagadh – Muslim ruler in Hindu-majority state; India intervened after a plebiscite.
- Hyderabad – Operation Polo (September 1948) ended the Nizam’s resistance.
- Kashmir – Instrument of Accession signed by Maharaja Hari Singh on 26 October 1947 after tribal invasion; led to the first Indo-Pak war.

By 1950 almost all states had been integrated, either as Part B states or merged into neighbouring provinces.

## Human Cost of Partition

Partition triggered one of the largest mass migrations in history. Estimates suggest 10–15 million people crossed the borders. Communal violence, particularly in Punjab, claimed between 200,000 and 2 million lives (estimates vary widely). Women suffered disproportionately through abduction, rape and forced conversions. Trains carrying refugees were attacked. The trauma of Partition remains a defining memory in the subcontinent’s history.

## Immediate Challenges after Independence

1. Rehabilitation of millions of refugees.
2. Integration of princely states.
3. Framing of the Constitution (completed by 26 November 1949, enforced 26 January 1950).
4. Economic disruption and food shortages.
5. Communal harmony and the assassination of Mahatma Gandhi on 30 January 1948.
6. First Indo-Pak war over Kashmir (1947–48) ending with UN-mediated ceasefire and Line of Control.

## Role of Key Leaders

- **Mahatma Gandhi** – Opposed Partition till the end; worked for communal harmony in Noakhali and Calcutta; fasted in Calcutta in September 1947 to stop violence.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** – First Prime Minister; delivered the “Tryst with Destiny” speech on the midnight of 14–15 August 1947.
- **Sardar Patel** – Architect of political integration.
- **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** – Opposed Partition; remained with India.
- **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** – Founder of Pakistan; first Governor-General.
- **Lord Mountbatten** – Last Viceroy and first Governor-General of independent India.

## Significance of 15 August 1947

Independence marked the end of nearly 200 years of British colonial rule. It established India as a sovereign democratic republic (formally from 26 January 1950) committed to secularism, democracy and social justice. The Constitution that followed reflected the ideals of the freedom struggle while providing institutional mechanisms for a diverse society.

The Partition, however, left a legacy of unresolved issues – Kashmir, river waters, minority rights and mutual suspicion – that continue to shape India–Pakistan relations.
