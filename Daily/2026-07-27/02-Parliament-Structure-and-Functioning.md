# Parliament of India: Structure and Functioning

## Constitutional Position

Parliament is the supreme legislative body of the Union of India. It is constituted under Article 79 of the Constitution, which provides that there shall be a Parliament for the Union consisting of the President and two Houses, known as the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha).

Although the President is an integral part of Parliament, he does not sit in either House. His role in the legislative process includes summoning and proroguing the Houses, dissolving the Lok Sabha, addressing Parliament, sending messages, and giving assent to Bills.

## Composition of the Rajya Sabha

The Rajya Sabha is the upper House and represents the States and Union Territories. Its maximum strength is fixed at 250, of which 238 are representatives of the States and Union Territories and 12 are nominated by the President.

The allocation of seats to States is made on the basis of population as provided in the Fourth Schedule. The representatives of each State are elected by the elected members of the Legislative Assembly of the State in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. The representatives of Union Territories are chosen in such manner as Parliament may by law prescribe.

The twelve nominated members are persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service.

The Rajya Sabha is a permanent body and is not subject to dissolution. One-third of its members retire every second year. The term of each member is six years.

The Vice-President of India is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Deputy Chairman is elected by the members of the Rajya Sabha from amongst themselves.

## Composition of the Lok Sabha

The Lok Sabha is the lower House and represents the people of India directly. Its maximum strength is 552, of which not more than 530 represent the States, not more than 20 represent the Union Territories, and not more than two members of the Anglo-Indian community could earlier be nominated by the President (this provision has been discontinued by the One Hundred and Fourth Amendment).

Members from the States are chosen by direct election on the basis of adult suffrage. The allocation of seats is based on population. Each State is divided into territorial constituencies in such manner that the ratio between the population of each constituency and the number of seats allotted to it is, so far as practicable, the same throughout the State.

The normal term of the Lok Sabha is five years from the date appointed for its first meeting. It may be dissolved earlier by the President. During a Proclamation of Emergency, the term may be extended by Parliament by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time, and not extending beyond six months after the Proclamation has ceased to operate.

The Speaker and Deputy Speaker are elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from amongst themselves. The Speaker presides over the sittings and maintains order. When the Speaker is absent, the Deputy Speaker performs the duties. The Speaker does not vote in the first instance but has a casting vote in the case of equality of votes.

## Presiding Officers and Secretariat

The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Vice-President) and the Speaker of the Lok Sabha are the principal presiding officers. They are responsible for the orderly conduct of business, interpretation of rules, and protection of the privileges of the Houses.

Each House has its own Secretariat staffed by officers and employees who are independent of the executive. The Secretary-General of each House is the principal administrative officer.

## Sessions of Parliament

The President summons each House of Parliament to meet from time to time. There must not be an interval of more than six months between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next session. In practice, Parliament meets in three sessions in a year: the Budget Session, the Monsoon Session, and the Winter Session.

A session is the period between the first sitting and the prorogation of the House. A sitting is a meeting of the House on a particular day. Adjournment suspends the sitting, while prorogation terminates the session. Dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha.

Quorum for a sitting of either House is one-tenth of the total number of members of the House.

## Legislative Procedure

### Ordinary Bills

An ordinary Bill may be introduced in either House. It passes through three readings in each House: the first reading (introduction), the second reading (consideration of principles and detailed scrutiny, often involving a Select or Joint Committee), and the third reading (final passage).

After a Bill is passed by one House, it is transmitted to the other House. If the other House passes it with amendments, it returns to the originating House. If there is disagreement, and if more than six months elapse from the date of reception of the Bill by the other House, the President may summon a joint sitting of both Houses to resolve the deadlock. At a joint sitting, the Bill is passed by a majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting.

After passage by both Houses (or by a joint sitting), the Bill is presented to the President for assent. The President may give assent, withhold assent, or return the Bill (if it is not a Money Bill) for reconsideration. If the Bill is passed again by both Houses with or without amendments and presented to the President, the President shall not withhold assent.

### Money Bills

A Money Bill is defined in Article 110. It deals exclusively with matters such as imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax; borrowing of money by the Government of India; appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India; and related financial matters.

A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the recommendation of the President. After passage by the Lok Sabha, it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha. The Rajya Sabha must return the Bill within fourteen days with its recommendations. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject all or any of the recommendations. If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within fourteen days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses.

There is no provision for a joint sitting in the case of a Money Bill. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill, and this certificate is final.

### Financial Bills

Financial Bills are of two categories. A Financial Bill under Article 117(1) contains any of the matters specified in Article 110 but does not contain only those matters. It can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the recommendation of the President, but in other respects it is treated like an ordinary Bill. A Financial Bill under Article 117(3) involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India but does not include any of the matters specified in Article 110. It can be introduced in either House, but the recommendation of the President is required at the stage of consideration.

### Constitutional Amendment Bills

Bills for amendment of the Constitution under Article 368 may be introduced in either House. They must be passed by each House by a majority of the total membership of that House and by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting. There is no provision for a joint sitting. Certain amendments also require ratification by not less than one-half of the State Legislatures.

## Devices of Parliamentary Proceedings

### Question Hour

The first hour of every sitting is generally available for the asking and answering of questions. Questions are of three types: starred (oral answers), unstarred (written answers), and short notice questions. The Question Hour is an important instrument of accountability.

### Zero Hour

The period immediately after Question Hour is known as Zero Hour. It is an Indian innovation and is not mentioned in the Rules of Procedure. Members raise matters of urgent public importance without prior notice.

### Motions

Various motions are used to raise discussions and seek decisions of the House. Important motions include the motion of thanks on the President's Address, adjournment motion, calling attention motion, no-confidence motion, censure motion, and privilege motion.

A no-confidence motion can be moved only in the Lok Sabha. If passed, the Council of Ministers must resign.

### Resolutions and Discussions

Members may move resolutions on matters of general public interest. Short duration discussions and half-hour discussions provide opportunities for focused debate.

## Parliamentary Committees

Parliamentary Committees are of two main types: Standing Committees and Ad hoc Committees.

Important Standing Committees include:

- Business Advisory Committee
- Committee on Petitions
- Committee of Privileges
- Committee on Subordinate Legislation
- Committee on Government Assurances
- Committee on Estimates
- Committee on Public Accounts
- Committee on Public Undertakings
- Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)

The Public Accounts Committee examines the accounts showing the appropriation of sums granted by Parliament and the reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General. The Estimates Committee reports on what economies, improvements in organisation, efficiency or administrative reform consistent with the policy underlying the estimates may be effected. The Committee on Public Undertakings examines the reports and accounts of public undertakings.

Departmentally Related Standing Committees examine Bills, demands for grants, and long-term policy documents related to the ministries under their jurisdiction.

## Privileges of Parliament

Each House of Parliament and its members and committees enjoy certain privileges. These include freedom of speech in Parliament, immunity from proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament, and the right of each House to regulate its own procedure and conduct of business.

The privileges are largely based on those of the British House of Commons as they existed at the commencement of the Constitution, and are also defined by law made by Parliament. Breach of privilege and contempt of the House are punishable by the House itself.

## Relationship between the Two Houses

In matters of ordinary legislation, both Houses have equal powers, subject to the joint sitting mechanism. In financial matters, the Lok Sabha has predominance. In the matter of confidence in the Council of Ministers, only the Lok Sabha has a role. The Rajya Sabha has special powers under Article 249 (legislation on State subjects in the national interest) and Article 312 (creation of All India Services).

## Key Points for Retention

- Parliament consists of the President, Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha.
- Rajya Sabha is permanent; one-third members retire every two years.
- Lok Sabha has a normal term of five years and can be dissolved.
- Money Bills can originate only in the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha has limited powers over them.
- Joint sitting is available only for ordinary Bills, not for Money Bills or Constitutional Amendment Bills.
- The Speaker certifies a Money Bill and the certificate is final.
- Parliamentary Committees play a crucial role in detailed scrutiny and accountability.
- No-confidence motion can be moved only in the Lok Sabha.
