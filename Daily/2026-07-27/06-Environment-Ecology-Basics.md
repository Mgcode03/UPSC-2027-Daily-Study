# Ecology and Environment: Foundational Concepts

## Introduction to Ecology

Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment. The term was coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866. The environment includes both biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components. Ecology examines how organisms adapt to their surroundings, how energy and nutrients flow through systems, and how populations, communities and ecosystems are structured and change over time.

Understanding ecological principles is essential for analysing environmental problems, designing conservation strategies, and appreciating the interconnectedness of natural systems that support human life.

## Levels of Organisation in Ecology

Ecological studies are conducted at several levels of organisation:

**Individual (Organism):** The basic unit. Autecology studies the relationship of an individual organism or species with its environment.

**Population:** A group of individuals of the same species living in a defined area at a given time. Population ecology examines size, density, distribution, age structure, birth and death rates, and population growth.

**Community:** An assemblage of populations of different species living and interacting in a given area. Synecology studies communities. Community structure includes species composition, diversity, dominance and trophic organisation.

**Ecosystem:** A community of organisms interacting with one another and with the abiotic components of their environment, functioning as a system. Ecosystems can be terrestrial (forests, grasslands, deserts) or aquatic (freshwater, marine).

**Biome:** A large regional ecosystem characterised by distinct climate, vegetation and animal life (e.g., tropical rainforest, temperate grassland, tundra, desert).

**Biosphere:** The global sum of all ecosystems; the zone of life on Earth, extending from the deepest ocean floors to the upper atmosphere where life exists.

## Components of an Ecosystem

### Abiotic Components

Abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical factors:

- Climatic factors: temperature, rainfall, humidity, light, wind
- Edaphic factors: soil characteristics (texture, pH, nutrients, organic matter)
- Topographic factors: altitude, slope, aspect
- Chemical factors: oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, salinity, pollutants

### Biotic Components

Biotic components are the living organisms, classified by their nutritional roles:

**Producers (Autotrophs):** Organisms that synthesise their own food from inorganic substances, primarily through photosynthesis (green plants, algae, cyanobacteria) or chemosynthesis (certain bacteria). Producers form the base of the food chain.

**Consumers (Heterotrophs):** Organisms that obtain food from other organisms. They are classified as:

- Primary consumers (herbivores): feed on producers
- Secondary consumers (carnivores): feed on herbivores
- Tertiary consumers: feed on secondary consumers
- Omnivores: feed on both plants and animals
- Detritivores: feed on dead organic matter (detritus)

**Decomposers (Saprotrophs):** Organisms (mainly bacteria and fungi) that break down dead organic matter and waste products, releasing nutrients back into the environment for reuse by producers. Decomposers complete the nutrient cycles.

## Energy Flow in Ecosystems

Energy flow is the unidirectional passage of energy through the trophic levels of an ecosystem. The ultimate source of energy for almost all ecosystems is the sun.

### Food Chains and Food Webs

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one organism eats another. There are two main types:

- Grazing food chain: begins with green plants and proceeds to herbivores and then carnivores
- Detritus food chain: begins with dead organic matter and proceeds to detritivores and their predators

In nature, food chains are interconnected to form food webs, which provide a more realistic picture of feeding relationships and greater stability to the ecosystem.

### Trophic Levels and Ecological Pyramids

Each step in a food chain represents a trophic level. Ecological pyramids represent the quantitative relationships between trophic levels:

- Pyramid of numbers: number of organisms at each trophic level
- Pyramid of biomass: total biomass at each trophic level
- Pyramid of energy: energy content at each trophic level (always upright because of energy loss at each transfer)

### The Ten Percent Law

Lindeman's Ten Percent Law states that only about ten percent of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level. The rest is lost as heat through respiration and other metabolic processes, or remains unconsumed. This limits the length of food chains and explains why top carnivores are relatively few in number.

## Nutrient Cycling (Biogeochemical Cycles)

Nutrients are cycled between the biotic and abiotic components of ecosystems. Major cycles include:

**Carbon Cycle:** Carbon is exchanged among the atmosphere (as carbon dioxide), biosphere (through photosynthesis and respiration), hydrosphere and lithosphere (as fossil fuels and carbonate rocks). Human activities, particularly the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation, have increased atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations, contributing to climate change.

**Nitrogen Cycle:** Nitrogen is abundant in the atmosphere but most organisms cannot use it directly. Nitrogen fixation (by bacteria, cyanobacteria, and industrial processes) converts atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms (ammonia, nitrates). Nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification complete the cycle. Human activities such as the use of synthetic fertilisers and the cultivation of legumes have significantly altered the nitrogen cycle.

**Phosphorus Cycle:** Phosphorus has no significant atmospheric component. It cycles through rocks, soil, water and living organisms. Phosphorus is often a limiting nutrient in aquatic ecosystems. Excess phosphorus from fertilisers and detergents contributes to eutrophication.

**Water Cycle (Hydrological Cycle):** Driven by solar energy, water evaporates, forms clouds, precipitates, and returns to water bodies through runoff and infiltration. Vegetation plays a key role in transpiration and in regulating runoff and groundwater recharge.

## Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the gradual and predictable process of change in the species structure of an ecological community over time. Succession occurs after a disturbance or on newly available substrate.

**Primary Succession:** Occurs on bare substrate where no soil exists (e.g., newly formed volcanic islands, retreating glaciers, sand dunes). Pioneer species (lichens, mosses) colonise first and contribute to soil formation. Over time, intermediate communities develop, leading eventually to a relatively stable climax community.

**Secondary Succession:** Occurs in areas where a community previously existed but has been removed by disturbance (e.g., abandoned farmland, forest clearings after fire or logging). Soil is already present, so succession proceeds more rapidly.

The climax community is determined by climate (climatic climax) and other environmental factors. Succession increases species diversity, biomass and structural complexity up to the climax stage.

## Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at all levels of organisation: genetic diversity (variation within species), species diversity (variety of species), and ecosystem diversity (variety of ecosystems and habitats).

Biodiversity is important for ecosystem stability, resilience, provision of ecosystem services (food, fibre, medicines, pollination, water purification, climate regulation), and intrinsic and cultural values.

Threats to biodiversity include habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation, invasive alien species, pollution, and climate change. Conservation strategies include protected areas (in-situ conservation), seed banks and captive breeding (ex-situ conservation), and sustainable use approaches.

India is one of the seventeen megadiverse countries of the world and contains several biodiversity hotspots (Himalaya, Western Ghats, Indo-Burma, Sundaland).

## Ecosystem Services

Ecosystem services are the benefits that humans derive from ecosystems. They are commonly classified as:

- Provisioning services: food, water, timber, fibre, genetic resources, medicines
- Regulating services: climate regulation, flood regulation, disease regulation, water purification
- Supporting services: nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production
- Cultural services: recreational, aesthetic, spiritual and educational benefits

The recognition of ecosystem services has influenced environmental policy and the development of concepts such as payment for ecosystem services and natural capital accounting.

## Key Points for Retention

- Ecology studies interactions between organisms and environment at multiple levels: individual, population, community, ecosystem, biome, biosphere.
- Ecosystems consist of abiotic and biotic (producers, consumers, decomposers) components.
- Energy flow is unidirectional; nutrient cycles are cyclic.
- Only about ten percent of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next.
- Ecological succession leads from pioneer stages to a climax community.
- Biodiversity exists at genetic, species and ecosystem levels and is essential for ecosystem function and human well-being.
- Ecosystem services are the benefits people obtain from ecosystems.
