# Medieval Indian History: The Delhi Sultanate

## Introduction to the Medieval Period

The medieval period of Indian history is conventionally dated from the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in the early thirteenth century to the decline of the Mughal Empire in the eighteenth century. This period witnessed significant political, administrative, economic, social and cultural transformations. The Delhi Sultanate represents the first major phase of this period, lasting from 1206 to 1526.

The Sultanate was not a monolithic entity. It comprised five successive dynasties: the Slave (or Mamluk) dynasty, the Khalji dynasty, the Tughlaq dynasty, the Sayyid dynasty, and the Lodi dynasty. Each dynasty brought its own administrative practices, military strategies and cultural influences, while operating within a broadly shared framework of Turko-Afghan political traditions adapted to Indian conditions.

## Background: The Ghurid Invasions

The foundations of the Delhi Sultanate were laid by the Ghurid invasions of the late twelfth century. Muizzuddin Muhammad bin Sam (Muhammad of Ghur) defeated Prithviraj Chauhan in the Second Battle of Tarain in 1192. This victory opened the Gangetic plains to Turkish conquest. Qutbuddin Aibak, a slave general of Muhammad of Ghur, consolidated control over the conquered territories. After the death of Muhammad of Ghur in 1206, Aibak assumed independent power and established the Slave dynasty.

## The Slave Dynasty (1206–1290)

### Qutbuddin Aibak (1206–1210)

Aibak established his capital at Lahore and later shifted focus to Delhi. He is known for initiating the construction of the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque and the Qutb Minar at Delhi. His reign was brief and largely occupied with consolidating authority and defending against rivals.

### Iltutmish (1211–1236)

Iltutmish is regarded as the real consolidator of the Delhi Sultanate. He shifted the capital permanently to Delhi, completed the Qutb Minar, and organised the administration on a firmer footing. He introduced the silver tanka and the copper jital as standard coins. He created a corps of Turkish nobles known as the Chalisa or Corps of Forty. He obtained formal recognition of his authority from the Abbasid Caliph, which enhanced the legitimacy of the Sultanate.

Iltutmish nominated his daughter Razia as his successor, recognising her administrative ability.

### Razia Sultan (1236–1240)

Razia was the first and only woman ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. She discarded purdah, appeared in public in male attire, and led military campaigns. Her attempts to assert authority against the Turkish nobility led to rebellion. She was defeated and killed. Her brief reign illustrated both the possibilities and the limits of female authority in a military aristocracy dominated by Turkish nobles.

### Later Slave Rulers

After Razia, a series of weak rulers followed until Ghiyasuddin Balban (1266–1287). Balban strengthened the monarchy by emphasising the divine origin of kingship (the theory of kingship based on Persian traditions), suppressing the power of the Chalisa, organising an efficient spy system, and undertaking military campaigns against the Mongols and internal rebels. He restored the prestige of the Sultanate after a period of instability.

Balban's death was followed by a brief period of confusion, ending with the seizure of power by Jalaluddin Khalji in 1290.

## The Khalji Dynasty (1290–1320)

### Jalaluddin Khalji (1290–1296)

Jalaluddin came to power at an advanced age and followed a policy of mildness. His relative Alauddin Khalji, the governor of Kara, assassinated him and seized the throne.

### Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316)

Alauddin Khalji represents the high point of the Delhi Sultanate's territorial expansion and administrative centralisation. His reign is notable for several major achievements.

**Territorial Expansion:** Alauddin conquered Gujarat, Ranthambhor, Chittor, Malwa, and parts of the Deccan. His general Malik Kafur led campaigns deep into South India, defeating the Yadavas of Devagiri, the Kakatiyas of Warangal, the Hoysalas of Dwarasamudra, and the Pandyas of Madurai. These campaigns brought immense wealth to Delhi and established a system of tributary relationships rather than direct annexation in the far south.

**Administrative and Economic Reforms:** Alauddin implemented a series of measures to strengthen the State and control the nobility. He confiscated religious endowments and free grants of land. He reorganised the revenue system by measuring land and assessing revenue on the basis of measurement. He fixed the prices of commodities in Delhi and regulated the markets through a network of officials and spies. He maintained a large standing army and introduced the system of branding of horses (dagh) and descriptive rolls of soldiers (chehra) to prevent corruption.

**Military Organisation:** The large standing army, paid in cash, enabled Alauddin to repel multiple Mongol invasions. His defensive measures included the repair and construction of forts and the maintenance of a strong frontier force.

Alauddin's death was followed by a period of weak successors, ending with the usurpation of power by Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq in 1320.

## The Tughlaq Dynasty (1320–1414)

### Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq (1320–1325)

Ghiyasuddin restored order after the chaos of the later Khaljis. He built the fortress city of Tughlaqabad. His reign ended with his death under suspicious circumstances, possibly engineered by his son Jauna Khan, who ascended the throne as Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

### Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351)

Muhammad bin Tughlaq is one of the most controversial figures in medieval Indian history. He was a man of learning and innovative ideas, but many of his experiments failed due to poor implementation and lack of practical assessment.

**Transfer of Capital:** He transferred the capital from Delhi to Devagiri (renamed Daulatabad) in the Deccan, intending to control the southern regions more effectively and to protect the capital from Mongol threats. The forced migration caused immense hardship, and the experiment was eventually abandoned.

**Token Currency:** He introduced token currency of brass and copper, intending it to circulate at the value of silver. The absence of proper control led to widespread forgery, and the experiment was withdrawn at great cost to the treasury.

**Taxation in the Doab:** Heavy taxation in the Doab region at a time of famine led to peasant rebellion and agricultural decline.

**Khurasan and Karajil Expeditions:** Ambitious military projects in the north-west and the Himalayas failed with heavy losses.

Despite these failures, Muhammad bin Tughlaq maintained a large empire for much of his reign and was a patron of scholars. His reign also saw the beginning of the disintegration of the Sultanate, with the establishment of independent kingdoms in various regions.

### Firuz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388)

Firuz Shah adopted a policy of conciliation and welfare. He reduced the severity of punishments, built canals, hospitals, rest houses and educational institutions, and established a department of charity. He revived the practice of granting hereditary assignments of land (iqta) to the nobility, which strengthened the nobles but weakened central control. He was a devout Muslim and imposed the jizya on non-Muslims more rigorously, including on Brahmins who had earlier been exempt.

After Firuz Shah, the Tughlaq dynasty declined rapidly. The invasion of Timur in 1398 devastated Delhi and accelerated the disintegration of the Sultanate.

## The Sayyid and Lodi Dynasties (1414–1526)

The Sayyid dynasty (1414–1451) ruled a much reduced territory around Delhi. The Lodi dynasty (1451–1526), of Afghan origin, attempted to restore the authority of the Sultanate. Bahlul Lodi and Sikandar Lodi expanded the territories and improved administration. Sikandar Lodi founded the city of Agra. Ibrahim Lodi faced rebellion from Afghan nobles and from the Governor of Punjab, Daulat Khan Lodi, who invited Babur to invade India. The First Battle of Panipat in 1526 ended the Delhi Sultanate and established Mughal rule.

## Administration under the Delhi Sultanate

The Sultan was the head of the State and combined supreme executive, legislative, military and often judicial authority. The theory of kingship varied: some Sultans emphasised Islamic legitimacy and the role of the Caliph, while others, particularly Balban and Alauddin, emphasised the absolute authority of the monarch.

The key central officials included the Wazir (head of the finance department), the Ariz-i-Mamalik (head of the military department), the Diwan-i-Insha (head of the royal correspondence), and the Qazi-ul-Quzat (chief judge).

The iqta system was the principal method of assigning revenue of a territory to a military officer (muqti or wali) in lieu of salary. The muqti was responsible for maintaining law and order and supplying troops. Over time, iqtas tended to become hereditary, especially under the later Tughlaqs and Lodis.

Revenue was assessed on land, and various methods of assessment and collection were employed. Alauddin's reforms based on measurement represented a significant attempt at systematisation.

## Society, Economy and Culture

The ruling class was predominantly Turkish and later Afghan, with a graded hierarchy of nobles. Indian Muslims and Hindus participated in administration at various levels, though the higher military commands remained largely with the immigrant elite for much of the period.

Agriculture remained the mainstay of the economy. Trade and urbanisation expanded, particularly under the Khaljis and Tughlaqs. Delhi became a major centre of commerce and culture.

The period saw the development of Indo-Islamic architecture, characterised by the use of arches, domes, minarets and geometric decoration, combined with indigenous techniques and motifs. The Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque, Qutb Minar, Alai Darwaza, Tughlaqabad fort, and the tombs of various Sultans are important examples.

Persian became the language of administration and high culture. Sufi orders, particularly the Chishti and Suhrawardi, played an important role in the cultural and religious life of the period and facilitated interaction between different communities.

## Key Points for Retention

- Delhi Sultanate: 1206–1526; five dynasties — Slave, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi.
- Iltutmish consolidated the Sultanate; Balban strengthened the monarchy; Alauddin expanded territory and reformed administration and markets; Muhammad bin Tughlaq undertook ambitious but largely unsuccessful experiments; Firuz Shah focused on welfare and hereditary iqtas.
- Iqta system was central to military and revenue administration.
- Alauddin's market control, dagh and chehra systems, and large standing army were distinctive features.
- Timur's invasion in 1398 severely weakened the Sultanate.
- First Battle of Panipat (1526) ended the Sultanate.
- Indo-Islamic architecture and Sufi traditions were major cultural contributions.
