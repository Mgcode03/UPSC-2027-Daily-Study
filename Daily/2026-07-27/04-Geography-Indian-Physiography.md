# Indian Physiography: Major Physiographic Divisions

## Introduction

The physiography of India is the product of a long geological history involving the northward drift of the Indian plate, its collision with the Eurasian plate, and subsequent processes of uplift, erosion, deposition and volcanic activity. India can be divided into several major physiographic divisions, each with distinctive relief, structure and drainage characteristics. Understanding these divisions is fundamental to the study of Indian geography, climate, soils, natural vegetation and human settlement patterns.

The major physiographic divisions are:

1. The Northern and North-eastern Mountains
2. The Northern Plains
3. The Peninsular Plateau
4. The Indian Desert
5. The Coastal Plains
6. The Islands

## The Northern and North-eastern Mountains

The Himalayan mountain system forms an arc of about 2,400 kilometres from the Indus to the Brahmaputra. It is the youngest and highest mountain system in the world, still undergoing uplift. The Himalayas are divided longitudinally into three parallel ranges and latitudinally into four regional divisions.

### Longitudinal Divisions

**The Greater Himalayas (Himadri):** This is the northernmost and highest range, with an average height of about 6,000 metres. It contains the highest peaks, including Mount Everest (8,848.86 metres), Kanchenjunga, Makalu, Dhaulagiri, Nanga Parbat and Nanda Devi. The Greater Himalayas are composed largely of crystalline and metamorphic rocks. They are perennially snow-bound and are the source of major rivers.

**The Lesser Himalayas (Himachal):** Lying to the south of the Greater Himalayas, this range has an average height of 3,700 to 4,500 metres. Important ranges within the Lesser Himalayas include the Pir Panjal, Dhaula Dhar, Nag Tibba and Mahabharat ranges. The Kashmir Valley, the Kangra Valley and the Kullu Valley are located in this zone. The region is known for hill stations such as Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital and Darjeeling.

**The Outer Himalayas (Shiwaliks):** This is the southernmost range, with an average height of 900 to 1,200 metres. The Shiwaliks are composed of unconsolidated sediments brought down by rivers from the main Himalayas. Between the Shiwaliks and the Lesser Himalayas lie the longitudinal valleys known as duns (e.g., Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun).

### Latitudinal Divisions

From west to east, the Himalayas are divided into:

- Punjab Himalayas (between Indus and Satluj)
- Kumaon Himalayas (between Satluj and Kali)
- Nepal Himalayas (between Kali and Tista)
- Assam Himalayas (between Tista and Dihang)

Beyond the Dihang gorge, the Himalayas bend sharply to the south and form the Eastern Hills or Purvanchal, which include the Patkai Bum, Naga Hills, Manipur Hills, Mizo Hills and the Garo-Khasi-Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya. These hills are relatively lower and are covered with dense forests.

### Significance of the Himalayas

The Himalayas act as a climatic barrier, protecting the Indian subcontinent from the cold winds of Central Asia and intercepting the monsoon winds to cause rainfall. They are the source of perennial rivers. They contain rich biodiversity, forest resources, and potential for hydropower. They have also influenced the cultural and historical evolution of the subcontinent by acting as a barrier to large-scale invasions from the north (with some important exceptions through passes).

## The Northern Plains

The Northern Plains extend from the Punjab plains in the west to the Brahmaputra valley in the east, covering a distance of about 2,400 kilometres. Their width varies from about 150 to 300 kilometres. These plains are formed by the alluvial deposits of the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra river systems and their tributaries.

### Divisions of the Northern Plains

**The Punjab Plains:** Formed by the Indus and its tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Satluj). A large part of this plain lies in Pakistan. The Indian portion is characterised by doabs (land between two rivers).

**The Ganga Plains:** Extend from the Yamuna river in the west to the Bangladesh border in the east. Major rivers include the Ganga, Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi, Son and Chambal. The plains are further subdivided into the Rohilkhand plain, Avadh plain, Bihar plain and Bengal plain.

**The Brahmaputra Plains:** Located in Assam, these plains are narrower and subject to frequent floods and river course changes due to the high sediment load and tectonic activity.

### Geomorphological Features of the Plains

**Bhabar:** A narrow belt of about 8 to 16 kilometres width along the foothills of the Shiwaliks. It consists of pebbles and coarse alluvium deposited by rivers descending from the mountains. Streams disappear underground in this belt.

**Terai:** South of the Bhabar, a wet, swampy and marshy region where the underground streams of the Bhabar re-emerge. It is densely forested and was historically malaria-infested. Much of it has been reclaimed for agriculture.

**Bhangar:** Older alluvium forming slightly elevated terraces. It is composed of calcareous concentrations known as kankar.

**Khadar:** Newer alluvium, low-lying and flooded almost every year. It is more fertile than Bhangar.

The Northern Plains are among the most fertile and densely populated regions of the world. They support intensive agriculture and are the heartland of Indian civilisation and political history.

## The Peninsular Plateau

The Peninsular Plateau is a tableland composed of old crystalline, igneous and metamorphic rocks. It is the oldest landmass of India and forms part of the ancient Gondwana land. The plateau is triangular in shape, broad in the north and tapering towards the south. It is bounded by the Aravallis in the north-west, the Delhi ridge, the Rajmahal Hills, the Shillong Plateau, and the Western and Eastern Ghats.

### Major Divisions of the Peninsular Plateau

**The Central Highlands:** Lie to the north of the Narmada river and cover a major portion of the Malwa plateau. The Aravalli Range extends from Gujarat to Delhi in a north-east to south-west direction. The Vindhyan Range and the Satpura Range lie further south. The Central Highlands are wider in the west and narrower in the east. The eastward extension of the Central Highlands is known as the Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand, and further east as the Chotanagpur Plateau.

**The Deccan Plateau:** A triangular landmass lying to the south of the Narmada. It is higher in the west and slopes gently eastwards. The Western Ghats (Sahyadris) form its western edge and are continuous, with an average elevation of 900 to 1,600 metres. Important peaks include Anai Mudi (highest in South India), Doda Betta and Makurti. The Eastern Ghats are discontinuous and lower, with average elevation of about 600 metres. They are dissected by rivers draining into the Bay of Bengal. The Deccan Plateau is drained by the Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi and other rivers.

**The North-eastern Plateau:** An extension of the main Peninsular Plateau, it includes the Meghalaya Plateau (Garo, Khasi and Jaintia Hills), the Karbi Anglong Plateau and the North Cachar Hills. The Meghalaya Plateau is rich in mineral resources and receives very high rainfall.

### Characteristics and Significance

The Peninsular Plateau is rich in minerals, including coal, iron ore, manganese, bauxite and mica. Black soils (regur) of the Deccan are highly suitable for cotton cultivation. The plateau has a complex drainage pattern with both west-flowing (Narmada, Tapi) and east-flowing rivers. The Western Ghats influence the distribution of monsoon rainfall.

## The Indian Desert

The Thar Desert, or the Great Indian Desert, lies to the north-west of the Aravalli Range. It is a region of undulating topography covered with sand dunes and barchans. Rainfall is very low (below 150 mm per year in the western parts), and the climate is extreme. The Luni is the only large river in this region; most streams are ephemeral. The desert is believed to be expanding, and various afforestation and stabilisation measures have been undertaken.

## The Coastal Plains

India has a long coastline of about 7,516.6 kilometres, including the islands. The coastal plains are divided into the Western Coastal Plains and the Eastern Coastal Plains.

### Western Coastal Plains

These are narrow plains lying between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea. They are divided into the Konkan Coast (Mumbai to Goa), the Kannad Coast (central part), and the Malabar Coast (Kerala). The western coast has estuaries, creeks and backwaters (kayals) in Kerala. The ports on this coast are generally natural harbours.

### Eastern Coastal Plains

These are broader plains lying between the Eastern Ghats and the Bay of Bengal. They are divided into the Northern Circars (north of the Godavari) and the Coromandel Coast (south of the Godavari). Large deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri are important features. Chilika Lake (Odisha) is the largest saltwater lake in India. The eastern coast has fewer natural harbours; Chennai is an artificial harbour.

## The Islands

**The Andaman and Nicobar Islands:** A group of 572 islands in the Bay of Bengal, extending from north to south. They are volcanic and tectonic in origin. The Andamans are separated from the Nicobars by the Ten Degree Channel. Indira Point in the Great Nicobar is the southernmost point of India.

**The Lakshadweep Islands:** A group of 36 islands in the Arabian Sea, of coral origin. They are grouped into the Amindivi Islands (north), the Laccadive Islands (central), and Minicoy Island (south). The islands are known for their coral atolls and lagoon formations.

## Key Points for Retention

- Himalayas: three longitudinal ranges — Greater, Lesser, Outer (Shiwaliks); still rising; climatic and riverine significance.
- Northern Plains: Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar; formed by Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra alluvium; highly fertile and densely populated.
- Peninsular Plateau: oldest landmass; Central Highlands and Deccan Plateau; rich in minerals; Western Ghats continuous and higher, Eastern Ghats discontinuous.
- Thar Desert: rain shadow of Aravallis; sand dunes; ephemeral streams.
- Western Coast: narrow, with estuaries and backwaters; Eastern Coast: broader, with large deltas.
- Andaman-Nicobar: tectonic/volcanic; Lakshadweep: coral.
