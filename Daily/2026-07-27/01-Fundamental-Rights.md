# Fundamental Rights

## Introduction to Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights are a set of rights guaranteed by the Constitution of India to all persons (in some cases only to citizens) against the State. They are enshrined in Part III of the Constitution, covering Articles 12 to 35. These rights are considered fundamental because they are essential for the all-round development of the individual and for the realisation of the ideals of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity set out in the Preamble.

The concept of Fundamental Rights draws inspiration from the Bill of Rights in the American Constitution, the Declaration of the Rights of Man in the French Constitution, and the Irish Constitution. In the Indian context, they serve as a limitation on the powers of the State and protect individuals from arbitrary state action.

Fundamental Rights are not absolute. They are subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by the State in the interest of sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, morality, and other grounds specified in the Constitution itself. The balance between individual liberty and social interest is a recurring theme in the interpretation of these rights by the Supreme Court.

## Article 12: Definition of State

Article 12 defines the term "State" for the purposes of Part III. The State includes:

- The Government and Parliament of India
- The Government and the Legislature of each of the States
- All local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India

The Supreme Court has given a wide interpretation to the term "other authorities". It includes bodies created by statute, bodies that perform public functions, and even private bodies performing public duties under certain circumstances. The test of instrumentality or agency of the State has been developed through case law to determine whether a body falls within the meaning of State under Article 12.

This wide definition is important because Fundamental Rights are primarily enforceable against the State. Actions of private individuals are generally not covered, except in limited cases such as Article 15(2), Article 17 and Article 23.

## Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Article 13 declares that all laws in force in the territory of India immediately before the commencement of the Constitution, in so far as they are inconsistent with the provisions of Part III, shall to the extent of such inconsistency be void. It also provides that the State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III, and any law made in contravention of this clause shall to the extent of the contravention be void.

The term "law" includes ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law. Constitutional amendments were initially held to be outside the scope of Article 13, but after the Kesavananda Bharati case, the basic structure doctrine has placed limitations on the amending power itself.

Article 13 thus establishes the supremacy of Fundamental Rights over ordinary laws and provides the foundation for judicial review of legislative and executive actions.

## Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18)

### Article 14: Equality before Law and Equal Protection of Laws

Article 14 states that the State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.

"Equality before the law" is a negative concept implying the absence of any special privilege in favour of any individual. It is of English origin and means that no person is above the law and every person, whatever his rank or condition, is subject to the jurisdiction of ordinary courts.

"Equal protection of the laws" is a positive concept of American origin. It means equality of treatment under equal circumstances. It permits classification, but such classification must be reasonable. The test of reasonable classification requires two conditions:

1. The classification must be founded on an intelligible differentia which distinguishes persons or things that are grouped together from others left out of the group.
2. The differentia must have a rational relation to the object sought to be achieved by the statute.

Article 14 has been interpreted to include the principle of non-arbitrariness. Any action of the State that is arbitrary is violative of Article 14. This has expanded the scope of the Article considerably.

### Article 15: Prohibition of Discrimination

Article 15(1) prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them.

Article 15(2) extends the prohibition to access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, and the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to the use of the general public.

Article 15(3) permits the State to make special provisions for women and children.

Article 15(4), inserted by the First Amendment, enables the State to make special provisions for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes.

Article 15(5), inserted by the Ninety-third Amendment, allows special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes or Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes in relation to admission to educational institutions, including private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided by the State, other than minority educational institutions.

Article 15(6), inserted by the One Hundred and Third Amendment, provides for special provisions for the advancement of any economically weaker sections of citizens other than the classes mentioned in clauses (4) and (5).

### Article 16: Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

Article 16(1) guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to any office under the State.

Article 16(2) prohibits discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence or any of them.

Article 16(3) permits Parliament to prescribe residence within a State as a condition for certain appointments.

Article 16(4) enables the State to make provisions for the reservation of appointments or posts in favour of any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in the services under the State.

Article 16(4A) provides for reservation in matters of promotion with consequential seniority to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Article 16(4B) deals with unfilled reserved vacancies being carried forward.

Article 16(5) saves laws relating to religious or denominational institutions.

Article 16(6) provides for reservation of appointments or posts in favour of any economically weaker sections of citizens.

The concept of creamy layer, the fifty per cent ceiling on reservations, and the distinction between adequacy of representation and proportionate representation have all been developed through judicial interpretation of Article 16.

### Article 17: Abolition of Untouchability

Article 17 abolishes "Untouchability" and forbids its practice in any form. The enforcement of any disability arising out of Untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law.

The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally the Untouchability Offences Act) and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 give effect to this constitutional mandate.

### Article 18: Abolition of Titles

Article 18 prohibits the State from conferring any title except military or academic distinctions. No citizen of India shall accept any title from any foreign State. No person holding any office of profit or trust under the State shall, without the consent of the President, accept any present, emolument or office of any kind from or under any foreign State.

## Right to Freedom (Articles 19 to 22)

### Article 19: Six Freedoms

Article 19(1) guarantees to all citizens six fundamental freedoms:

1. Freedom of speech and expression
2. Freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms
3. Freedom to form associations or unions or cooperative societies
4. Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India
5. Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India
6. Freedom to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business

These freedoms are not absolute. Clauses (2) to (6) of Article 19 permit the State to impose reasonable restrictions on these freedoms on specified grounds. The reasonableness of restrictions is justiciable, and the courts examine whether the restriction is proportionate to the object sought to be achieved.

Freedom of speech and expression has been interpreted to include freedom of the press, the right to know, the right to information, the right to silence, and commercial speech, among others. The test of clear and present danger or the balancing of interests is applied in determining the validity of restrictions.

### Article 20: Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

Article 20 provides three protections:

1. No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence (protection against ex post facto laws).
2. No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once (protection against double jeopardy).
3. No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself (protection against self-incrimination).

These protections are available to all persons, citizens and non-citizens alike, and apply only in criminal proceedings.

### Article 21: Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

Initially interpreted narrowly in A.K. Gopalan, the scope of Article 21 expanded dramatically after Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India. The procedure established by law must be fair, just and reasonable, and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. The right to life has been interpreted to include the right to live with human dignity, and a large number of unenumerated rights have been read into Article 21, including:

- Right to livelihood
- Right to shelter
- Right to health
- Right to clean environment
- Right to education (later made explicit as Article 21A)
- Right to privacy
- Right to free legal aid
- Right against solitary confinement
- Right to speedy trial
- Right against handcuffing
- Right to dignity of the dead

The expansion of Article 21 represents one of the most significant developments in Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

### Article 21A: Right to Education

Article 21A, inserted by the Eighty-sixth Amendment, provides that the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the State may, by law, determine. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 gives effect to this right.

### Article 22: Protection against Arrest and Detention

Article 22 provides procedural safeguards against arbitrary arrest and detention. It has two parts:

1. Rights of persons arrested under ordinary law: right to be informed of the grounds of arrest, right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, right to be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours, and prohibition of detention beyond twenty-four hours without magisterial authority.
2. Rights of persons detained under preventive detention laws: the maximum period of detention without obtaining the opinion of an advisory board is three months (subject to exceptions), the grounds of detention must be communicated as soon as possible, and the detenu must be given the earliest opportunity of making a representation.

Preventive detention is a unique feature of the Indian Constitution and has been the subject of considerable debate and judicial scrutiny.

## Right against Exploitation (Articles 23 and 24)

### Article 23: Prohibition of Traffic in Human Beings and Forced Labour

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour. Any contravention is an offence punishable in accordance with law. The State may impose compulsory service for public purposes, but in imposing such service no discrimination shall be made on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class or any of them.

### Article 24: Prohibition of Employment of Children

Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below the age of fourteen years in any factory or mine or in any other hazardous employment. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act and subsequent amendments give effect to this provision.

## Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25 to 28)

### Article 25: Freedom of Conscience and Free Profession, Practice and Propagation of Religion

Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health, and to the other provisions of Part III. The State may regulate or restrict any economic, financial, political or other secular activity associated with religious practice, and may provide for social welfare and reform or the throwing open of Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus.

### Article 26: Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

Subject to public order, morality and health, every religious denomination or any section thereof has the right to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes, to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, to own and acquire movable and immovable property, and to administer such property in accordance with law.

### Article 27: Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of any Religion

No person shall be compelled to pay any taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

### Article 28: Freedom as to Attendance at Religious Instruction

No religious instruction shall be provided in any educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds. In institutions administered by the State but established under any endowment or trust requiring religious instruction, such instruction may be provided. Persons attending State-recognised or State-aided institutions cannot be required to take part in religious instruction or attend religious worship without consent.

## Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29 and 30)

### Article 29: Protection of Interests of Minorities

Any section of the citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script or culture of its own shall have the right to conserve the same. No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them.

### Article 30: Right of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions

All minorities, whether based on religion or language, shall have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The State shall not, in granting aid to educational institutions, discriminate against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority, whether based on religion or language.

The interpretation of minority rights, particularly the extent of autonomy in administration and the applicability of regulatory measures, has been the subject of extensive litigation.

## Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III. The Supreme Court has the power to issue directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate.

Article 32 has been described as the heart and soul of the Constitution by Dr B.R. Ambedkar. It is itself a Fundamental Right. The High Courts have concurrent jurisdiction under Article 226 to issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as for any other purpose.

Public Interest Litigation has expanded access to justice under Article 32 and Article 226, allowing any public-spirited person to approach the courts on behalf of those who are unable to do so themselves.

## Suspension and Restriction of Fundamental Rights

During a Proclamation of Emergency under Article 352, the rights guaranteed by Article 19 are automatically suspended. The President may also suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of other Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 and 21 after the Forty-fourth Amendment) by an order under Article 359.

Article 33 permits Parliament to modify the application of Fundamental Rights to the armed forces, police forces and similar forces. Article 34 deals with restriction of rights while martial law is in force. Article 35 confers power on Parliament to make laws for giving effect to certain provisions of Part III.

## Key Points for Retention

- Fundamental Rights are justiciable and enforceable against the State as defined in Article 12.
- Article 14 is the foundation of the equality code and includes non-arbitrariness.
- Reasonable classification is permitted under Article 14, but class legislation is not.
- Article 19 freedoms are available only to citizens; Articles 14, 20, 21 and 22 are available to all persons.
- Article 21 has become a reservoir of unenumerated rights through expansive interpretation.
- Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and the primary mechanism for enforcement.
- Fundamental Rights are subject to reasonable restrictions and can be amended, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

This completes the foundational treatment of Fundamental Rights. The detailed case law and contemporary applications form part of advanced study in subsequent modules.
