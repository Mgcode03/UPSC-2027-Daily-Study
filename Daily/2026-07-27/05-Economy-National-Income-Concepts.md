# National Income: Concepts and Measurement

## Introduction

National income is a measure of the monetary value of the flow of goods and services produced in an economy over a period of time, usually a year. It is a central concept in macroeconomics and provides a quantitative basis for assessing the economic performance of a country, comparing living standards over time and across countries, and formulating economic policy.

In India, national income estimates are prepared by the National Statistical Office (NSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The estimates are available at both current prices and constant prices. Constant price estimates (real national income) eliminate the effect of price changes and allow comparison of real output over time.

## Key Aggregates of National Income

### Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country in a given period. Domestic territory includes the political frontiers, territorial waters, ships and aircraft operated by residents, and embassies and consulates located abroad.

GDP includes the production of both residents and non-residents within the domestic territory. It excludes intermediate goods to avoid double counting and includes only final goods and services.

GDP can be measured at market prices (including indirect taxes net of subsidies) or at factor cost (excluding net indirect taxes). In the present system of national accounts, the preferred aggregate is GDP at market prices.

### Gross National Product (GNP)

GNP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced by the residents of a country in a given period, regardless of where the production takes place. It is related to GDP by the following identity:

GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)

Net Factor Income from Abroad is the difference between factor income earned by residents from abroad and factor income earned by non-residents within the domestic territory. Factor income includes wages, rent, interest and profits.

When NFIA is positive, GNP is greater than GDP. When NFIA is negative (as has often been the case for India), GNP is less than GDP.

### Net Domestic Product (NDP) and Net National Product (NNP)

Gross aggregates include depreciation (consumption of fixed capital). Net aggregates exclude depreciation:

NDP = GDP − Depreciation

NNP = GNP − Depreciation

NNP at factor cost is often referred to as national income in a narrow sense, because it represents the sum of factor incomes earned by the residents of the country.

### National Income at Factor Cost and at Market Prices

National income at factor cost = NNP at market prices − Net Indirect Taxes

Net Indirect Taxes = Indirect Taxes − Subsidies

National income at factor cost measures the income earned by factors of production. National income at market prices measures the value of output at the prices paid by final consumers.

### Personal Income and Disposable Income

Personal income is the income received by households and non-corporate businesses. It is derived from national income by subtracting undistributed corporate profits, corporate taxes and social security contributions, and adding transfer payments (such as pensions, unemployment benefits and interest on public debt).

Personal disposable income is personal income minus personal taxes. It is the income available to households for consumption and saving.

## Methods of Measuring National Income

There are three alternative methods of measuring national income, which should in principle yield the same result.

### Product Method (Value Added Method)

This method estimates the contribution of each producing unit by calculating the value added by that unit. Value added is the difference between the value of output and the value of intermediate consumption. Summing the value added of all producing units in the domestic territory gives GDP at market prices.

The economy is divided into sectors (primary, secondary, tertiary, or more detailed industrial classifications), and value added is estimated for each sector.

### Income Method

This method sums the factor incomes earned in the production process: compensation of employees (wages and salaries), rent, interest, profits (including mixed income of self-employed), and in some presentations, the consumption of fixed capital and net indirect taxes. The sum of factor incomes gives national income at factor cost.

### Expenditure Method

This method sums the final expenditures on the goods and services produced. The main components are:

- Private final consumption expenditure
- Government final consumption expenditure
- Gross fixed capital formation (investment in fixed assets)
- Change in stocks (inventory investment)
- Net exports (exports minus imports)

The sum of these expenditures gives GDP at market prices.

In practice, data limitations mean that the three methods do not always produce identical results, and a statistical discrepancy is often shown in the accounts.

## Real and Nominal National Income

Nominal national income is measured at current prices. Real national income is measured at constant prices of a base year. The conversion is done using price indices (deflators). The GDP deflator is a commonly used price index for this purpose:

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

Real GDP growth is the most widely used indicator of economic growth.

## Per Capita Income

Per capita national income is national income divided by the population. It is a rough indicator of the average standard of living, though it does not capture distribution of income, non-market production, environmental costs, or quality of life factors. Per capita income at constant prices is used to track changes in average living standards over time.

## Limitations of National Income as a Measure of Welfare

National income and per capita income have several limitations as measures of economic welfare:

- They do not account for the distribution of income. A high average may conceal large inequalities.
- They exclude non-market activities such as household work and voluntary work.
- They do not deduct the costs of environmental degradation and resource depletion.
- They include expenditure on regrettable necessities such as defence and commuting costs that do not directly enhance welfare.
- They do not capture leisure, quality of life, health and education outcomes, or social cohesion.
- The underground or black economy is only partially captured.

Alternative measures such as the Human Development Index (HDI), Green GDP, and various happiness or well-being indices have been developed to supplement national income statistics.

## National Income Accounting in India

India has a long tradition of national income estimation, beginning with the work of Dadabhai Naoroji in the nineteenth century and later systematic estimates by the National Income Committee (1949) under P.C. Mahalanobis, D.R. Gadgil and V.K.R.V. Rao.

Currently, the National Statistical Office releases annual and quarterly estimates of GDP and related aggregates. The base year is revised periodically to reflect structural changes in the economy. The current series uses a recent base year and incorporates improvements in coverage and methodology, including the use of the MCA21 database for the corporate sector and improved estimates for the unorganised sector.

Sectoral composition of GDP shows the rising share of the tertiary (services) sector and the declining share of the primary sector over the decades, consistent with the pattern of structural transformation in a developing economy.

## Key Points for Retention

- GDP measures production within domestic territory; GNP measures production by residents.
- GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad.
- Net aggregates exclude depreciation; factor cost aggregates exclude net indirect taxes.
- Three methods of measurement: product (value added), income, and expenditure.
- Real GDP (at constant prices) measures real growth; nominal GDP is at current prices.
- Per capita income is an average and does not reflect distribution or non-income dimensions of welfare.
- National income has limitations as a welfare measure; supplementary indicators are necessary.
