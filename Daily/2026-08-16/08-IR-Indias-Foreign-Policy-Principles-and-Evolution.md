# India’s Foreign Policy: Principles and Evolution

## Foundational Principles

India’s foreign policy has been shaped by a set of principles that emerged during the freedom struggle and the early years of independence. These include:

**Non-alignment**: The refusal to join military blocs led by the major powers of the Cold War. Non-alignment was not neutrality; it was an assertion of independence in foreign policy and the right to judge each issue on its merits.

**Panchsheel**: The five principles of peaceful coexistence — mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty, mutual non-aggression, mutual non-interference in internal affairs, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence. These principles were articulated in the context of relations with China and became a wider normative reference.

**Anti-colonialism and Anti-racialism**: Support for decolonisation movements and opposition to racial discrimination, particularly apartheid, reflected the historical experience of colonial rule and the moral stance of the freedom movement.

**Promotion of International Peace and Cooperation**: Faith in the United Nations and in multilateral approaches to global problems.

## Evolution through Phases

In the Nehruvian period, non-alignment, Afro-Asian solidarity and a moral-diplomatic style were prominent. The 1962 conflict with China and the 1965 and 1971 wars with Pakistan tested and modified certain assumptions. The Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with the Soviet Union in 1971 reflected a pragmatic shift while formal non-alignment was retained.

The end of the Cold War, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and the economic reforms of 1991 created a new context. India moved towards closer engagement with the United States, improved relations with Israel, and a more pragmatic approach to economic diplomacy. The Look East Policy (later Act East) signalled greater attention to Southeast and East Asia.

In the twenty-first century, India’s foreign policy has been characterised by multi-alignment or issue-based alignment, greater emphasis on economic and technological dimensions, active participation in new groupings such as BRICS, SCO and the Quad, and a more assertive articulation of national interests. The rise of China, the evolving Indo-Pacific concept, and global issues such as climate change and terrorism have shaped priorities.

## Continuity and Change

Certain elements show continuity: the preference for strategic autonomy, the importance attached to the neighbourhood, the commitment to multilateralism, and the use of soft power resources such as culture and diaspora. Changes are visible in the greater weight given to economic interests, the willingness to enter into security partnerships short of alliances, and the more proactive style of diplomacy.

## Neighbourhood and Extended Neighbourhood

Relations with immediate neighbours remain central. The neighbourhood first policy reflects the recognition that instability or hostility in the neighbourhood constrains India’s broader ambitions. Relations with Pakistan continue to be dominated by the legacy of Partition, territorial disputes and cross-border terrorism. Relations with China combine elements of cooperation, competition and contestation. Engagement with other South Asian countries, the Indian Ocean region, and the extended neighbourhood of Southeast Asia, West Asia and Africa has expanded in scope and intensity.

## Conceptual Points

- Non-alignment was a strategy of autonomy rather than isolation or equidistance.
- Foreign policy principles provide normative guidance but are applied in the light of changing capabilities and circumstances.
- Strategic autonomy remains a core objective even as partnerships multiply.
- Economic growth and technological capability have become more central to foreign policy calculation.
- The management of relations with major powers and with neighbours simultaneously is a persistent challenge.

A conceptual understanding of the principles and evolution of India’s foreign policy is necessary for analysing contemporary international relations questions and for situating current events within a longer historical and strategic perspective.
