# Banking Sector Reforms and Financial Stability in India

## Evolution of the Indian Banking System

The modern Indian banking system has its roots in the colonial period, with the establishment of the three Presidency Banks that later merged to form the Imperial Bank of India. After independence, the Reserve Bank of India was nationalised in 1949 and the Imperial Bank became the State Bank of India in 1955. A major wave of bank nationalisation occurred in 1969 (fourteen major commercial banks) and another in 1980 (six more banks). The objective was to extend banking to rural and neglected sectors and to align credit allocation with developmental priorities.

By the late 1980s the banking system suffered from low profitability, high non-performing assets, directed lending pressures and limited competition. The balance of payments crisis of 1991 provided the context for comprehensive economic reforms, of which banking sector reform was a central component.

## Narasimham Committee Recommendations

The Committee on the Financial System (Narasimham Committee I, 1991) and the Committee on Banking Sector Reforms (Narasimham Committee II, 1998) laid down the roadmap for reform. Key recommendations included:

- Reduction of statutory pre-emptions (Cash Reserve Ratio and Statutory Liquidity Ratio) to release resources for commercial lending.
- Introduction of prudential norms relating to income recognition, asset classification and provisioning.
- Capital adequacy requirements aligned with international standards (Basel norms).
- Greater competition through entry of new private sector banks and foreign banks.
- Improvement in the governance and autonomy of public sector banks.
- Creation of asset reconstruction companies to deal with non-performing assets.
- Strengthening of the supervisory framework of the Reserve Bank of India.

Many of these recommendations were implemented in phases through the 1990s and 2000s.

## Key Reform Measures and Their Logic

Prudential norms required banks to classify assets as standard, sub-standard, doubtful or loss and to make provisions accordingly. This brought transparency to the true quality of bank portfolios. Capital adequacy norms required banks to maintain capital in proportion to risk-weighted assets, thereby linking capital to the riskiness of the business.

The entry of new private banks in the mid-1990s introduced competition, technology and better customer service. Public sector banks were given greater operational flexibility while remaining under government ownership. The creation of debt recovery tribunals and later the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest (SARFAESI) Act improved the legal framework for recovery of dues.

## Basel Norms and Capital Regulation

India adopted Basel I norms in the 1990s and moved to Basel II in the following decade. Basel III, which emphasises higher quality capital, leverage ratios and liquidity standards, has been implemented in phases. The objective is to ensure that banks hold sufficient high-quality capital to absorb losses and continue functioning even under stress.

## Non-Performing Assets and Resolution Mechanisms

Non-performing assets (NPAs) have been a recurring challenge. Periods of rapid credit growth, especially to infrastructure and corporate sectors, were followed by stress when projects faced delays or economic conditions deteriorated. The Asset Quality Review conducted by the Reserve Bank in 2015 revealed the true extent of stressed assets and led to higher recognition of NPAs.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 provided a time-bound framework for resolution of stressed assets. It shifted the balance of power towards creditors and introduced the possibility of liquidation if resolution failed. The Code has become a central instrument of financial stability policy.

## Financial Stability: Concept and Institutions

Financial stability refers to a condition in which the financial system is able to withstand shocks without major disruption to the intermediation process and the real economy. It involves the soundness of individual institutions, the resilience of markets, and the robustness of the payment and settlement infrastructure.

The Reserve Bank of India is the primary institution responsible for financial stability in India. It conducts macro-prudential surveillance, publishes the Financial Stability Report, and uses tools such as capital buffers, provisioning norms and liquidity requirements. The Financial Stability and Development Council provides a high-level forum for coordination among regulators.

## Conceptual Points

- Banking reform in India has been gradual and sequenced rather than a single big-bang event.
- Prudential regulation aims to ensure that banks price risk correctly and maintain adequate capital.
- Ownership structure (public versus private) interacts with governance and incentives in complex ways.
- Resolution of stressed assets is as important as prevention for maintaining stability.
- Financial stability is a public good that requires continuous monitoring and adaptive regulation.

Understanding the logic of banking reforms and the concept of financial stability is essential for analysing monetary policy transmission, credit growth, fiscal-banking linkages and the overall resilience of the Indian economy.
