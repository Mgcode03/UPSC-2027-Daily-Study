# Indian River Systems and Water Resources: Conceptual Framework

## Classification of Indian Rivers

Indian rivers are broadly classified into Himalayan rivers and Peninsular rivers on the basis of their origin, nature of flow and hydrological characteristics.

Himalayan rivers originate in the snow-clad ranges of the Himalayas and the trans-Himalayan region. They are perennial because they receive water from both rainfall and the melting of snow and glaciers. Major Himalayan river systems include the Indus, the Ganga and the Brahmaputra.

Peninsular rivers originate in the Peninsular plateau and the central highlands. Most of them are seasonal or rain-fed, with flow concentrated in the monsoon months. Important Peninsular systems include the Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery, Mahanadi, Narmada and Tapi.

## The Indus System

The Indus rises near Lake Mansarovar in Tibet. It flows northwestward through the Ladakh region before entering Pakistan. Its major left-bank tributaries in India are the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej, collectively known as the Punjab rivers. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 between India and Pakistan governs the sharing of the waters of this system. Under the treaty, the waters of the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) are allocated primarily to India, while the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) are allocated primarily to Pakistan, with certain rights retained by India for non-consumptive uses.

## The Ganga System

The Ganga is formed by the confluence of the Bhagirathi and Alaknanda at Devprayag. It flows through the plains of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and West Bengal before entering Bangladesh. Major tributaries include the Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi and Son. The Yamuna itself is a major river with its own system of tributaries such as the Chambal, Betwa and Ken.

The Ganga basin is the most densely populated and intensively cultivated river basin in India. It supports a large proportion of the country’s population and agricultural output. The river is also of immense cultural and religious significance.

## The Brahmaputra System

The Brahmaputra originates in the Chemayungdung glacier near Mansarovar and flows eastward through Tibet as the Tsangpo. It enters India in Arunachal Pradesh, where it is known as the Siang or Dihang, and then flows through Assam as the Brahmaputra. It receives numerous tributaries from the Himalayas and the hills of the Northeast. The river is characterised by high volume, heavy sediment load and frequent channel shifts, giving rise to the distinctive floodplain and riverine island morphology of Assam.

## Peninsular River Systems

The Godavari is the largest Peninsular river. It rises in the Western Ghats near Nashik and flows eastward across the Deccan to the Bay of Bengal. Its major tributaries include the Penganga, Wardha, Wainganga, Indravati and Manjra.

The Krishna rises near Mahabaleshwar and also flows eastward. Important tributaries are the Bhima, Tungabhadra and Musi. The Cauvery rises in the Western Ghats of Karnataka and flows through Tamil Nadu. Its basin is the site of long-standing inter-state water disputes.

The Narmada and Tapi are unique among major Peninsular rivers in that they flow westward into the Arabian Sea. They occupy rift valleys and have relatively straight courses with limited tributaries.

The Mahanadi rises in Chhattisgarh and flows through Odisha. Its delta is one of the most fertile in eastern India.

## Water Resources: Conceptual Categories

Water resources may be classified as surface water and groundwater. Surface water includes rivers, lakes, reservoirs and wetlands. Groundwater is stored in aquifers of varying depth and yield.

India receives an average annual precipitation of about 4000 billion cubic metres. After accounting for evaporation and other losses, the utilisable water resource is estimated at roughly 1123 billion cubic metres, of which a significant portion is surface water and the remainder groundwater.

## Issues in Water Resource Management

Spatial and temporal variability of rainfall creates regions of surplus and deficit. Inter-state river water disputes arise because river basins cut across political boundaries. The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act provides a mechanism for adjudication, but resolution has often been slow and politically contested.

Over-extraction of groundwater in many parts of the country has led to declining water tables, particularly in the northwest and in hard-rock areas of the peninsula. Quality issues include salinity, fluoride, arsenic and nitrate contamination in different regions.

Floods and droughts remain recurrent challenges. Flood management involves structural measures such as embankments and reservoirs as well as non-structural measures such as forecasting, zoning and insurance. Drought management requires both relief measures and long-term strategies of water conservation and livelihood diversification.

## Key Conceptual Points

- Himalayan rivers are perennial; most Peninsular rivers are seasonal.
- River basins are natural hydrological units that often transcend state boundaries, creating the need for cooperative federal arrangements.
- The distinction between water availability and utilisable water is crucial for planning.
- Groundwater and surface water are interconnected; sustainable management requires an integrated approach.
- Cultural, economic and ecological values of rivers must be considered alongside their use as sources of irrigation, hydropower and domestic supply.

Understanding the geography of Indian rivers and the conceptual framework of water resources is fundamental to analysing agriculture, regional development, inter-state relations, environmental sustainability and disaster management in India.
