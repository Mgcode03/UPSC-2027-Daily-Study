# Forest Resources and Forest Policy in India

## Forest Cover and Classification

Forests in India are classified on the basis of canopy density into very dense forest, moderately dense forest and open forest. The Forest Survey of India periodically assesses forest cover using satellite data and field verification. Forest cover is distinguished from tree cover outside recorded forest areas.

Recorded forest area includes reserved forests, protected forests and unclassed forests as notified under forest law. Actual forest cover may differ from recorded forest area because of encroachment, degradation or, conversely, regeneration and afforestation outside recorded areas.

## Ecological and Economic Significance

Forests perform multiple ecological functions: conservation of biodiversity, regulation of hydrological cycles, carbon sequestration, soil conservation and moderation of micro-climate. They also provide livelihood support to millions of people through timber, non-timber forest products, grazing and shifting cultivation in certain regions.

The economic value of forests includes marketed products as well as non-marketed ecosystem services. Valuation of these services remains methodologically challenging but is increasingly recognised in policy discourse.

## Historical Evolution of Forest Policy

Colonial forest policy was oriented primarily towards commercial timber extraction and the assertion of state control over forest resources. The Indian Forest Act of 1865 and its successors created categories of reserved and protected forests and restricted local rights.

After independence, the National Forest Policy of 1952 continued to emphasise the productive and protective functions of forests while setting a target of one-third of the geographical area under forest. The National Forest Policy of 1988 marked a significant shift. It accorded primacy to environmental stability and the conservation of biodiversity, recognised the symbiotic relationship between tribal communities and forests, and emphasised the meeting of local needs for fuelwood, fodder and non-timber products. Commercial exploitation was subordinated to these objectives.

## Joint Forest Management and Participatory Approaches

Joint Forest Management (JFM) emerged in the 1990s as a participatory approach in which village communities and the forest department share responsibilities and benefits of forest protection and management. JFM committees were formed in many states, and success varied with the quality of institutional design, benefit-sharing arrangements and local leadership.

The recognition of community forest rights under the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 further altered the legal landscape by creating a framework for recording individual and community rights over forest land and resources.

## Contemporary Challenges

Forest degradation remains a concern even where canopy cover appears stable, because of changes in species composition, loss of biodiversity and reduction in forest quality. Diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes (mining, infrastructure, industry) requires compensatory afforestation and payment of net present value, but the ecological equivalence of compensation is often debated.

Human-wildlife conflict has increased in many areas as habitats shrink and animal populations recover in protected areas. Climate change adds further stress through altered rainfall patterns, increased frequency of extreme events and shifts in species distribution.

## Key Conceptual Points

- Forest cover and recorded forest area are related but distinct concepts.
- Forest policy has evolved from a commercial-colonial orientation to a conservation and livelihood-oriented framework.
- Participatory management and recognition of community rights represent attempts to reconcile conservation with local dependence.
- Forests are simultaneously ecological systems, economic resources and cultural landscapes.
- Sustainable forest management requires integration of ecological science, local knowledge and institutional design.

A clear understanding of forest resources and the evolution of forest policy is necessary for analysing environmental governance, tribal rights, biodiversity conservation and the broader relationship between development and ecological sustainability in India.
