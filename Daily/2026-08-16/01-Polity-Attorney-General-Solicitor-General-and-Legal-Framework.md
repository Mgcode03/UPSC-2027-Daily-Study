# Attorney General of India, Solicitor General and the Legal Framework of the Union

## Constitutional Position of the Attorney General

Article 76 of the Constitution of India provides for the office of the Attorney General for India. The Attorney General is the highest law officer of the country and is appointed by the President. The person appointed must be qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court. This means the individual must be a citizen of India and must have been for at least five years a Judge of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession, or an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession for at least ten years, or a distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President.

The Attorney General holds office during the pleasure of the President. There is no fixed tenure. The remuneration is also determined by the President. In practice, the Attorney General resigns when the government that appointed him or her demits office, reflecting the political character of the appointment while the office itself remains constitutional.

## Duties and Functions

The Attorney General has the following primary duties:

- To give advice to the Government of India upon such legal matters as may be referred to him or her by the President.
- To perform such other duties of a legal character as may be assigned by the President.
- To discharge the functions conferred on him or her by the Constitution or by any other law for the time being in force.

In the performance of these duties, the Attorney General has the right of audience in all courts in the territory of India. He or she also has the right to speak and otherwise take part in the proceedings of either House of Parliament or any joint sitting or any committee of Parliament of which he or she may be named a member, but without a right to vote.

The Attorney General is not a full-time counsel for the Government. He or she is allowed to take up private practice provided it does not conflict with official duties and does not involve appearance against the Government of India. The office is therefore unique in combining constitutional status with limited private practice permission.

## Limitations and Conventions

Although the Constitution does not explicitly bar the Attorney General from political activity, constitutional conventions and the nature of the office demand political neutrality in the discharge of legal advice. The Attorney General is expected to provide independent legal advice even when that advice is inconvenient to the government of the day. In practice, the degree of independence has varied with individuals and political circumstances.

The Attorney General cannot defend accused persons in criminal prosecutions without the permission of the Government of India. Appearance against the Government is not permitted. These restrictions preserve the fiduciary character of the relationship between the law officer and the Union.

## Solicitor General and Additional Solicitors General

The Constitution does not mention the offices of Solicitor General or Additional Solicitors General. These are statutory and conventional offices created to assist the Attorney General. The Solicitor General is the second highest law officer of the country. Additional Solicitors General rank next. Their appointments are made by the Government of India and they assist in the conduct of government litigation, especially in the Supreme Court and High Courts.

The Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules regulate their tenure, remuneration and conditions of service. Like the Attorney General, they hold office during the pleasure of the government and customarily resign with a change of government.

## Role in Constitutional Interpretation and Litigation

The Attorney General plays a significant role when the Supreme Court seeks the assistance of the Union on constitutional questions. In presidential references under Article 143, the Attorney General is usually heard. In cases involving the interpretation of the Constitution, fundamental rights, federal disputes and the validity of legislation, the views expressed by the Attorney General carry considerable weight, though they are not binding on the Court.

The Attorney General also represents the Union in important international legal proceedings and in matters before the International Court of Justice when India is a party, subject to government instructions.

## Comparison with the Advocate General of a State

Article 165 provides for the Advocate General of a State. The Advocate General is the highest law officer of the State and is appointed by the Governor. The qualifications are the same as those required for appointment as a Judge of a High Court. The Advocate General has the right of audience in all courts within the State and the right to take part in the proceedings of the State Legislature without voting rights.

The relationship between the Attorney General and the Advocates General is not hierarchical. Each advises his or her respective government. Coordination occurs in matters of concurrent interest or when the Union and States appear on the same side or opposite sides in federal disputes.

## Key Points for Conceptual Clarity

- The Attorney General is a constitutional office under Article 76; the Solicitor General is not.
- Qualification is identical to that of a Supreme Court Judge.
- Tenure is during the pleasure of the President; resignation with change of government is conventional.
- Right of audience in all Indian courts and right to participate in parliamentary proceedings without vote.
- Limited private practice is permitted subject to non-conflict.
- The office combines legal expertise with political appointment, creating a permanent tension between independence and accountability.

## Conceptual Significance in the Constitutional Scheme

The existence of an independent law officer who can advise the executive and appear for it in court is a feature of parliamentary systems derived from the British tradition. In India the office has acquired additional importance because of the written Constitution, the power of judicial review, and the frequent constitutional litigation that characterises the Indian polity. The quality of legal advice tendered to the government and the manner in which the Union is represented in the higher judiciary affect both the quality of governance and the development of constitutional jurisprudence.

Understanding the precise constitutional status, functions and limitations of the Attorney General is essential for analysing the separation of powers, the relationship between the executive and the judiciary, and the institutional mechanisms that support the rule of law in India.
