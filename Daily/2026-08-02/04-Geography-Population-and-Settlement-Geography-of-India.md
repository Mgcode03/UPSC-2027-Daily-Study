# Population and Settlement Geography of India

## Introduction to Population Geography

Population geography studies the spatial distribution, composition, growth, and movement of human populations, and the relationship between population and the environment and resources. For India, population is both a resource and a challenge. Understanding demographic patterns is essential for planning, resource allocation, and policy design.

India is the most populous country in the world (having overtaken China in recent estimates). Its demographic features have profound implications for the economy, society, environment, and politics.

## Sources of Population Data

The primary source of population data in India is the Census of India, conducted every ten years. The first synchronous census was held in 1881. The Census provides data on size, growth, distribution, density, sex ratio, literacy, occupation, migration, and other characteristics. Sample Registration System (SRS), National Family Health Survey (NFHS), and other surveys supplement census data with more frequent estimates of vital rates and health indicators.

## Distribution and Density of Population

### Spatial Distribution

Population in India is highly unevenly distributed. The factors influencing distribution include:

- **Physical factors**: Relief, climate, soil, water availability. The Northern Plains, coastal plains, and fertile river valleys support high densities. The Himalayan region, Thar Desert, and parts of the northeastern hills have low densities.
- **Economic factors**: Availability of agricultural land, industrial development, urbanisation, and employment opportunities attract population.
- **Historical and social factors**: Areas of early settlement and historical development often retain higher densities. Social factors such as the prevalence of joint family systems and cultural preferences also play a role.

The Indo-Gangetic plain is one of the most densely populated regions in the world. States such as Bihar, West Bengal, and Uttar Pradesh have high absolute populations and high densities. In contrast, states such as Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, and Sikkim have very low densities.

### Density of Population

Population density is the number of persons per unit area (usually per square kilometre). India has a high overall density compared to the world average. Density varies enormously across states and districts.

- High density regions: Bihar, West Bengal, Kerala, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and parts of the National Capital Region.
- Moderate density: Many parts of peninsular India.
- Low density: Himalayan states, desert areas of Rajasthan, and sparsely populated parts of the Northeast and central India.

Physiological density (persons per unit of arable land) and agricultural density (agricultural population per unit of arable land) are useful additional measures because they relate population more closely to productive capacity.

## Growth of Population

### Phases of Population Growth in India

Indian demographic history is often divided into phases based on census data:

1. **Period of Stagnant or Steady Growth (before 1921)**: High birth rate matched by high death rate. Epidemics, famines, and poor health conditions kept growth low. The year 1921 is often called the Year of the Great Divide because after this the population began to grow more consistently.
2. **Period of Steady Growth (1921–1951)**: Decline in mortality due to improvements in health and sanitation, while fertility remained high. Growth rate increased.
3. **Period of Rapid High Growth (1951–1981)**: Further decline in death rates due to expanded public health measures, while birth rates declined only slowly. This produced a population explosion phase with high annual growth rates.
4. **Period of High Growth with Signs of Slowing Down (1981 onwards)**: Birth rates began to decline more noticeably due to fertility transition, family planning programmes, rising literacy (especially female literacy), urbanisation, and socio-economic changes. Growth rate has been declining, though absolute addition to population remains large because of the large base.

### Determinants of Population Growth

Population growth is determined by the balance of births, deaths, and net migration.

- **Fertility**: Measured by Crude Birth Rate (CBR) and Total Fertility Rate (TFR). TFR has declined significantly in India and is approaching or has reached replacement level (around 2.1) at the national level in recent estimates, though with large interstate variations.
- **Mortality**: Crude Death Rate (CDR) and Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) have declined substantially. Life expectancy has increased.
- **Migration**: Internal migration redistributes population within the country. International migration has a smaller impact on national growth rates but is significant for certain regions and for the Indian diaspora.

### Demographic Transition

India is passing through the demographic transition. Different states are at different stages. Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and some other southern states have low fertility and mortality and are in the advanced stage of transition. Some northern and central states still have higher fertility and are in earlier stages. This differential has implications for future population distribution, labour force, and political representation.

## Composition of Population

### Age Composition

The age structure of the population is usually represented by a population pyramid. India has a relatively young population, with a large proportion in the working-age group (15–59 years). This creates the potential for a demographic dividend if the working-age population is productively employed and skilled. At the same time, a large young population requires investment in education, health, and job creation. The proportion of the elderly is also rising and will require attention to social security and healthcare.

### Sex Ratio

Sex ratio is the number of females per thousand males. India’s overall sex ratio has improved in recent censuses but remains adverse. The child sex ratio (0–6 years) has been a matter of serious concern because of sex-selective practices. Factors influencing sex ratio include preference for male children, female foeticide, higher female mortality in some age groups, and differential migration. States such as Kerala have a favourable sex ratio, while some northern states have highly adverse ratios.

### Literacy

Literacy rate is a key indicator of human development. India has made significant progress in literacy since Independence, but gaps remain between males and females, rural and urban areas, and across states and social groups. Female literacy is particularly important because of its strong correlation with lower fertility, better child health, and overall development.

### Rural-Urban Composition

India remains predominantly rural, though the urban population has been growing steadily. Urbanisation is driven by natural increase in urban areas, rural-to-urban migration, and reclassification of rural areas as urban. Urbanisation brings opportunities (employment, services, innovation) and challenges (housing, infrastructure, pollution, inequality, and the growth of slums).

### Linguistic and Religious Composition

India is characterised by enormous linguistic and religious diversity. The Constitution recognises multiple official languages and protects the rights of linguistic and religious minorities. This diversity is a source of cultural richness and also requires careful management of identity politics and centre-state and inter-community relations.

## Migration

Migration is the movement of people from one place to another. It can be internal or international, temporary or permanent, voluntary or forced.

### Internal Migration in India

Major streams of internal migration include:

- Rural to rural (often for marriage or agricultural work)
- Rural to urban (for employment, education, and better services)
- Urban to urban
- Urban to rural (less common; includes return migration)

Push factors include poverty, unemployment, lack of amenities, natural disasters, and social conflict. Pull factors include employment opportunities, better education and health facilities, and urban amenities. Migration has significant implications for the labour market, urban planning, remittances to rural areas, and social change.

### International Migration

India has a large diaspora. Emigration for work (to the Gulf countries, for example) and for education and skilled employment (to North America, Europe, and Australia) is significant. Remittances are an important source of foreign exchange. Issues related to the welfare of migrant workers, brain drain versus brain gain, and the rights of the diaspora are part of policy discourse.

## Settlement Geography

### Rural Settlements

Rural settlements in India vary in form, size, and function.

- **Clustered or nucleated settlements**: Houses are built close to each other; common in fertile plains and in areas where security or water availability encourages concentration.
- **Semi-clustered or fragmented settlements**: Result from segregation of a large village or from the settling of a group away from the main village.
- **Hamleted settlements**: Fragmented into several units physically separated but sharing a common name and identity; common in parts of the middle and lower Ganga plain and in tribal areas.
- **Dispersed or isolated settlements**: Found in areas of extreme fragmentation of terrain, forested areas, or extensive agriculture; common in Himalayan region, parts of peninsular India, and desert areas.

Factors influencing rural settlement patterns include nature of terrain, availability of water, quality of soil, defence needs, and social structure (including caste-based segregation in some regions).

### Urban Settlements

Urban settlements are classified on the basis of size, function, and administrative status. The Census of India defines urban areas on the basis of population size, density, and occupational structure (non-agricultural workforce). Cities and towns perform multiple functions: administrative, industrial, commercial, transport, educational, and cultural.

Functional classification of towns includes administrative towns, industrial towns, transport towns, commercial towns, mining towns, cantonment towns, and educational towns, among others. Many large cities are multifunctional.

Urban morphology in India often shows a mix of old historic cores, colonial additions, planned post-Independence developments, and unplanned peripheral growth including informal settlements.

### Problems of Urbanisation

- Housing shortage and growth of slums and informal settlements
- Inadequate infrastructure (water, sanitation, transport, electricity)
- Traffic congestion and pollution
- Solid waste management challenges
- Urban poverty and inequality
- Pressure on land and environment
- Governance and planning deficits

Smart Cities Mission, AMRUT, PMAY, and other programmes represent policy responses to urban challenges.

## Population Policies and Issues

India adopted an official family planning programme early (1952). Over time the approach evolved from a purely clinical and target-oriented approach to a broader reproductive and child health approach emphasising choice, quality of care, and women’s empowerment. The National Population Policy 2000 set goals for reducing fertility and improving maternal and child health. Current discourse emphasises the demographic dividend, skill development, employment, education, health, and the need to address regional imbalances in demographic transition.

Key issues for policy and public debate include:

- Realising the demographic dividend through education, skills, and jobs
- Addressing adverse child sex ratio and gender inequality
- Managing urbanisation and migration
- Providing social security for the growing elderly population
- Ensuring sustainable use of resources in the context of a large population
- Balancing population stabilisation goals with human rights and equity

## Key Points to Remember

- 1921 is the Year of the Great Divide in India’s demographic history.
- Population distribution is highly uneven; physical and economic factors are primary determinants.
- India is in the advanced stages of demographic transition at the national level, with large interstate variation.
- Sex ratio, child sex ratio, and female literacy are critical indicators of social development.
- Demographic dividend refers to the economic potential of a large working-age population.
- Rural settlement types: clustered, semi-clustered, hamleted, dispersed.
- Urbanisation brings both opportunities and serious challenges of infrastructure and governance.
- Migration is a major factor in redistributing population and shaping labour markets.

Population geography is not merely about numbers. It is about the relationship between people, place, resources, and development. For a country of India’s size and diversity, demographic understanding is foundational to almost every area of public policy.
