# Freedom Struggle: Moderates and Extremists (1885–1907 and Beyond)

## Background: Rise of Indian Nationalism

The second half of the nineteenth century witnessed the growth of modern Indian nationalism. Several factors contributed to this development:

- The economic critique of British rule by early nationalists, highlighting drain of wealth, deindustrialisation, and poverty.
- The impact of Western education, which introduced ideas of liberty, equality, democracy, and nationalism.
- The role of the press and literature in creating a sense of shared identity and disseminating nationalist ideas.
- Socio-religious reform movements that fostered a spirit of inquiry and pride in Indian heritage while also creating social awareness.
- The unifying impact of British administrative and economic unification of India, which created common grievances and a common platform.
- The racial arrogance of the British and discriminatory policies that hurt Indian self-respect.

The Indian National Congress was founded in December 1885 at Bombay by A.O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, along with prominent Indian leaders. The first session was held under the presidency of W.C. Bonnerjee. The early Congress was a moderate organisation that sought reforms within the framework of British rule through constitutional means.

## The Moderate Phase (1885–1905 approximately)

### Leadership and Character

The early leaders of the Congress are known as Moderates. Prominent among them were Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea, Romesh Chandra Dutt, Dinshaw Wacha, and others. They were mostly educated in the Western system, belonged to the professional middle class, and believed in the British sense of justice and fair play.

### Methods of the Moderates

The Moderates believed in constitutional agitation within the bounds of law. Their methods included:

- Passing resolutions and sending petitions, memorials, and prayers to the British government and Parliament.
- Using the press to educate public opinion and criticise government policies.
- Holding annual sessions of the Congress to discuss issues and formulate demands.
- Sending deputations to England to present the Indian case.
- Working through legislative councils (after the Indian Councils Act of 1892 provided limited opportunities).

They rejected mass agitation, passive resistance, or any form of violence. They believed that the British government could be persuaded through reasoned argument and moral pressure.

### Demands of the Moderates

The Moderates raised a wide range of demands that laid the foundation of the nationalist programme:

**Political Demands:**
- Expansion and reform of legislative councils with greater Indian representation and real powers.
- Indianisation of the higher civil services through simultaneous examinations in India and England, and raising the age limit.
- Separation of the judiciary from the executive.
- Repeal of repressive laws such as the Arms Act and Vernacular Press Act (the latter had already been repealed by then, but the principle remained).
- Greater opportunity for Indians in the administration and military.

**Economic Demands:**
- Reduction of land revenue and revision of revenue settlements to protect peasantry.
- Reduction of military expenditure and home charges.
- Protection of Indian industries through tariffs and government support.
- Inquiry into India’s poverty and the drain of wealth.
- Development of banking, irrigation, and infrastructure for the benefit of Indians.

**Administrative and Other Demands:**
- Civil liberties including freedom of speech and press.
- Primary education and technical education.
- Reduction of the burden of the salt tax and other oppressive taxes.

Dadabhai Naoroji’s work on the drain theory (in books such as Poverty and Un-British Rule in India) provided a powerful economic critique of colonialism. R.C. Dutt’s economic history of India further strengthened this critique. Gokhale’s budget speeches in the Imperial Legislative Council were models of reasoned economic and political analysis.

### Evaluation of the Moderate Phase

**Achievements:**
- Created a national political platform and a sense of common all-India interests.
- Developed a coherent economic critique of colonialism that remained influential for decades.
- Trained a generation of political workers in the methods of organisation and public debate.
- Forced the British government to make some concessions (for example, the Indian Councils Act of 1892, though limited).
- Laid the ideological and organisational foundations for the later, more militant phases of the national movement.

**Limitations:**
- Narrow social base; largely confined to the educated urban middle class.
- Faith in British justice proved misplaced; the government remained largely unresponsive.
- Methods of petitioning and prayer were inadequate against a colonial power that did not depend on Indian consent.
- Failure to mobilise the masses or to connect with the peasantry and workers in a sustained manner.
- Some Moderates were perceived as too loyal and too dependent on British goodwill.

By the turn of the century, dissatisfaction with Moderate methods and limited achievements led to the rise of a more radical stream within the national movement.

## The Extremist Phase and the Rise of Militant Nationalism

### Causes for the Rise of Extremism

Several factors contributed to the growth of Extremism or militant nationalism around the turn of the century:

1. **Recognition of the true nature of British rule**: The economic critique developed by Moderates themselves convinced many that British rule was fundamentally exploitative and would not reform itself out of existence.
2. **International influences**: The defeat of Russia by Japan (1905) showed that an Asian power could defeat a European power. Revolutionary movements in other countries and the writings of European thinkers also had an impact.
3. **Reaction to British policies**: The partition of Bengal (1905), the Indian Universities Act, the Official Secrets Act, and the general attitude of Lord Curzon (Viceroy 1899–1905) generated widespread anger.
4. **Growth of education and the press**: A larger number of educated Indians and a more assertive vernacular press created a receptive audience for radical ideas.
5. **Influence of cultural and religious revival**: Leaders like Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Swami Vivekananda, and the Theosophical Society contributed to a sense of cultural confidence and pride that fed into political assertiveness.
6. **Dissatisfaction with Moderate methods**: The younger generation felt that petitions and prayers had failed and that a more assertive approach was needed.

### Leadership and Ideology of the Extremists

Prominent Extremist leaders included Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai (together known as Lal-Bal-Pal), Aurobindo Ghosh, and others. They drew inspiration from India’s past, emphasised self-reliance (swadeshi), and called for more direct action.

**Key Elements of Extremist Ideology:**

- **Swaraj**: Tilak declared “Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.” Swaraj was understood as self-rule, though the exact content (dominion status or complete independence) evolved over time.
- **Swadeshi**: Boycott of foreign goods and promotion of indigenous industries and education.
- **Boycott**: Extension of boycott from foreign goods to government schools, colleges, courts, titles, and services in the more radical versions.
- **National Education**: Establishment of national schools and colleges free from government control.
- **Passive Resistance**: Refusal to cooperate with the government in certain spheres; in Aurobindo’s formulation, it included organised refusal to pay taxes and other forms of non-cooperation.
- **Mass Mobilisation**: Unlike Moderates, Extremists sought to involve broader sections of the people, including students, youth, and in some regions the lower middle classes and peasantry.
- **Cultural Nationalism**: Emphasis on Hindu cultural symbols and festivals (for example, Tilak’s Ganapati and Shivaji festivals) as a means of political mobilisation. This also created tensions with Muslim political identity in some contexts.

### The Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi Movement (1905–1908)

The partition of Bengal, announced by Lord Curzon and effected in October 1905, was justified on administrative grounds but was widely seen as an attempt to divide the Bengali-speaking population on religious lines and to weaken the nationalist movement. It became the immediate catalyst for the Swadeshi Movement.

**Features of the Swadeshi Movement:**

- Boycott of British goods, especially Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt.
- Public bonfires of foreign cloth.
- Promotion of swadeshi enterprises (textile mills, soap and match factories, national banks, insurance companies).
- National education movement: establishment of national schools and colleges (for example, Bengal National College with Aurobindo as Principal).
- Use of traditional festivals, songs (Rabindranath Tagore and others composed patriotic songs), and rakhi-bandhan as symbols of unity.
- Participation of students, women, and in some areas the urban lower middle class.
- In some regions, the movement took a more radical turn with passive resistance and calls for non-cooperation.

The movement was strongest in Bengal but also spread to other parts of India, including Maharashtra, Punjab, and Madras Presidency, with local variations.

### The Surat Split (1907)

Differences between Moderates and Extremists came to a head at the Surat session of the Congress in 1907. The issues included the election of the President and the inclusion of swadeshi, boycott, and national education in the Congress programme. The session witnessed chaotic scenes and a formal split. The Extremists were effectively excluded from the Congress for several years.

The split weakened the national movement at a critical time. The Moderates were left with a diminished organisation, while the Extremists faced severe government repression. Many Extremist leaders were imprisoned or forced into exile or inactivity. The period after 1908 saw a decline in open mass agitation and a temporary setback for the national movement, until the Home Rule Leagues and the arrival of Gandhi opened new phases.

## Comparison: Moderates and Extremists

| Aspect | Moderates | Extremists |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Social Base | Educated urban middle class, professionals | Broader; included students, youth, lower middle class; attempted mass reach |
| Goal | Constitutional reforms, greater Indian share in administration; eventual self-government within Empire | Swaraj; more assertive demand for self-rule |
| Methods | Petitions, prayers, resolutions, constitutional agitation, press | Swadeshi, boycott, national education, passive resistance, mass mobilisation |
| Attitude to British | Faith in British justice and capacity for reform | Distrust of British intentions; belief that freedom had to be wrested |
| View of Masses | Limited effort at mass mobilisation | Greater emphasis on involving the people |
| Cultural Idiom | Largely secular, liberal, Western-influenced | Stronger use of religious and cultural symbols |
| Key Leaders | Naoroji, Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea | Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo |

## Significance of the Moderate and Extremist Phases

1. The Moderates established the Indian National Congress as an all-India organisation and developed the economic critique of colonialism that remained central to nationalist ideology.
2. The Extremists expanded the social base of the movement, introduced new methods of agitation, and made swaraj a popular slogan.
3. The Swadeshi Movement was the first major mass movement under Congress leadership and demonstrated the potential of economic boycott and national education.
4. The split at Surat showed the dangers of organisational division and the need for a broader unity of purpose.
5. Both streams contributed to the evolution of Indian nationalism. Later leaders, including Gandhi, drew upon elements from both traditions while transforming the movement into a genuine mass movement with new methods of non-violent resistance.

## Key Points to Remember

- Congress founded in 1885; early phase dominated by Moderates.
- Drain theory associated with Dadabhai Naoroji.
- Partition of Bengal in 1905 triggered the Swadeshi Movement.
- Lal-Bal-Pal: Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal.
- Surat Split in 1907 between Moderates and Extremists.
- Tilak’s slogan: “Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.”
- Methods of Extremists: swadeshi, boycott, national education, passive resistance.
- The period laid the foundations for the later phases of the freedom struggle under Gandhi and others.

This phase of the freedom struggle is essential for understanding the evolution of Indian nationalism from elite constitutionalism to mass politics, and for analysing the continuous tension between different strategies of anti-colonial resistance.
