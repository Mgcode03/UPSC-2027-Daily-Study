# Constitutional Amendments and the Basic Structure Doctrine

## Introduction to Constitutional Amendments

The Constitution of India is a living document. It was designed to adapt to changing social, economic, and political needs of the nation. Article 368 of the Constitution provides the procedure for its amendment. Unlike ordinary laws, constitutional amendments require a special procedure to ensure that fundamental changes are made only after careful deliberation and broad consensus.

The power to amend the Constitution is vested in the Parliament. However, this power is not absolute. The Supreme Court of India has placed important limitations on this power through judicial interpretation, most notably through the Basic Structure Doctrine.

## Types of Amendments under Article 368

Article 368 provides for three categories of amendments based on the majority required and the involvement of states:

### 1. Amendments by Simple Majority

These are not considered constitutional amendments under Article 368 in the strict sense. They are passed by a simple majority of members present and voting in each House of Parliament, similar to ordinary legislation. Examples include:

- Admission or establishment of new states (Article 2)
- Formation of new states and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing states (Article 3)
- Abolition or creation of Legislative Councils in states (Article 169)
- Rules of procedure in Parliament
- Privileges of Parliament and its members
- Citizenship provisions in certain cases
- Use of official language

These changes do not require the special majority prescribed under Article 368.

### 2. Amendments by Special Majority

Most constitutional amendments fall under this category. They require:

- A majority of the total membership of each House of Parliament, **and**
- A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

This is known as the special majority. Examples of amendments under this category include changes to Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and most other provisions of the Constitution.

### 3. Amendments by Special Majority plus Ratification by States

Certain amendments that affect the federal structure of the Constitution require, in addition to the special majority in Parliament, ratification by the legislatures of at least one-half of the states. The provisions that require such ratification include:

- Election of the President
- Extent of the executive power of the Union and the states
- Supreme Court and High Courts
- Distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the states
- Representation of states in Parliament
- The amendment procedure itself (Article 368)

This requirement protects the federal character of the Constitution by ensuring that states have a say in changes that affect the balance of power between the Centre and the states.

## Procedure for Amendment

1. An amendment bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament.
2. It must be passed by the required majority in each House separately. There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve disagreements between the two Houses on a constitutional amendment bill.
3. After passage by both Houses, the bill is presented to the President for assent. The President is bound to give assent; there is no power to withhold or return a constitutional amendment bill.
4. For amendments requiring state ratification, the bill is sent to the states after passage by Parliament. Once ratified by the required number of states, it is presented to the President.

## Important Constitutional Amendments – Key Facts

### First Amendment (1951)

- Added restrictions on freedom of speech and expression under Article 19 in the interest of public order, friendly relations with foreign states, and incitement to an offence.
- Inserted Articles 31A and 31B to protect land reform laws from challenge on the ground of violation of Fundamental Rights.
- Added the Ninth Schedule to the Constitution to place certain laws beyond judicial review on the ground of Fundamental Rights violation.

### Seventh Amendment (1956)

- Implemented the recommendations of the States Reorganisation Commission.
- Reorganised states on linguistic lines.
- Abolished the classification of states into Part A, B, C and D categories.
- Provided for the appointment of a Governor for two or more states.

### Twenty-Fourth Amendment (1971)

- Affirmed the power of Parliament to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
- Made it obligatory for the President to give assent to a constitutional amendment bill.
- Was a response to the Supreme Court judgment in the Golaknath case (1967).

### Forty-Second Amendment (1976)

- Known as the Mini-Constitution because of the large number of changes it introduced.
- Added the words Socialist, Secular and Integrity to the Preamble.
- Added Fundamental Duties (Part IVA).
- Extended the term of Lok Sabha and State Assemblies from five to six years (later reversed).
- Gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights in certain cases.
- Curtailed the power of judicial review in several ways.
- Made changes to the amendment procedure itself.

### Forty-Fourth Amendment (1978)

- Restored many of the features altered by the Forty-Second Amendment.
- Reduced the term of Lok Sabha and State Assemblies back to five years.
- Made the right to property a legal right under Article 300A instead of a Fundamental Right.
- Strengthened the protection of Fundamental Rights during Emergency by requiring that Article 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even during Emergency.
- Restored the power of judicial review in several areas.

### Seventy-Third and Seventy-Fourth Amendments (1992)

- Seventy-Third Amendment gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions (Part IX and Eleventh Schedule).
- Seventy-Fourth Amendment gave constitutional status to urban local bodies (Part IXA and Twelfth Schedule).
- These amendments institutionalised democratic decentralisation and local self-government.

### Eighty-Sixth Amendment (2002)

- Made elementary education a Fundamental Right under Article 21A for children between 6 and 14 years.
- Modified the corresponding Fundamental Duty and Directive Principle.

### Ninety-Ninth Amendment (2014) and One Hundredth Amendment (2015)

- Ninety-Ninth Amendment sought to establish the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC). It was struck down by the Supreme Court as violating the Basic Structure.
- One Hundredth Amendment operationalised the land boundary agreement between India and Bangladesh, involving exchange of enclaves.

### One Hundred and First Amendment (2016)

- Introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST).
- Amended several articles related to taxation and created the GST Council.

### One Hundred and Third Amendment (2019)

- Provided for 10 percent reservation in education and public employment for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) from the general category.
- Inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6).

## The Basic Structure Doctrine

### Origin and Evolution

The Basic Structure Doctrine is a judicial innovation. It holds that while Parliament has wide powers to amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot destroy or alter the basic features or the fundamental framework of the Constitution.

**Key Cases:**

- **Shankari Prasad (1951)**: Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
- **Sajjan Singh (1965)**: Reaffirmed the above view.
- **Golaknath (1967)**: Overruled previous judgments. Held that Fundamental Rights cannot be amended by Parliament. An amendment was treated as law under Article 13 and hence subject to Fundamental Rights.
- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**: A 13-judge bench held by a majority of 7:6 that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. This is the foundation of the Basic Structure Doctrine.
- **Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)**: Applied the doctrine to strike down a part of the Thirty-Ninth Amendment that sought to place the election of the Prime Minister beyond judicial review.
- **Minerva Mills (1980)**: Strengthened the doctrine. Held that the limited amending power itself is a basic feature. Struck down clauses of the Forty-Second Amendment that gave unlimited amending power to Parliament and that gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights in a manner that destroyed the balance.
- **Waman Rao (1981)**: Clarified that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after the Kesavananda judgment would be open to challenge on the ground of violation of basic structure.
- **I.R. Coelho (2007)**: Held that even laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of Kesavananda judgment) are open to challenge if they destroy the basic structure.
- **NJAC Case (2015)**: Struck down the Ninety-Ninth Amendment and the NJAC Act on the ground that they violated the independence of the judiciary, which is part of the basic structure.

### Elements of the Basic Structure (as identified by the Supreme Court over time)

The Court has not given an exhaustive list. The following have been recognised as part of the basic structure in various judgments:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Republican and democratic form of government
- Secular character of the Constitution
- Separation of powers between the legislature, executive and judiciary
- Federal character of the Constitution
- Unity and integrity of the nation
- Sovereignty of India
- Individual freedom and dignity
- Rule of law
- Independence of the judiciary
- Judicial review
- Free and fair elections
- Limited amending power of Parliament
- Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
- Principle of equality
- Effective access to justice
- Welfare state / social justice

### Significance of the Doctrine

1. It acts as a check on the absolute power of Parliament to amend the Constitution.
2. It protects the core values and identity of the Constitution from being destroyed by temporary majorities.
3. It strengthens the role of the judiciary as the guardian of the Constitution.
4. It maintains the balance between flexibility (needed for adaptation) and rigidity (needed to protect fundamental principles).
5. It has been praised as a unique contribution of Indian constitutional jurisprudence to the world.

### Criticism of the Doctrine

- It is not expressly mentioned in the Constitution; it is a judicial creation.
- Critics argue that it places the judiciary above Parliament, which is the elected representative body of the people.
- There is no fixed and exhaustive list of basic features, leading to uncertainty.
- Some argue that it can lead to judicial overreach.

Despite criticisms, the doctrine has become an accepted and integral part of Indian constitutional law. Successive governments and Parliaments have worked within its framework.

## Key Points to Remember

- Article 368 is the amendment provision.
- Three types of majority requirements exist for different kinds of amendments.
- No joint sitting is possible for constitutional amendment bills.
- President cannot withhold assent to a constitutional amendment bill.
- Basic Structure Doctrine originated in Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
- Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure even while amending the Constitution.
- Judicial review and independence of judiciary are part of the basic structure.
- Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 are open to basic structure challenge.
- The doctrine balances the need for change with the need to preserve constitutional identity.

## Conceptual Clarity for Mains and Prelims

For Prelims, focus on:
- Exact majority requirements
- Which amendments require state ratification
- Landmark cases and their holdings
- Specific changes made by major amendments (especially 1st, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 86th, 101st, 103rd)

For Mains, be prepared to discuss:
- The tension between parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy
- The evolution and significance of the Basic Structure Doctrine
- Whether the doctrine is a legitimate judicial innovation or an example of overreach
- How the amendment process balances flexibility and rigidity
- The impact of major amendments on the federal structure, Fundamental Rights, and the nature of the Indian polity

The Constitution has been amended more than 100 times. This reflects both the adaptive capacity of the document and the dynamic nature of Indian democracy. The Basic Structure Doctrine ensures that adaptation does not become destruction of the constitutional soul.
