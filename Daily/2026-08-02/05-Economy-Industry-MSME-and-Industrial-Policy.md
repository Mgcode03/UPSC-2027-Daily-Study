# Industry, MSME and Industrial Policy in India

## Role of Industry in Economic Development

Industry is a critical sector of the economy. It contributes to GDP, employment, exports, technological progress, and the overall structural transformation of the economy from primary (agriculture) to secondary (industry) and tertiary (services) activities. Industrialisation has historically been associated with rising productivity, higher incomes, and improved living standards.

In India, the industrial sector includes manufacturing, mining, electricity, gas, water supply, and construction in the broader sense used in national accounts. Manufacturing is the core of industrial policy discussion. The share of manufacturing in India’s GDP has remained relatively modest compared to the ambitions of successive governments and compared to the experience of East Asian industrialisers. Raising the share of manufacturing and creating industrial employment remain important policy objectives.

## Structure of Indian Industry

Indian industry is characterised by a dual structure:

- **Large-scale organised sector**: Includes large private corporations, public sector enterprises, and multinational companies. It is capital-intensive, relatively more productive, and accounts for a large share of output and exports but a smaller share of employment.
- **Small and medium enterprises and the unorganised sector**: Labour-intensive, widespread geographically, important for employment and for serving local markets, but often constrained by access to credit, technology, infrastructure, and markets.

This dualism has implications for productivity, wages, and the nature of industrial growth.

## Evolution of Industrial Policy in India

### Pre-1991 Phase: Planned Industrialisation and the Licence-Permit Raj

After Independence, India adopted a strategy of planned economic development with a strong role for the state. The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948 and, more comprehensively, the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 defined the framework.

**Key features of the 1956 Resolution:**

- Classification of industries into three schedules:
  - Schedule A: Industries reserved for the public sector (arms and ammunition, atomic energy, railways, air transport, and others).
  - Schedule B: Industries where the public sector would play a leading role but private sector could also exist.
  - Schedule C: Remaining industries left to the private sector.
- Emphasis on heavy industry and capital goods as the foundation of long-term industrial development (influenced by the Mahalanobis strategy in the Second Five-Year Plan).
- Protection of small-scale industries through reservation of certain products for exclusive production by the small-scale sector.
- Regulation of private investment through industrial licensing under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951.
- Controls on monopoly and concentration of economic power (later formalised under the MRTP Act).
- Emphasis on import substitution and self-reliance.

The system of industrial licensing, import controls, and other regulations came to be known as the Licence-Permit Quota Raj. While it helped create a diversified industrial base and a large public sector, it also led to inefficiencies, delays, corruption, restricted competition, and technological stagnation in many areas.

### The 1991 Industrial Policy and Economic Reforms

The balance of payments crisis of 1991 triggered a comprehensive set of economic reforms. The New Industrial Policy of 1991 marked a decisive shift.

**Major changes:**

1. **Abolition of industrial licensing**: Licensing was abolished for most industries except a small list related to security, strategic, and environmental concerns.
2. **Public sector reforms**: The list of industries reserved for the public sector was drastically reduced. Disinvestment and privatisation of public sector enterprises were initiated. The public sector was to focus on strategic, high-tech, and essential infrastructure areas.
3. **MRTP Act reforms**: The pre-entry scrutiny of investment decisions of MRTP companies was abolished. The focus shifted to controlling and regulating monopolistic, restrictive, and unfair trade practices. (The Competition Act, 2002 later replaced the MRTP Act.)
4. **Foreign investment**: Automatic approval for foreign direct investment (FDI) up to specified limits in many industries; higher limits and full ownership allowed in a progressive manner over the years.
5. **Foreign technology agreements**: Automatic approval for technology agreements in high-priority industries within specified parameters.
6. **Liberalisation of trade and exchange rate**: Reduction of tariffs, removal of quantitative restrictions over time, and move towards a market-determined exchange rate.

The 1991 reforms opened the Indian economy to competition, both domestic and foreign. They led to increased investment, modernisation of industry, growth of the services sector (especially information technology), and integration with the global economy. Criticisms include concerns about inequality, regional imbalances, vulnerability to global shocks, and insufficient growth of manufacturing employment.

### Subsequent Policy Developments

Industrial policy has continued to evolve. Key themes in recent decades include:

- Make in India initiative to promote manufacturing and attract investment.
- Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for specific sectors to boost domestic manufacturing and exports.
- National Manufacturing Policy and efforts to create National Investment and Manufacturing Zones.
- Focus on ease of doing business, single-window clearances, and reduction of regulatory burden.
- Emphasis on startups, innovation, and the digital economy.
- Atmanirbhar Bharat (self-reliant India) discourse combining openness with the strengthening of domestic capabilities.
- Sector-specific policies for automobiles, electronics, pharmaceuticals, textiles, defence manufacturing, and others.

## Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)

### Definition and Classification

The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development (MSMED) Act, 2006 provides the legal framework. Classification is based on investment in plant and machinery or equipment and, more recently, also on turnover (composite criteria have been introduced and revised over time).

MSMEs are the backbone of the Indian economy in terms of employment, number of enterprises, and contribution to manufacturing output and exports. They are present in almost every sector and are crucial for inclusive growth and regional development.

### Importance of MSMEs

- Large contribution to employment (second only to agriculture in many estimates).
- Significant share in manufacturing output and exports.
- Promotion of entrepreneurship and innovation at the grassroots level.
- Better regional dispersal of industry compared to large-scale units.
- Flexibility and ability to serve niche and local markets.
- Potential for linkages with large industry through subcontracting and supply chains.

### Challenges Faced by MSMEs

1. **Access to credit**: Despite priority sector lending norms, many MSMEs face difficulties in obtaining timely and adequate credit at reasonable cost. Informal sources remain important for a large number of units.
2. **Technology and productivity**: Many MSMEs operate with outdated technology and low productivity.
3. **Infrastructure**: Inadequate power, logistics, and common facility infrastructure.
4. **Market access**: Difficulty in accessing larger markets, including government procurement and export markets; competition from large firms and imports.
5. **Regulatory compliance**: Multiplicity of regulations and compliance costs that are disproportionately burdensome for small units.
6. **Skilled labour**: Shortage of skilled workers and limited capacity for in-house training.
7. **Delayed payments**: Delayed payments from large buyers and government departments affect working capital.
8. **Formalisation**: A large part of the MSME sector remains informal, limiting access to formal credit, schemes, and social security.

### Policy Support for MSMEs

Successive governments have implemented a range of measures:

- Priority sector lending targets for banks.
- Credit Guarantee schemes to encourage banks to lend without collateral.
- Schemes for technology upgradation, quality certification, and marketing support.
- Cluster development programmes to provide common facilities.
- Reservation of products for exclusive manufacture by MSMEs (largely phased out as part of reforms, though some preference in procurement remains).
- Public procurement policy providing preferential treatment to MSMEs in government purchases.
- Emergency credit lines and support packages during crises (for example, during the COVID-19 pandemic).
- Efforts to promote formalisation through registration portals and linkage with schemes.
- Focus on ZED (Zero Defect Zero Effect) certification, lean manufacturing, and digital adoption.

The performance of the MSME sector remains central to the goal of employment-intensive industrial growth.

## Public Sector Enterprises

The public sector played a dominant role in the Nehru-Mahalanobis strategy. Public Sector Enterprises (PSEs) were established in core and strategic areas. Over time, many PSEs faced issues of inefficiency, overstaffing, political interference, and financial losses. Since 1991, the policy has shifted towards disinvestment, strategic sale, and limiting the public sector to strategic areas. Navratna and Maharatna status were given to better-performing PSEs to grant them greater operational autonomy. The debate on the appropriate role and size of the public sector continues.

## Key Industrial Sectors – Brief Overview

- **Textiles and garments**: Employment-intensive; important for exports; faces competition and needs modernisation and scale.
- **Pharmaceuticals**: India is a major global supplier of generic drugs; strengths in process chemistry; challenges in innovation and quality regulation.
- **Automobiles and auto components**: Significant growth after liberalisation; integrated into global value chains; transition to electric vehicles is a major current theme.
- **Electronics and semiconductors**: Focus of recent PLI schemes and policy push for domestic manufacturing and reduced import dependence.
- **Steel and metals**: Core industry; capacity expansion and competitiveness are ongoing issues.
- **Chemicals and petrochemicals**: Large and diversified; feedstock and environmental issues.
- **Defence manufacturing**: Push for indigenisation and private sector participation under Atmanirbhar Bharat.
- **Food processing**: Potential for linking agriculture with industry, reducing wastage, and creating rural employment.

## Industrial Location Factors

The location of industries is influenced by:

- Availability of raw materials
- Power and energy
- Labour (skilled and unskilled)
- Market access
- Transport and logistics
- Water
- Government policy and incentives (industrial estates, SEZs, tax benefits)
- Agglomeration economies (benefits of locating near other firms)
- Environmental regulations and land availability

Major industrial regions in India include the Mumbai-Pune belt, the Ahmedabad-Vadodara region, the Chota Nagpur plateau (heavy industry), the Bengaluru-Tamil Nadu industrial corridor areas, the National Capital Region, and emerging corridors and clusters in various states.

## Key Points to Remember

- Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 established the framework of public sector dominance and licensing.
- 1991 New Industrial Policy abolished licensing for most industries, reduced public sector reservation, and opened the economy to FDI and competition.
- MSMEs are critical for employment and entrepreneurship but face constraints in credit, technology, and markets.
- Make in India, PLI schemes, and ease of doing business reforms are central to current industrial strategy.
- The share of manufacturing in GDP and in employment remains a key policy concern.
- Dualism between the organised large sector and the unorganised/MSME sector is a structural feature of Indian industry.
- Public sector reforms, disinvestment, and the appropriate role of the state in industry remain live debates.

Industrial development is not only about growth of output. It is about productive employment, technological capability, regional balance, environmental sustainability, and the ability of the economy to compete and innovate in a globalised world. Understanding the evolution of policy and the structure of industry is essential for analysing India’s development trajectory.
