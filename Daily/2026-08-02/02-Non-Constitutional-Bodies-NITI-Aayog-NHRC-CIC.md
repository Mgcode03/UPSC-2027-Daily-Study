# Non-Constitutional Bodies: NITI Aayog, NHRC, CIC and Others

## Distinction between Constitutional and Non-Constitutional Bodies

Constitutional bodies are those that derive their authority and existence directly from the Constitution of India. Examples include the Election Commission (Article 324), Union Public Service Commission (Article 315), Comptroller and Auditor General (Article 148), Finance Commission (Article 280), and National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338).

Non-constitutional bodies, also called statutory bodies or extra-constitutional bodies, are created by an Act of Parliament or by executive resolution. They do not find mention in the Constitution. Their powers, composition, and functions are defined by the law or the resolution that creates them. They can be modified or abolished by ordinary legislation or executive action, subject to the terms of the creating instrument.

Understanding this distinction is important because constitutional bodies enjoy a higher degree of permanence and protection, while non-constitutional bodies are more flexible and can be redesigned as policy needs evolve.

## NITI Aayog

### Background and Creation

NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) was established on 1 January 2015 by a resolution of the Union Cabinet. It replaced the Planning Commission, which had been functioning since 1950. The Planning Commission was also a non-constitutional, extra-statutory body created by executive resolution.

The shift from Planning Commission to NITI Aayog reflected a change in the philosophy of economic planning and Centre-State relations. The Planning Commission was seen as a top-down, control-oriented body that allocated resources and approved state plans. NITI Aayog was conceived as a think tank and policy advisory body that would foster cooperative federalism and bottom-up planning.

### Composition

- **Chairperson**: Prime Minister of India
- **Governing Council**: Chief Ministers of all states and Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories
- **Regional Councils**: Formed to address specific regional issues; convened by the Prime Minister and comprising Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors of the concerned region
- **Vice-Chairperson**: Appointed by the Prime Minister; given the rank of a Cabinet Minister
- **Full-time Members**: Experts appointed by the Prime Minister
- **Part-time Members**: Maximum of two from leading universities and research institutions, on rotation
- **Ex-officio Members**: Maximum of four Union Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister
- **Chief Executive Officer**: Appointed by the Prime Minister for a fixed tenure; rank of Secretary to the Government of India
- **Secretariat**: Officials and experts supporting the institution

### Objectives and Functions

NITI Aayog has been given a broad mandate. Its key objectives and functions include:

1. **Cooperative Federalism**: Foster involvement of states in the national development process and promote continuous engagement with states.
2. **Bottom-up Planning**: Design strategies at the village level and aggregate them progressively at higher levels.
3. **Policy Think Tank**: Provide strategic and technical advice to the Central and State governments on key policy matters.
4. **Innovation and Knowledge Hub**: Create a knowledge and innovation ecosystem; identify and disseminate best practices.
5. **Monitoring and Evaluation**: Monitor the implementation of programmes and evaluate their impact.
6. **Resource Allocation Interface**: Although it does not allocate funds like the Planning Commission, it plays a role in the discourse on resource allocation and prioritisation.
7. **Special Attention to Sections**: Focus on sections of society that may be at risk of not benefiting adequately from economic progress.
8. **International Engagement**: Partner with national and international think tanks and institutions.

### Key Initiatives and Work Areas

NITI Aayog has been associated with several initiatives and reports, including work on Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) localisation, Aspirational Districts Programme, Atal Innovation Mission, strategies on artificial intelligence, energy, health, education, and water, and various indices that rank states on different parameters (such as health index, education index, water index, and innovation index).

### Comparison with Planning Commission

| Aspect | Planning Commission | NITI Aayog |
|--------|---------------------|------------|
| Nature | Extra-constitutional, executive body | Extra-constitutional, executive body |
| Approach | Top-down planning and resource allocation | Bottom-up, cooperative, advisory |
| States' Role | Limited; states sought plan approval | Governing Council with CMs; emphasis on cooperative federalism |
| Power over Funds | Significant influence on plan funds | No direct allocation power |
| Focus | Five-year plans and allocation | Strategy, innovation, monitoring, SDGs |
| Structure | Deputy Chairman of Cabinet rank; Members | Vice-Chairperson of Cabinet rank; more flexible membership |

The transformation reflects the shift from a centrally planned economy towards a more market-oriented and federal approach to development policy.

## National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

### Legal Basis

The National Human Rights Commission was established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 (as amended). It is a statutory body. The Act also provides for State Human Rights Commissions and Human Rights Courts.

### Composition

The NHRC consists of:

- A Chairperson who has been a Chief Justice of India or a Judge of the Supreme Court
- One Member who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court
- One Member who is or has been the Chief Justice of a High Court
- Three Members (at least one woman) from among persons having knowledge of, or practical experience in, matters relating to human rights
- Deemed Members: Chairpersons of the National Commission for Minorities, National Commission for Scheduled Castes, National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, National Commission for Women, National Commission for Backward Classes, National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities (for the discharge of functions under the Act relating to their respective areas)

The Chairperson and Members are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Prime Minister (Chairperson), Speaker of Lok Sabha, Home Minister, Leaders of Opposition in both Houses, and Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha.

### Functions and Powers

The NHRC is mandated to:

1. Inquire into complaints of violation of human rights or abetment thereof, or negligence in the prevention of such violation by a public servant.
2. Intervene in any proceeding involving allegation of violation of human rights pending before a court, with the approval of the court.
3. Visit jails and other places of detention to study the living conditions of inmates and make recommendations.
4. Review the safeguards provided by the Constitution or any law for the protection of human rights and recommend measures for their effective implementation.
5. Review factors that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights and recommend appropriate remedial measures.
6. Study international treaties and instruments on human rights and make recommendations for their effective implementation.
7. Undertake and promote research in the field of human rights.
8. Spread human rights literacy and promote awareness through publications, media, seminars and other means.
9. Encourage the efforts of non-governmental organisations working in the field of human rights.
10. Perform such other functions as it may consider necessary for the promotion of human rights.

The Commission has the powers of a civil court while inquiring into complaints. It can summon witnesses, receive evidence, requisition public records, and issue commissions for examination of witnesses. It can recommend payment of compensation, initiation of proceedings against guilty public servants, and other remedial measures. Its recommendations are not binding, but the concerned government is required to respond within a stipulated time indicating the action taken.

### Limitations

- The Commission cannot inquire into any matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting violation of human rights is alleged to have been committed (with limited exceptions).
- It has limited jurisdiction over the armed forces; it can only seek a report from the Central Government and make recommendations.
- Its recommendations are advisory in nature.
- State Human Rights Commissions face issues of capacity, independence, and resources in several states.

Despite limitations, the NHRC has played an important role in highlighting human rights issues, intervening in cases of custodial violence, rights of prisoners, rights of vulnerable groups, and promoting a culture of human rights awareness.

## Central Information Commission (CIC)

### Legal Basis

The Central Information Commission was established under the Right to Information Act, 2005. It is a statutory body. The Act also provides for State Information Commissions.

### Composition

- Chief Information Commissioner
- Up to ten Information Commissioners

They are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Prime Minister (Chairperson), Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha, and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. They should be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge and experience in law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media, or administration and governance. They should not be Members of Parliament or state legislatures, or hold any other office of profit, or be connected with any political party, or carry on any business, or pursue any profession.

The Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners hold office for a term as prescribed (currently three years) or until the age of 65 years, whichever is earlier. They are not eligible for reappointment in the same capacity, subject to the provisions of the Act as amended.

### Powers and Functions

The CIC is the apex body for implementing the RTI Act at the Central level. Its functions include:

1. Receiving and inquiring into complaints regarding inability to submit a request, refusal of information, no response within time limits, unreasonable fees, incomplete or misleading information, or any other matter relating to requesting or obtaining access to records under the Act.
2. Hearing second appeals against the decisions of the Central Public Information Officers and First Appellate Authorities of Central public authorities.
3. While inquiring, the Commission has the powers of a civil court in respect of summoning and enforcing attendance of persons, requiring discovery and inspection of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit, requisitioning public records, and issuing summons for examination of witnesses or documents.
4. The Commission can require the public authority to compensate the complainant for any loss or other detriment suffered, and can impose penalties on the Public Information Officer for persistent default (up to Rs 25,000).
5. The Commission can recommend disciplinary action against the Public Information Officer under the service rules.
6. The Commission prepares an annual report on the implementation of the Act, which is laid before Parliament.

### Significance of RTI and CIC

The Right to Information Act is one of the most important transparency and accountability legislations in India. It empowers citizens to seek information from public authorities and thereby promotes transparency, reduces corruption, and strengthens democracy. The CIC and State Information Commissions are the institutional mechanisms that give teeth to this right. Landmark orders of the CIC have expanded the scope of the Act and clarified the obligations of public authorities.

Amendments to the RTI Act in 2019 changed the tenure and salary determination of the Information Commissioners, bringing them more under the control of the Central Government. These changes generated debate regarding the independence of the Commission.

## Other Important Non-Constitutional / Statutory Bodies

### Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)

- Established on the recommendation of the Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption.
- Initially set up by executive resolution in 1964; given statutory status by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003.
- Consists of a Central Vigilance Commissioner and not more than two Vigilance Commissioners.
- Appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee comprising the Prime Minister, Home Minister, and Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha.
- Superintendence over the Delhi Special Police Establishment (CBI) in cases under the Prevention of Corruption Act.
- Advises the Central Government on vigilance matters and reviews the vigilance systems of ministries and departments.
- Receives complaints of corruption and recommends investigation or other action.

### Lokpal and Lokayuktas

- The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 provides for the establishment of a Lokpal at the Centre and Lokayuktas in states to inquire into allegations of corruption against public functionaries.
- The Lokpal consists of a Chairperson and up to eight Members (judicial and non-judicial).
- Jurisdiction covers the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, Members of Parliament, and Group A, B, C, D officers of the Central Government, among others.
- The institution is a key part of the anti-corruption architecture, though its operational effectiveness depends on appointments, resources, and political will.

### National Commission for Women (NCW)

- Statutory body under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990.
- Works for the protection and promotion of the rights of women.
- Reviews legal and constitutional safeguards for women, recommends remedial measures, takes up cases of violation of women’s rights, and advises the government on policy matters affecting women.

### National Green Tribunal (NGT)

- Established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
- Specialised body for effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection and conservation of forests and other natural resources.
- Has the powers of a civil court and follows principles of natural justice; not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure.
- Applies the principles of sustainable development, precautionary principle, and polluter pays principle.

## Key Points for Examination

- NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission in 2015; it is a policy think tank emphasising cooperative federalism.
- NHRC is a statutory body under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993; its recommendations are advisory.
- CIC is established under the RTI Act, 2005; it hears second appeals and can impose penalties.
- CVC has statutory status under the 2003 Act; it exercises superintendence over CBI in corruption cases.
- Constitutional bodies have constitutional protection; non-constitutional bodies are creatures of statute or executive resolution and are more easily modifiable.
- Independence of these bodies depends on the method of appointment, security of tenure, financial autonomy, and the political culture of accountability.

These institutions form an important part of the architecture of governance, accountability, and rights protection in India. Their effectiveness ultimately depends not only on legal design but also on the quality of appointments, institutional culture, and the broader democratic environment.
