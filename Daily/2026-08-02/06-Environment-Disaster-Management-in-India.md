# Disaster Management in India

## Understanding Disasters

A disaster is a serious disruption of the functioning of a community or society involving widespread human, material, economic, or environmental losses and impacts that exceed the ability of the affected community or society to cope using its own resources. Disasters can be natural or human-induced (anthropogenic).

**Natural disasters** include earthquakes, floods, cyclones, droughts, landslides, tsunamis, heat waves, and cold waves. **Human-induced disasters** include industrial accidents, chemical disasters, nuclear accidents, major transport accidents, and fires. Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of many hydro-meteorological disasters.

India is among the most disaster-prone countries in the world because of its geo-climatic conditions. A large part of the landmass is prone to earthquakes, floods, cyclones, and droughts. High population density, poverty, unplanned urbanisation, and environmental degradation increase vulnerability.

## Hazard, Vulnerability, Risk and Capacity

- **Hazard**: A dangerous phenomenon, substance, human activity, or condition that may cause loss of life, injury, property damage, social and economic disruption, or environmental degradation.
- **Vulnerability**: The characteristics and circumstances of a community, system, or asset that make it susceptible to the damaging effects of a hazard. Vulnerability has physical, social, economic, and environmental dimensions.
- **Risk**: The combination of the probability of a hazardous event and its consequences. Risk is a function of hazard, vulnerability, and exposure, and is reduced by capacity.
- **Capacity**: The combination of all the strengths, attributes, and resources available within a community or organisation that can be used to achieve agreed goals, including coping with and recovering from disasters.

Disaster risk reduction aims to reduce the underlying risk factors and strengthen the resilience of communities and systems.

## Types of Disasters Common in India

### Earthquakes

India lies at the junction of the Indian plate and the Eurasian plate. The Himalayan region is highly seismic. The Bureau of Indian Standards has classified the country into different seismic zones (II to V, with V being the highest risk). Major earthquakes have occurred in Assam, Bihar-Nepal border, Uttarakhand, Gujarat (Bhuj 2001), Kashmir, and other regions. Earthquakes can cause massive loss of life and property, especially in poorly constructed buildings, and can trigger landslides and floods.

### Floods

Floods are the most frequent natural disaster in India. Causes include heavy rainfall, river overflow, inadequate drainage in urban areas, siltation of rivers, and in some cases dam failures or mismanagement of releases. The Ganga, Brahmaputra, and their tributaries, as well as rivers in the coastal and peninsular regions, cause recurrent floods. Floods displace millions, destroy crops and infrastructure, and cause loss of life.

### Cyclones

The east coast of India (Bay of Bengal) is more prone to cyclones than the west coast. Cyclones bring high winds, storm surges, and heavy rainfall, causing destruction in coastal areas. Improved early warning systems and evacuation have significantly reduced the loss of life in recent cyclones, though economic damage remains high.

### Droughts

Drought is a prolonged period of deficient rainfall leading to water scarcity and crop failure. Large parts of India, especially in the arid and semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana, are drought-prone. Drought has severe impacts on agriculture, rural livelihoods, drinking water, and the economy.

### Landslides

Landslides are common in the Himalayan region and the Western Ghats. They are triggered by heavy rainfall, earthquakes, deforestation, and unplanned construction. They cause loss of life, damage to roads and infrastructure, and isolation of communities.

### Other Hazards

Heat waves, cold waves, lightning, forest fires, industrial and chemical accidents (for example, Bhopal gas tragedy), and the potential for nuclear and radiological emergencies are also part of the disaster spectrum.

## Institutional Framework for Disaster Management in India

### Evolution

Until the 1990s, the approach to disasters in India was largely reactive and relief-centric. The focus was on post-disaster response, rescue, and relief. The International Decade for Natural Disaster Reduction (1990s), the impact of major disasters (such as the Latur earthquake 1993, Orissa super cyclone 1999, and Bhuj earthquake 2001), and the growing understanding of disaster risk reduction led to a paradigm shift towards prevention, mitigation, and preparedness.

### Disaster Management Act, 2005

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 provides the legal and institutional framework for disaster management in India. It defines disaster and disaster management comprehensively and establishes institutions at the national, state, and district levels.

**Key institutions under the Act:**

1. **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)**
   - Chaired by the Prime Minister.
   - Responsible for laying down policies, plans, and guidelines for disaster management.
   - Approves the National Plan and plans of ministries and departments.
   - Coordinates enforcement and implementation of policy and plan.
   - Provides guidelines to state authorities.

2. **National Executive Committee (NEC)**
   - Chaired by the Home Secretary.
   - Assists the NDMA in the performance of its functions.
   - Prepares the National Plan.
   - Coordinates response in the event of a threatening disaster situation or disaster.

3. **State Disaster Management Authority (SDMA)**
   - Chaired by the Chief Minister.
   - Lays down state policy and plans consistent with national guidelines.
   - Approves the State Plan and coordinates implementation.

4. **District Disaster Management Authority (DDMA)**
   - Chaired by the District Collector / District Magistrate / Deputy Commissioner.
   - Prepares district disaster management plan.
   - Coordinates response at the district level.
   - Ensures that the guidelines of NDMA and SDMA are followed.

5. **National Institute of Disaster Management (NIDM)**
   - Responsible for human resource development, training, research, documentation, and policy advocacy in the field of disaster management.

6. **National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)**
   - Specialised force constituted for the purpose of specialised response to natural and man-made disasters.
   - Consists of battalions trained and equipped for various contingencies.
   - Plays a key role in search and rescue operations.

The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry for disaster management at the national level. Other ministries have sectoral responsibilities (for example, Ministry of Agriculture for drought, Ministry of Earth Sciences for early warning of hydro-meteorological events).

### Financial Arrangements

The Act provides for the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) and State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) for meeting the expenses of emergency response, relief, and rehabilitation. There are also provisions related to mitigation funds. The Finance Commission makes recommendations regarding the size and sharing of these funds.

## Disaster Management Cycle

Disaster management is conceptualised as a continuous cycle with the following phases:

1. **Prevention and Mitigation**: Measures to prevent hazards from becoming disasters or to reduce their impact. Examples: building codes, flood embankments, afforestation, land-use planning, drought-resistant crops, awareness programmes.
2. **Preparedness**: Measures taken in advance to ensure effective response. Examples: early warning systems, evacuation plans, stockpiling of relief materials, training of response forces, mock drills, community awareness.
3. **Response**: Actions taken during and immediately after a disaster to save lives, reduce health impacts, ensure public safety, and meet the basic subsistence needs of the affected people. Examples: search and rescue, medical care, temporary shelter, food and water supply, restoration of critical infrastructure.
4. **Recovery and Reconstruction**: Restoration and improvement of facilities, livelihoods, and living conditions of disaster-affected communities, including efforts to reduce disaster risk factors. Build Back Better is an important principle.

The cycle emphasises that disaster management is not only about response; investment in prevention, mitigation, and preparedness is more cost-effective and saves lives.

## Early Warning Systems

India has developed significant capacity in early warning for cyclones, floods, tsunamis, and weather-related events through institutions such as the India Meteorological Department (IMD), the Central Water Commission, the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS), and others. Effective early warning, combined with last-mile connectivity and community preparedness, has dramatically reduced mortality in cyclones in recent years.

## Community-Based Disaster Management

Communities are the first responders in any disaster. Community-based disaster management emphasises the participation of local communities in risk assessment, planning, preparedness, and response. Village Disaster Management Plans, training of volunteers, and involvement of local self-government institutions (Panchayats and urban local bodies) are important elements. The role of civil society organisations and the private sector is also recognised.

## Climate Change and Disaster Risk

Climate change is altering the frequency, intensity, and spatial distribution of extreme weather events. Rising temperatures, changing monsoon patterns, sea-level rise, and glacier melt increase disaster risk. Disaster risk reduction and climate change adaptation are closely linked. Integrating these agendas in development planning is essential for building resilience.

## Challenges in Disaster Management in India

- Enforcement of building codes and land-use regulations remains weak in many areas.
- Urban flooding has become a major challenge due to unplanned growth and inadequate drainage.
- Coordination among multiple agencies and levels of government can be complex.
- Last-mile connectivity of early warnings and preparedness in remote and vulnerable communities needs continuous strengthening.
- Recovery and reconstruction sometimes recreate the same vulnerabilities.
- Data, research, and risk assessment capacity need further improvement.
- Financing for mitigation (as opposed to response) remains limited relative to needs.

## Key Points to Remember

- Disaster Management Act, 2005 is the principal legislation.
- NDMA is chaired by the Prime Minister; SDMA by the Chief Minister; DDMA by the District Collector.
- NDRF is the specialised response force.
- Paradigm shift from relief-centric to prevention, mitigation, and preparedness.
- Disaster management cycle: prevention/mitigation, preparedness, response, recovery.
- India is highly multi-hazard prone; vulnerability is increased by socio-economic factors.
- Early warning systems have significantly reduced mortality in cyclones.
- Community participation and Build Back Better are key principles.
- Climate change is a risk multiplier for disasters.

Disaster management is an integral part of sustainable development. Reducing disaster risk protects development gains, saves lives, and builds the resilience of people and systems in the face of an uncertain and changing risk landscape.
