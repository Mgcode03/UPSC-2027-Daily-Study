# The Sangam Age and Early South Indian Kingdoms

## Introduction to the Sangam Age

The Sangam Age refers to the period of early Tamil literature and the political and social life of the Tamil-speaking regions of South India, roughly from the 3rd century BCE to the 3rd century CE. The term “Sangam” means an assembly or academy of Tamil poets and scholars that is said to have flourished under the patronage of the Pandya kings of Madurai. Three Sangams are traditionally mentioned in later literature, though the historicity of the first two is debated; the third Sangam is associated with the extant corpus of Sangam literature.

Sangam literature provides the primary source for reconstructing the history, society, economy, and culture of early Tamilakam (the Tamil country). It consists of poems collected in anthologies such as the Ettuttokai (Eight Anthologies) and Pattuppattu (Ten Idylls), along with the Tolkappiyam (a work on grammar and poetics) and later didactic works like the Tirukkural.

## Geographical and Political Setting

Tamilakam roughly corresponded to the modern states of Tamil Nadu and Kerala, and parts of southern Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka. The region was divided among three major dynasties known as the Muvendar (Three Crowned Kings):

1. **The Cheras**: Ruled over the western coast (Kerala region) with capitals at Vanji (Karur) and later other centres. Their emblem was the bow. They controlled the ports of Muziris (Muchiri) and Tondi, important for Roman trade.
2. **The Cholas**: Dominated the Kaveri delta region with capitals at Uraiyur and later Puhar (Kaveripattinam). Their emblem was the tiger. They were known for naval strength and overseas contacts.
3. **The Pandyas**: Ruled the southern part with capital at Madurai. Their emblem was the fish (carp). They patronised the Sangam assemblies and controlled the pearl fisheries of the Gulf of Mannar.

Besides these three, there were numerous minor chieftains called Velir who controlled smaller territories and often acted as intermediaries or rivals.

## Sources for the Sangam Age

- **Sangam literature**: Poems classify life into akam (interior/love themes) and puram (exterior/war, heroism, ethics). They describe kings, battles, landscapes, trade, and social customs.
- **Archaeological evidence**: Megalithic burial sites, Roman pottery (rouletted ware, amphorae), coins, and port remains at Arikamedu, Puhar, and Muziris.
- **Foreign accounts**: Greek and Roman writers such as the author of the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, Ptolemy, and Pliny mention South Indian ports, trade in pepper, pearls, and textiles, and the names of local rulers.
- **Inscriptions**: Early Brahmi and Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions, Ashokan edicts mentioning the southern kingdoms, and later copper plates.

## Political Organisation

Kingship was hereditary and the king was the central figure. He was expected to be a warrior, protector of dharma, and generous patron of poets and temples. The ideal of the hero-king (puram ideal) is prominent in the literature. Royal assemblies, ministers, and local assemblies (sabha, ur, nagaram) played roles in administration, though details are limited. Revenue came from land, trade, and plunder. Warfare among the three kingdoms and with Velir chieftains was frequent; cattle raids and heroic battles are celebrated in poems.

## Society and Social Structure

Sangam society was organised around the concept of tinai – eco-zones or landscapes associated with particular occupations, deities, and emotional themes:

- Kurinji (hills) – hunting, gathering
- Mullai (pastoral) – cattle rearing
- Marutam (agricultural plains) – farming
- Neytal (coastal) – fishing, salt, trade
- Palai (dry/arid) – associated with separation and hardship

Social groups included:

- Ruling elites and warriors (arasar, velir)
- Brahmins (who received land grants and performed rituals)
- Farmers, herders, artisans, merchants
- Occupational groups such as fishermen, salt manufacturers, and weavers

Women appear in literature as poets, queens, and participants in social life. The institution of marriage and ideals of chastity (karpu) are discussed. Slavery and war captives are mentioned. Caste as a rigid varna system is less pronounced than in later periods, though distinctions based on occupation and status existed.

## Economy and Trade

Agriculture formed the backbone, especially in the fertile river valleys (Kaveri, Vaigai, Periyar). Rice was the staple. Cattle wealth was important. Crafts included weaving (cotton and silk), metalwork, bead-making, and pearl diving.

Long-distance trade was a defining feature. South India exported pepper, pearls, textiles, ivory, and precious stones to the Roman Empire, Southeast Asia, and other regions. Imports included gold, silver, wine, and luxury goods. The ports of Muziris, Puhar, Korkai, and Arikamedu flourished. Roman gold coins found in large numbers testify to the volume of trade. Internal trade was facilitated by roads and markets.

## Religion and Culture

Religion was a mix of indigenous and northern influences. Early deities included Murugan (the hill god, later identified with Kartikeya), Mayon (Vishnu), Seyon, and local mother goddesses. Hero stones (natukal) commemorated fallen warriors. Buddhism and Jainism had a presence, especially in urban centres and among merchants. Brahmanical rituals and the worship of Vedic gods also appear.

Cultural life centred on poetry, music, and dance. The Sangam poets (both men and women) composed in classical Tamil. The Tolkappiyam codified literary conventions. Performing arts and festivals were part of social life.

## Decline of the Sangam Age and Transition

By the 3rd–4th centuries CE, the classical Sangam polities declined. Possible factors include the disruption of Roman trade, internal conflicts, and the rise of new powers. The Kalabhra interregnum (sometimes called a “dark age” in traditional accounts) intervened before the rise of the later Pallavas, Pandyas, and Cholas in the early medieval period.

## Key Features of Early South Indian Kingdoms beyond Sangam

After the Sangam period, the Pallavas of Kanchi (c. 3rd–9th centuries CE) emerged as a major power in northern Tamil Nadu, known for rock-cut architecture (Mahabalipuram), temple building, and patronage of Sanskrit and Tamil. The early medieval Pandyas and the imperial Cholas (from the 9th century) built upon earlier foundations, developing sophisticated administration, temple-centred economy, and maritime empire.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Sangam Age: roughly 3rd century BCE – 3rd century CE; Tamilakam; sources = Sangam literature + archaeology + foreign accounts.
- Three crowned kings (Muvendar): Chera (bow), Chola (tiger), Pandya (fish).
- Tinai system: five eco-zones linking landscape, occupation, and emotion.
- Economy: agriculture + extensive overseas trade (pepper, pearls, textiles) with Rome and Southeast Asia.
- Society: relatively flexible compared with later rigid caste; women poets and participation visible.
- Religion: indigenous deities (Murugan prominent) + Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain elements.
- Cultural peak: classical Tamil poetry; Tolkappiyam as foundational text.

## Conceptual Notes

The Sangam Age represents one of the earliest well-documented phases of South Indian history independent of the northern Gangetic focus. It demonstrates that complex political organisation, long-distance trade, sophisticated literature, and urban centres existed in the far south centuries before the large medieval empires. Understanding Sangam society helps correct the earlier historiographical imbalance that treated South India as peripheral. The continuity of certain cultural and political motifs (temple patronage, maritime orientation, Tamil literary tradition) from Sangam times into later periods is a key theme in the historical geography of the region.
