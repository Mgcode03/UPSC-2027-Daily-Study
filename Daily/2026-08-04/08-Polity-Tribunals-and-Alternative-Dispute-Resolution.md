# Tribunals and Alternative Dispute Resolution Mechanisms

## Concept of Tribunals

Tribunals are specialised quasi-judicial bodies established to adjudicate disputes in specific fields. They are intended to provide speedy, inexpensive and expert resolution of disputes that would otherwise burden the regular courts. Tribunals combine judicial and technical expertise and follow procedures that are relatively flexible compared with ordinary courts.

### Constitutional Basis

Articles 323A and 323B were inserted by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976.

- **Article 323A** empowers Parliament to constitute administrative tribunals for the adjudication of disputes relating to recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of any State or of any local or other authority.
- **Article 323B** empowers the appropriate legislature (Parliament or State Legislature) to constitute tribunals for the adjudication of disputes relating to matters specified in the article, such as taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to legislatures, food stuffs, and rent and tenancy rights.

The 42nd Amendment also sought to exclude the jurisdiction of High Courts and the Supreme Court over the decisions of these tribunals. However, in the landmark case of L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure of the Constitution. Consequently, the decisions of tribunals are subject to scrutiny by a Division Bench of the High Court within whose jurisdiction the tribunal falls.

### Major Tribunals in India

**Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)**  
Established under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985. It adjudicates disputes relating to service matters of central government employees and employees of certain public sector undertakings. It has a Principal Bench in New Delhi and several other benches across the country. The CAT is headed by a Chairman who is a retired High Court Judge or a person qualified to be a High Court Judge.

**State Administrative Tribunals**  
Several states have established their own administrative tribunals for state government employees.

**National Green Tribunal (NGT)**  
Established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. It deals with cases relating to environmental protection, conservation of forests and other natural resources, and enforcement of any legal right relating to the environment. It has the power to order relief and compensation for damages to persons and property and for restitution of the environment. The NGT has a Principal Bench in New Delhi and regional benches.

**Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT)**  
A quasi-judicial body that hears appeals against orders of income-tax authorities. It is the final fact-finding body in income-tax matters; appeals from its orders lie to the High Court only on substantial questions of law.

**Other Important Tribunals**  
- Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT)  
- Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)  
- National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)  
- Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs) and Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunals (DRATs)  
- Armed Forces Tribunal  
- Railway Claims Tribunal  
- Intellectual Property Appellate Board (now merged into High Courts in certain respects)

### Advantages of Tribunals

- Specialisation and technical expertise
- Speedier disposal of cases compared with ordinary courts
- Less formal and less expensive procedures
- Relief to the regular judiciary from the burden of specialised litigation

### Criticisms and Challenges

- Lack of uniformity in appointment, tenure and service conditions of members
- Perceived lack of independence in some tribunals because of executive influence in appointments and administration
- Inadequate infrastructure and vacancies leading to delays
- Multiplicity of tribunals creating confusion and forum shopping
- Questions about the quality of justice in some instances

The Supreme Court and various Law Commission reports have repeatedly emphasised the need for reforms to ensure the independence, efficiency and accountability of tribunals.

## Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)

Alternative Dispute Resolution refers to methods of resolving disputes without resorting to conventional litigation in courts. The objective is to provide quicker, cheaper and more amicable settlement of disputes while reducing the burden on the judiciary.

### Major ADR Mechanisms

**1. Arbitration**  
Arbitration is a process in which the disputing parties agree to submit their dispute to one or more neutral third parties (arbitrators) whose decision (award) is binding. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (as amended) governs arbitration in India. It is based on the UNCITRAL Model Law. Key features include party autonomy in the choice of arbitrators and procedure, limited grounds for court intervention, and the enforceability of arbitral awards.

**2. Conciliation**  
Conciliation is a non-binding process in which a neutral third party (conciliator) assists the parties in reaching a mutually acceptable settlement. The conciliator may make proposals for settlement. If the parties reach an agreement, it is reduced to writing and becomes binding as a settlement agreement.

**3. Mediation**  
Mediation is a voluntary, non-binding process in which a neutral mediator facilitates communication between the parties and helps them explore options for settlement. The mediator does not impose a solution. Mediation has been given statutory recognition and institutional support in recent years. The Mediation Act, 2023 provides a comprehensive legal framework for mediation in India, including pre-litigation mediation in certain cases.

**4. Negotiation**  
Negotiation is a direct discussion between the parties with a view to reaching a settlement without the involvement of a third party. It is the most informal ADR method.

**5. Lok Adalat**  
Lok Adalat (People’s Court) is a statutory forum under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. It provides for the amicable settlement of disputes through conciliation and persuasion. Awards of Lok Adalats are deemed to be decrees of civil courts and are final and binding. No appeal lies against a Lok Adalat award. Lok Adalats have been particularly successful in settling compoundable criminal cases, motor accident claims, bank recovery cases and matrimonial disputes.

**6. Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)**  
With the growth of e-commerce and digital transactions, online platforms for negotiation, mediation and arbitration are emerging. ODR has the potential to make dispute resolution more accessible and efficient.

### Advantages of ADR

- Speedier resolution
- Lower cost
- Flexibility of procedure
- Confidentiality
- Preservation of relationships (especially important in commercial and family disputes)
- Party autonomy and control over the process
- Reduction of backlog in courts

### Limitations

- Not suitable for all types of disputes (e.g., serious criminal cases, constitutional questions)
- Quality and training of neutrals vary
- Power imbalances between parties may affect fairness
- Limited discovery and evidence-taking compared with court proceedings
- Enforceability issues in some cross-border contexts

## Key Points for Retention

- Articles 323A and 323B provide the constitutional basis for tribunals.
- L. Chandra Kumar (1997) restored the power of judicial review of High Courts over tribunal decisions.
- Major tribunals include CAT, NGT, ITAT, NCLT, DRTs, etc.
- ADR methods include arbitration, conciliation, mediation, negotiation and Lok Adalat.
- The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and the Mediation Act, 2023 are the principal statutes.
- Lok Adalat awards have the status of civil court decrees and are final.

A clear conceptual grasp of tribunals and ADR is important for questions on access to justice, judicial reforms, and the institutional design of dispute resolution in India.
