# External Sector and Balance of Payments

## Concept of the External Sector

The external sector of an economy comprises all economic transactions of a country with the rest of the world. These transactions include trade in goods and services, income flows, unilateral transfers, and capital flows. The performance of the external sector is reflected in the Balance of Payments (BoP) accounts.

## Balance of Payments: Meaning and Structure

The Balance of Payments is a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world during a given period, usually a year. It is prepared according to the guidelines of the International Monetary Fund.

The BoP consists of two main accounts:

### 1. Current Account

The current account records transactions relating to the exchange of goods, services, income and unilateral transfers.

**Components of the Current Account:**

- **Merchandise Trade (Visible Trade)**: Exports and imports of physical goods. The difference between exports and imports of goods is called the Trade Balance or Balance of Trade. A surplus occurs when exports exceed imports; a deficit occurs when imports exceed exports.

- **Invisibles**:  
  - **Services**: Receipts and payments for services such as travel, transportation, insurance, software, business services, financial services, etc.  
  - **Income**: Investment income (interest, dividends, profits) and compensation of employees.  
  - **Transfers**: Unilateral transfers such as remittances from workers abroad, gifts, grants and donations.

The Current Account Balance is the sum of the trade balance and the balance on invisibles. A current account surplus means the country is a net lender to the rest of the world; a current account deficit means it is a net borrower.

### 2. Capital Account

The capital account records transactions that lead to a change in the assets or liabilities of residents vis-à-vis non-residents. It includes:

- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: Investment that gives the investor a lasting interest and control in an enterprise in another country.
- **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI)**: Investment in equity and debt securities that does not confer control.
- **External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs)**: Loans raised by domestic entities from foreign lenders.
- **External Assistance**: Loans and grants from foreign governments and international institutions.
- **Banking Capital**: Changes in the foreign assets and liabilities of commercial banks.
- **Other Capital**: Residual items including leads and lags in export receipts, etc.
- **Rupee Debt Service**: Repayment of debt denominated in rupees.
- **NRI Deposits**: Deposits of Non-Resident Indians.

In the present IMF framework, the capital account is often presented together with the financial account. The overall Balance of Payments is the sum of the current account and the capital/financial account. Any imbalance is settled through changes in the foreign exchange reserves of the central bank.

### Overall Balance and Reserve Changes

If the sum of the current and capital accounts is positive, there is an overall surplus and foreign exchange reserves increase. If it is negative, there is an overall deficit and reserves decrease (or the country borrows from the IMF or other sources). The change in reserves is shown as a below-the-line item so that the BoP always balances in an accounting sense.

## Exchange Rate

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. India follows a managed floating exchange rate system in which the Reserve Bank of India intervenes in the foreign exchange market to prevent excessive volatility while allowing the market to determine the broad trend.

**Types of Exchange Rate Regimes (conceptual):**
- Fixed exchange rate
- Floating exchange rate
- Managed floating (intermediate)

**Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER)** is the weighted average of the bilateral exchange rates of the home currency against a basket of currencies of major trading partners, weighted by trade shares.

**Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER)** is the NEER adjusted for inflation differentials between the home country and its trading partners. An increase in REER indicates appreciation of the currency in real terms and a possible loss of export competitiveness.

## Foreign Exchange Reserves

Foreign exchange reserves consist of:
- Foreign currency assets
- Gold
- Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)
- Reserve Tranche Position in the IMF

Reserves provide a cushion against external shocks, enable the central bank to intervene in the foreign exchange market, and enhance the confidence of international investors.

## Trade Policy and Convertibility

India’s trade policy has evolved from a highly protectionist regime to a more open and liberalised regime after 1991. Tariffs have been reduced, quantitative restrictions largely removed, and procedures simplified.

**Current Account Convertibility**: India achieved full current account convertibility in 1994. This means that residents can convert local currency into foreign currency and vice versa for all current account transactions without restriction.

**Capital Account Convertibility**: India has not yet adopted full capital account convertibility. Capital flows are regulated. There is gradual liberalisation of capital account transactions, but controls remain on certain types of capital outflows and inflows.

## Key Indicators and Concepts

- **Trade Openness**: Ratio of merchandise trade (exports + imports) to GDP.
- **Export Intensity**: Share of exports in GDP.
- **Import Intensity**: Share of imports in GDP.
- **Terms of Trade**: Ratio of export prices to import prices. An improvement in terms of trade means a country can obtain more imports for the same volume of exports.
- **External Debt**: Total debt owed by residents to non-residents. Indicators such as debt-GDP ratio, debt-service ratio and short-term debt to reserves ratio are monitored.
- **Net International Investment Position**: Difference between a country’s external financial assets and liabilities.

## Challenges and Issues

- Persistent merchandise trade deficit financed largely by services surplus and remittances.
- Volatility in capital flows, especially portfolio flows.
- Pressure on the exchange rate during periods of global risk aversion.
- Need to enhance export competitiveness through infrastructure, logistics, skills and technology.
- Managing external debt sustainability.
- Geopolitical risks and supply-chain disruptions affecting trade.

Understanding the structure of the Balance of Payments, the nature of capital flows, and the exchange rate regime is essential for analysing India’s external sector stability and for answering questions on economic reforms, trade policy and globalisation.
