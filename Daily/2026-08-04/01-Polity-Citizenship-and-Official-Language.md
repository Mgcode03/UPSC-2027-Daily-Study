# Citizenship and Official Language in the Indian Constitution

## Concept of Citizenship

Citizenship denotes the legal membership of a person in a state. It creates a permanent political relationship between the individual and the state, conferring both rights and duties. In a democratic republic such as India, citizenship is the foundation of political rights including the right to vote, the right to contest elections, and the right to hold public office under certain conditions.

The Constitution of India deals with citizenship in Part II (Articles 5 to 11). These articles determine who became a citizen of India at the commencement of the Constitution on 26 January 1950 and empower Parliament to regulate the right of citizenship by law thereafter. The detailed law on acquisition and termination of citizenship is contained in the Citizenship Act, 1955, as amended from time to time.

### Citizenship at the Commencement of the Constitution

Article 5 provides that every person who had his domicile in the territory of India and satisfied any one of the following three conditions became a citizen of India on 26 January 1950:

- He was born in the territory of India, or
- Either of his parents was born in the territory of India, or
- He had been ordinarily resident in the territory of India for not less than five years immediately preceding the commencement of the Constitution.

Article 6 deals with the rights of citizenship of persons who migrated to India from Pakistan. A person who migrated from Pakistan to India before 19 July 1948 became a citizen if he or either of his parents or any of his grandparents was born in undivided India. If he migrated on or after 19 July 1948, he had to register himself as a citizen of India before a prescribed authority.

Article 7 provides for the rights of citizenship of certain persons of Indian origin who migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947 but returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return.

Article 8 confers citizenship rights on persons of Indian origin residing outside India. Any person who or either of whose parents or grandparents was born in undivided India and who is ordinarily residing in any country outside India can register as a citizen of India with the diplomatic or consular representative of India in that country.

Article 9 states that no person shall be a citizen of India if he has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of any foreign state.

Article 10 provides for the continuance of the rights of citizenship. Every person who is or is deemed to be a citizen of India under any of the foregoing provisions shall continue to be such citizen, subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament.

Article 11 empowers Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship.

### Modes of Acquisition of Citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955

The Citizenship Act, 1955 provides five modes of acquiring Indian citizenship:

1. **Citizenship by Birth**  
   A person born in India on or after 26 January 1950 but before 1 July 1987 is a citizen by birth irrespective of the nationality of his parents. A person born in India on or after 1 July 1987 but before the commencement of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003 is a citizen by birth if either of his parents is a citizen of India at the time of his birth. A person born in India on or after the commencement of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003 is a citizen by birth only if both of his parents are citizens of India or one of his parents is a citizen of India and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of his birth.

2. **Citizenship by Descent**  
   A person born outside India on or after 26 January 1950 but before 10 December 1992 is a citizen of India by descent if his father was a citizen of India at the time of his birth. A person born outside India on or after 10 December 1992 is a citizen by descent if either of his parents is a citizen of India at the time of his birth. After the 2003 amendment, registration of the birth at an Indian consulate within one year is generally required for persons born outside India.

3. **Citizenship by Registration**  
   Certain categories of persons can apply for citizenship by registration. These include persons of Indian origin who are ordinarily resident in India for seven years, persons married to Indian citizens and ordinarily resident in India for seven years, minor children of Indian citizens, and persons of full age and capacity whose parents are registered as citizens of India. The Central Government may also register a person as a citizen if it is satisfied that special circumstances exist.

4. **Citizenship by Naturalisation**  
   A foreigner can acquire citizenship by naturalisation if he satisfies certain qualifications prescribed in the Third Schedule to the Act. These include residence in India for twelve months immediately preceding the application and for eleven years out of the fourteen years preceding the twelve months, good character, adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule, and intention to reside in India or to serve under the Government of India. The Government may waive some of these conditions in special cases.

5. **Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory**  
   If any territory becomes a part of India, the Central Government may, by order notified in the Official Gazette, specify the persons who shall be citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory.

### Loss of Citizenship

Citizenship can be lost in three ways:

- **Renunciation**: A citizen of full age and capacity can make a declaration renouncing his Indian citizenship. Upon registration of the declaration he ceases to be a citizen. If a person renounces citizenship while a minor, the minor child also ceases to be a citizen, though the child can resume citizenship on attaining majority.

- **Termination**: When a citizen of India voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, he automatically ceases to be a citizen of India.

- **Deprivation**: The Central Government may deprive a person of Indian citizenship if it is satisfied that citizenship was obtained by fraud, false representation or concealment of material fact, or that the person has shown himself by act or speech to be disloyal towards the Constitution of India, or has unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy during war, or has been ordinarily resident outside India for seven continuous years without registering annually, or has been sentenced to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years within five years of registration or naturalisation.

### Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIO)

The concept of dual citizenship in a limited form was introduced through the Overseas Citizen of India scheme. An OCI cardholder enjoys certain rights such as multiple entry lifelong visa, exemption from registration with the Foreigners Regional Registration Office for any length of stay, and parity with Non-Resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields. OCI does not confer political rights. The PIO scheme was later merged with the OCI scheme.

### Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 amended the Citizenship Act, 1955 to provide that persons belonging to Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi or Christian communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh or Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 shall not be treated as illegal migrants. Such persons are eligible for citizenship by naturalisation after a reduced period of residence of five years instead of eleven years. The Act does not apply to the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura included in the Sixth Schedule and to the areas covered under the Inner Line Permit system.

## Official Language of the Union and the States

### Constitutional Provisions

Part XVII of the Constitution (Articles 343 to 351) deals with the official language.

Article 343 declares that the official language of the Union shall be Hindi in Devanagari script. The form of numerals to be used for the official purposes of the Union shall be the international form of Indian numerals. However, for a period of fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution, the English language shall continue to be used for all the official purposes of the Union for which it was being used immediately before such commencement. Parliament may by law provide for the use of English language or the Devanagari form of numerals after the expiry of fifteen years.

Article 344 provides for the constitution of a Commission and a Committee of Parliament on Official Language to make recommendations regarding the progressive use of Hindi, restrictions on the use of English, and other related matters.

Article 345 allows the Legislature of a State to adopt any one or more of the languages in use in the State or Hindi as the language or languages to be used for all or any of the official purposes of that State.

Article 346 provides that the language for the time being authorised for use in the Union for official purposes shall be the official language for communication between one State and another State and between a State and the Union. If two or more States agree that Hindi should be the official language for communication between such States, that language may be used.

Article 347 empowers the President to direct that a language spoken by a substantial proportion of the population of a State shall also be officially recognised throughout that State or any part thereof.

Articles 348 and 349 deal with the language to be used in the Supreme Court and High Courts and for Acts, Bills, etc. Until Parliament provides otherwise, all proceedings in the Supreme Court and every High Court and the authoritative texts of all Bills, Acts, Orders, Rules, etc., shall be in the English language.

Article 350 ensures that every person is entitled to submit a representation for the redress of any grievance to any officer or authority of the Union or a State in any of the languages used in the Union or in the State, as the case may be.

Article 350A directs every State and every local authority within the State to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage of education to children belonging to linguistic minority groups.

Article 350B provides for the appointment of a Special Officer for linguistic minorities by the President. It is the duty of the Special Officer to investigate all matters relating to the safeguards provided for linguistic minorities under the Constitution.

Article 351 directs the Union to promote the spread of the Hindi language, to develop it so that it may serve as a medium of expression for all the elements of the composite culture of India, and to secure its enrichment by assimilating the forms, style and expressions used in Hindustani and in the other languages of India specified in the Eighth Schedule.

### Eighth Schedule

The Eighth Schedule originally contained fourteen languages. Through successive amendments the number has risen to twenty-two. The languages are: Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santhali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu.

Inclusion of a language in the Eighth Schedule does not automatically confer any special status or financial benefits, but it enables the language to be represented on the Official Language Commission and to be developed under Article 351.

### Official Language Act, 1963 and Subsequent Developments

The Official Language Act, 1963 was enacted to provide for the continued use of English beyond the fifteen-year period. It provides that English shall continue to be used in addition to Hindi for all official purposes of the Union and for the transaction of business in Parliament. It also provides for the use of Hindi or English in communication between the Union and the States that have not adopted Hindi as their official language.

The Official Language Rules, 1976, as amended, classify States into three regions (A, B and C) and prescribe the extent of use of Hindi and English in correspondence between the Central Government offices and the States and among the Central Government offices themselves.

### Key Points for Retention

- Citizenship is governed by Part II of the Constitution and the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- Five modes of acquisition: birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory.
- Three modes of loss: renunciation, termination, deprivation.
- Official language of the Union is Hindi in Devanagari script; English continues under the Official Language Act, 1963.
- Eighth Schedule contains twenty-two languages.
- Special provisions exist for linguistic minorities and for the development of Hindi.

Understanding these provisions is essential for both Prelims factual questions and Mains analytical answers on issues of national integration, linguistic diversity and federal relations.
