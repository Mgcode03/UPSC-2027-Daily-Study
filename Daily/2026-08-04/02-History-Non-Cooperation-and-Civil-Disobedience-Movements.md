# Non-Cooperation Movement and Civil Disobedience Movement

## Background to the Non-Cooperation Movement

The period immediately after the First World War witnessed a series of developments that prepared the ground for a mass movement under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi. The Rowlatt Act of 1919 authorised the government to imprison any person without trial and trial without jury. The widespread protest against the Act culminated in the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 13 April 1919 at Amritsar, where troops under General Dyer fired on an unarmed gathering, killing hundreds. The Hunter Committee report was widely regarded as inadequate. Simultaneously, the Khilafat issue arising from the treatment of the Ottoman Caliph by the Allied powers after the war agitated the Muslim community in India. Gandhi saw in the Khilafat question an opportunity to unite Hindus and Muslims in a common struggle against colonial rule.

The Indian National Congress, at its special session in Calcutta in September 1920, accepted Gandhi’s programme of non-cooperation. The Nagpur session in December 1920 confirmed the decision and restructured the Congress organisation to make it a mass organisation with a network of committees down to the village level.

## Programme of Non-Cooperation

The Non-Cooperation Movement had a dual programme of constructive work and boycott.

**Boycott of:**
- Government titles, honours and honorary offices
- Civil services, army, police and courts
- Government schools and colleges
- Legislative councils
- Foreign goods, especially cloth

**Constructive programme included:**
- Establishment of national schools and colleges
- Promotion of khadi and spinning
- Hindu-Muslim unity
- Removal of untouchability
- Boycott of liquor shops
- Promotion of communal harmony

The movement began with the surrender of titles and the boycott of the visit of the Prince of Wales in 1921. Students left government schools and colleges in large numbers. Lawyers suspended their practice. The boycott of foreign cloth was particularly successful; bonfires of foreign cloth became a common sight. The import of foreign cloth fell sharply.

## Course of the Movement and its Suspension

The movement gained momentum throughout 1921. In many places it took a radical turn. The visit of the Prince of Wales was met with hartals and protests. In some areas peasants and workers organised themselves under the Congress or Khilafat banner and raised local demands.

The movement was suspended by Gandhi in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident in Gorakhpur district of the United Provinces. A procession of peasants was fired upon by the police; the crowd retaliated by setting fire to the police station, killing twenty-two policemen. Gandhi, committed to non-violence, felt that the country was not yet ready for mass civil disobedience and called off the movement. The decision was criticised by many leaders including C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, but Gandhi insisted on the primacy of non-violence.

## Significance of the Non-Cooperation Movement

- It was the first truly mass movement on an all-India scale involving students, peasants, workers, women and the urban middle class.
- It demonstrated the capacity of the Congress to mobilise people under a common programme.
- It brought the urban and rural masses into the national movement.
- It popularised the ideas of swadeshi, khadi and non-violence.
- It created a cadre of workers who later became the backbone of the freedom struggle.
- The Hindu-Muslim unity achieved during the movement, though temporary, was significant.
- The movement marked the transformation of the Congress from an elite organisation into a mass organisation.

## The Civil Disobedience Movement

After the suspension of Non-Cooperation, the political atmosphere remained subdued until the late 1920s. The Simon Commission of 1927, appointed without any Indian member, was boycotted with the slogan “Simon Go Back”. The Nehru Report of 1928 demanded dominion status. When the British government failed to give a clear commitment, the Lahore Congress of December 1929 under the presidentship of Jawaharlal Nehru declared complete independence (Purna Swaraj) as the goal and authorised the launching of civil disobedience.

### The Salt March and the Beginning of Civil Disobedience

Gandhi chose salt as the symbol of the movement because it was a basic necessity of life and the salt tax affected every Indian. On 12 March 1930 Gandhi began the historic Dandi March from Sabarmati Ashram with seventy-eight chosen followers. After covering 240 miles in twenty-four days, he reached Dandi on 5 April and on 6 April violated the salt law by picking up a handful of salt. This act was the signal for mass civil disobedience throughout the country.

The programme of the Civil Disobedience Movement included:
- Violation of salt laws
- Boycott of foreign cloth and liquor
- Non-payment of taxes in selected areas
- Boycott of government institutions
- Defiance of forest laws in some regions
- No-rent campaigns in certain areas

Women participated in large numbers for the first time. Sarojini Naidu, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Kasturba Gandhi and many others led processions and courted arrest. Students and youth played an active role. In the North-West Frontier Province, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan organised the Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts) on non-violent lines.

### Government Response and the Gandhi-Irwin Pact

The government responded with severe repression. Ordinances were promulgated, Congress organisations were banned, and leaders were arrested. Over 90 000 people were imprisoned. Despite repression the movement continued.

In March 1931 the Gandhi-Irwin Pact was signed. The government agreed to release political prisoners not convicted of violence, to permit peaceful picketing of liquor and foreign cloth shops, and to allow collection of salt by people living in coastal areas. Gandhi agreed to suspend the Civil Disobedience Movement and to participate in the Second Round Table Conference.

### Second Phase and Final Withdrawal

Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London in 1931 but returned empty-handed as the British government refused to concede the basic national demands. On his return the government launched a fresh wave of repression. The Civil Disobedience Movement was resumed in January 1932. It continued at a lower intensity until May 1934 when it was finally withdrawn. The Government of India Act 1935 was enacted in the meantime.

## Comparison of the Two Movements

| Aspect                  | Non-Cooperation (1920-22)              | Civil Disobedience (1930-34)                  |
|-------------------------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Immediate cause         | Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala, Khilafat     | Failure of dominion status demand, Salt Tax   |
| Method                  | Boycott and constructive programme     | Direct defiance of specific laws              |
| Symbol                  | Khadi and foreign cloth boycott        | Salt                                          |
| Hindu-Muslim unity      | Strong due to Khilafat                 | Weaker; separate electorates issue resurfaced |
| Participation of women  | Limited                                | Extensive and visible                         |
| Geographical spread     | Widespread but uneven                  | More intense in certain regions               |
| Outcome                 | Suspended after Chauri Chaura          | Suspended twice; led to Round Table Conferences |

## Key Facts for Retention

- Non-Cooperation was launched in 1920 and withdrawn in February 1922 after Chauri Chaura.
- Civil Disobedience began with the Dandi March on 12 March 1930 and salt law violation on 6 April 1930.
- Gandhi-Irwin Pact was signed in March 1931.
- The movement was finally withdrawn in 1934.
- Both movements expanded the social base of the national movement and established Gandhi as the undisputed leader of the masses.

These two movements represent the high points of Gandhian mass politics and remain central to the understanding of India’s freedom struggle.
