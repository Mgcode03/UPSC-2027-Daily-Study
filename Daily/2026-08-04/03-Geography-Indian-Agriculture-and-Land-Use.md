# Indian Agriculture and Land Use Patterns

## Importance of Agriculture in the Indian Economy

Agriculture remains the largest source of livelihood in India. Although its share in the Gross Domestic Product has declined over the decades, it continues to employ a substantial portion of the workforce. Agriculture provides raw materials to agro-based industries, contributes to export earnings, and sustains the rural economy. Food security of the nation depends critically on agricultural performance.

## Land Use Classification in India

The reporting area of India is classified into several categories for statistical purposes:

- **Forests**: Area under forests according to legal classification or recorded forest area.
- **Area under non-agricultural uses**: Land occupied by buildings, roads, railways, water bodies, etc.
- **Barren and unculturable land**: Land that cannot be brought under cultivation without prohibitive cost, such as deserts, mountains and rocky areas.
- **Permanent pastures and other grazing lands**: Land under permanent pastures.
- **Land under miscellaneous tree crops and groves**: Land under casuarina, thatching grass, bamboo, orchards, etc., that is not included in net sown area.
- **Culturable waste land**: Land available for cultivation but not cultivated for more than five years.
- **Fallow lands other than current fallow**: Land that has been left fallow for one to five years.
- **Current fallow**: Land left fallow during the current year.
- **Net sown area**: The total area sown with crops and orchards. Area sown more than once is counted only once.

**Gross cropped area** is the total area sown once or more than once in a particular year; it is the sum of net sown area and area sown more than once.

**Cropping intensity** is calculated as (Gross cropped area / Net sown area) × 100. Higher cropping intensity indicates more intensive use of land.

## Types of Farming in India

### Subsistence Farming
Most Indian farmers practise subsistence farming. The produce is mainly for family consumption and only a small surplus, if any, is marketed. Holdings are small and fragmented. Traditional tools and methods are still widely used, though modern inputs have increased.

### Commercial Farming
Commercial farming is practised in areas where farms are larger and the produce is mainly for the market. Plantation crops such as tea, coffee, rubber and spices are classic examples of commercial farming. In the irrigated plains of Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, wheat and rice are also grown largely for the market.

### Plantation Agriculture
Plantation agriculture involves the cultivation of a single crop on a large scale using modern methods and hired labour. It is capital intensive and export oriented. Tea in Assam and the Nilgiris, coffee in Karnataka, rubber in Kerala, and spices in the Western Ghats are important plantation crops.

### Mixed Farming
Mixed farming combines crop cultivation with livestock rearing. It provides additional income and helps in maintaining soil fertility through organic manure.

## Major Cropping Seasons

India has three main cropping seasons:

1. **Kharif Season**  
   Sowing is done with the onset of the south-west monsoon (June-July) and harvesting takes place in September-October. Major kharif crops are rice, maize, jowar, bajra, ragi, tur (arhar), moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut and soybean.

2. **Rabi Season**  
   Sowing is done in winter (October-December) and harvesting in summer (April-June). Major rabi crops are wheat, barley, gram, peas, mustard and linseed. Rabi crops require irrigation because the monsoon rains have already withdrawn.

3. **Zaid Season**  
   A short season between the rabi and kharif seasons. Crops grown include watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables and fodder crops. These crops require warm and dry weather.

## Major Crops and Their Distribution

### Food Grains

**Rice**  
Rice is the staple food of the majority of the population. It is a kharif crop that requires high temperature (above 25°C), high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm. It is grown in the plains of north and north-eastern India, coastal areas and the deltaic regions. West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Bihar and Odisha are major producers. In areas of less rainfall, rice is grown with the help of irrigation.

**Wheat**  
Wheat is the second most important food grain. It is a rabi crop that requires cool growing season and bright sunshine at the time of ripening. It requires 50 to 75 cm of annual rainfall evenly distributed. The Ganga-Satluj plains in the north-west and the black soil region of the Deccan are the two main wheat-growing zones. Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Bihar are the leading producers.

**Millets**  
Jowar, bajra and ragi are the important millets. They are also known as coarse grains and have high nutritional value. Jowar is grown in Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh. Bajra is grown in Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Haryana. Ragi is grown in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Jharkhand and Arunachal Pradesh.

**Maize**  
Maize is used both as food and fodder. It is a kharif crop that requires temperature between 21°C and 27°C and grows well in old alluvial soil. Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are major producers. In some states it is grown in the rabi season as well.

**Pulses**  
India is the largest producer and consumer of pulses. Pulses are the major source of protein in a vegetarian diet. Being leguminous crops, they help restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen. Major pulses are tur, urad, moong, masur, peas and gram. Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka are major pulse-producing states.

### Food Crops other than Grains

**Sugarcane**  
Sugarcane is a tropical as well as subtropical crop. It grows well in hot and humid climate with temperature of 21°C to 27°C and annual rainfall of 75 to 100 cm. It can be grown on a variety of soils. India is the second largest producer of sugarcane after Brazil. Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana are major producers.

**Oilseeds**  
India is the largest producer of oilseeds in the world. Different oilseeds are grown in both kharif and rabi seasons. Groundnut, mustard, coconut, sesamum (til), soybean, castor seeds, cotton seeds, linseed and sunflower are the main oilseeds. Groundnut is a kharif crop and accounts for about half of the major oilseeds produced. Gujarat, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu are major groundnut producers. Mustard is a rabi crop grown mainly in Rajasthan, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal.

### Fibre Crops

**Cotton**  
Cotton requires high temperature, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days and bright sunshine. It grows best on black cotton soil of the Deccan plateau. Major cotton-producing states are Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan. India has the largest area under cotton cultivation and is among the leading producers.

**Jute**  
Jute requires a hot and humid climate with temperature between 24°C and 35°C and heavy rainfall. It grows well on well-drained fertile soils in the flood plains. West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Odisha and Meghalaya are the major jute-producing states. India is the largest producer of raw jute and jute goods.

### Beverage Crops

**Tea**  
Tea is a beverage crop grown in tropical and subtropical climates endowed with deep and fertile well-drained soil, rich in humus and organic matter. It requires warm, moist and frost-free climate throughout the year. Major tea-producing states are Assam, the hills of Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Kerala. India is one of the leading producers of tea in the world.

**Coffee**  
India produces about four per cent of the world’s coffee. The Arabica variety brought from Yemen is produced in the country. Coffee is grown in the highlands of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

## Agricultural Development and Challenges

The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s introduced high-yielding varieties of seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and assured irrigation, particularly in the wheat and rice growing regions of north-western India. It transformed India from a food-deficit to a food-surplus nation. However, it also created regional imbalances, environmental problems such as soil degradation and groundwater depletion, and increased inequality among farmers.

Major challenges facing Indian agriculture include:
- Small and fragmented land holdings
- Dependence on monsoon
- Inadequate irrigation coverage
- Declining soil fertility
- Low productivity compared to many other countries
- Inadequate storage and marketing facilities
- Indebtedness of farmers
- Climate change and increasing frequency of extreme weather events

Land reforms, expansion of irrigation, soil health management, diversification of crops, strengthening of agricultural marketing, and promotion of sustainable agricultural practices remain critical for the future of Indian agriculture.
