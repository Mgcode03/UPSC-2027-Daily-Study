# Air Pollution: Causes, Effects and Control

## Nature of Air Pollution

Air pollution refers to the presence of substances in the atmosphere at concentrations that are harmful to human health, living organisms, ecosystems or materials. Pollutants may be primary (emitted directly) or secondary (formed by chemical reactions in the atmosphere). Common pollutants include particulate matter (PM10 and PM2.5), nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulphur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O3), volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and lead.

Particulate matter, especially PM2.5, is of particular concern because fine particles can penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream.

## Major Sources of Air Pollution

**Anthropogenic Sources**

- Combustion of fossil fuels in power plants, industries and vehicles.
- Industrial processes (cement, steel, chemicals, refining).
- Vehicular emissions, especially in urban areas.
- Agricultural practices including stubble burning and use of fertilisers.
- Construction and road dust.
- Household combustion of solid fuels (biomass, coal) for cooking and heating.
- Waste burning.

**Natural Sources**

- Volcanic eruptions, dust storms, sea salt, pollen and forest fires. Natural sources can be significant regionally but anthropogenic sources dominate in densely populated and industrialised areas.

## Effects of Air Pollution

**Human Health**

Short-term exposure can cause respiratory irritation, asthma exacerbation and cardiovascular stress. Long-term exposure is linked to chronic respiratory diseases, reduced lung function, heart disease, stroke, lung cancer and adverse birth outcomes. Vulnerable groups include children, the elderly and those with pre-existing conditions.

**Environment and Ecosystems**

Acid rain from SO2 and NOx damages forests, soils and freshwater systems. Ground-level ozone harms vegetation and reduces crop yields. Particulate matter affects visibility and climate (some particles cool, others warm). Deposition of pollutants can alter nutrient balances in ecosystems.

**Materials and Economy**

Corrosion of buildings and monuments, damage to crops and reduced labour productivity impose economic costs. Healthcare expenditures and premature mortality represent major burdens.

## Air Quality Monitoring and Standards

Air quality is monitored through networks measuring concentrations of key pollutants. National Ambient Air Quality Standards specify limits for various pollutants. The Air Quality Index (AQI) communicates pollution levels to the public in an easily understandable form, with categories ranging from good to severe.

## Control Strategies and Measures

**Technological Measures**

- Flue gas desulphurisation and selective catalytic reduction in power plants and industries.
- Electrostatic precipitators and bag filters for particulate control.
- Catalytic converters and improved engine technology in vehicles.
- Transition to cleaner fuels (CNG, LPG, electric vehicles).
- Improved cookstoves and clean cooking fuels to address household pollution.

**Regulatory and Policy Measures**

- Emission standards for industries and vehicles.
- Zoning and siting regulations.
- Promotion of public transport, non-motorised transport and compact urban planning.
- Crop residue management alternatives to burning.
- Dust control at construction sites.
- Enforcement of standards and penalties for non-compliance.

**International Dimension**

Transboundary movement of pollutants requires regional cooperation. Climate policies that reduce fossil fuel use also deliver air quality co-benefits.

## Indian Context

Many Indian cities experience high levels of particulate pollution, especially in the Indo-Gangetic Plain during winter. Sources include vehicles, industry, construction, biomass burning and seasonal stubble burning. Initiatives include the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) targeting reduction in particulate concentrations, expansion of monitoring, promotion of electric mobility, cleaner industrial fuels and city-specific action plans. Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) mechanisms are used in the Delhi-NCR region to implement emergency measures based on pollution levels.

Household air pollution from solid fuels remains a significant issue in rural areas, addressed through schemes promoting LPG connections.

## Key Concepts

- Primary vs secondary pollutants.
- PM2.5 as a critical health-relevant pollutant.
- Major anthropogenic sources: combustion, industry, vehicles, agriculture, households.
- Health, environmental and economic impacts.
- Control through technology, regulation, planning and behavioural change.
- Importance of monitoring, standards and public communication (AQI).

## Summary

Air pollution is a major environmental and public health challenge. Effective control requires a combination of cleaner technologies, stringent standards, improved urban and agricultural practices, clean household energy and sustained institutional effort. Understanding sources, impacts and control options is essential for policy analysis and environmental management.
