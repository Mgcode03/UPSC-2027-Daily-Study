# Major Ethical Theories and Their Relevance to Public Service

## Introduction

Ethical theories provide systematic frameworks for determining what is right and wrong, and for guiding moral decision-making. In public service, civil servants frequently face situations involving conflicting values, competing interests and questions of fairness, duty and consequences. Familiarity with major ethical theories helps in analysing such situations rigorously and in articulating reasoned justifications for decisions.

## Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, holds that the right action is the one that maximises overall happiness or utility. Bentham’s version emphasises quantitative calculation of pleasure and pain; Mill introduced qualitative distinctions and the importance of higher pleasures.

In public policy, utilitarian reasoning appears in cost-benefit analysis, impact assessments and decisions that seek the greatest good for the greatest number. Challenges include the difficulty of measuring utility, the risk of sacrificing minority interests, and the question of whether all consequences can be anticipated.

For a civil servant, utilitarian thinking encourages focus on outcomes for citizens and society, but must be balanced against rights and justice considerations.

## Deontological Ethics

Deontology, most prominently associated with Immanuel Kant, focuses on duties and rules rather than consequences. An action is right if it is done from duty and conforms to moral law. Kant’s categorical imperative requires that one act only according to maxims that can be universalised, and that persons be treated as ends in themselves and never merely as means.

In public service, deontological ethics underpins adherence to rules, laws, codes of conduct and principles such as honesty, non-discrimination and respect for rights. It provides a strong foundation for integrity even when rule-breaking might appear to produce better short-term outcomes. Limitations include rigidity in complex situations and potential conflicts between duties.

## Virtue Ethics

Virtue ethics, rooted in Aristotle, emphasises the character of the moral agent rather than rules or consequences. The central question is “What kind of person should I be?” Virtues are excellences of character such as courage, temperance, justice, wisdom, honesty and compassion, developed through practice and habit.

For civil servants, virtue ethics highlights the importance of cultivating personal integrity, empathy, impartiality and public-spiritedness. A virtuous official is more likely to exercise sound judgement in novel situations where rules are silent or conflicting. The approach complements rule-based systems by focusing on the moral quality of the decision-maker.

## Rights-Based Approaches

Rights-based ethics emphasises the inherent rights of individuals—civil, political, economic, social and cultural. Actions and policies are evaluated according to whether they respect, protect and fulfil these rights. This perspective is central to constitutional governance and human rights frameworks.

In administration, it requires that decisions do not arbitrarily infringe rights, that due process is followed, and that vulnerable groups receive special attention. It provides a strong check against purely utilitarian calculations that might overlook individual entitlements.

## Care Ethics and Relational Approaches

Care ethics emphasises relationships, empathy, responsibility for particular others and the context of moral situations. It critiques abstract, impartial theories for neglecting the concrete webs of relationships in which people live. In public service, especially in welfare administration, education and health, an ethic of care encourages attentiveness to individual circumstances and the quality of interactions with citizens.

## Application in Public Service Dilemmas

Real situations often require integrating insights from multiple theories:

- A decision on resource allocation may use utilitarian analysis of overall benefit while respecting rights and ensuring fairness (justice as a virtue and a deontological constraint).
- Whistle-blowing involves duty to truth and public interest (deontology and utilitarianism) as well as personal courage (virtue).
- Implementing a policy that harms a small group for larger benefit requires careful rights analysis and mitigation measures.

Ethical decision-making frameworks used in training often combine identification of stakeholders, consequences, duties, rights and character considerations.

## Indian Philosophical Contributions

Indian traditions offer rich ethical resources. The concept of dharma encompasses duty, righteousness and the order that sustains society. The Bhagavad Gita discusses nishkama karma (action without attachment to fruits), which resonates with the ideal of duty-oriented public service. Buddhist ethics emphasises compassion, non-harming and mindfulness. These traditions enrich the toolkit available to public servants in the Indian context.

## Key Points

- Utilitarianism focuses on consequences and overall good.
- Deontology focuses on duties, rules and treating persons as ends.
- Virtue ethics focuses on character and moral excellence.
- Rights-based approaches prioritise inherent entitlements.
- Care ethics emphasises relationships and contextual responsibility.
- Effective public service ethics often draws on multiple frameworks.
- Indian concepts of dharma and nishkama karma offer complementary insights.

## Summary

Major ethical theories provide distinct but complementary lenses for moral reasoning. Utilitarianism directs attention to outcomes, deontology to duties and rights, virtue ethics to character, and care ethics to relationships. In the complex environment of public administration, the ability to analyse issues through these lenses and to integrate them judiciously is a core component of ethical competence.
