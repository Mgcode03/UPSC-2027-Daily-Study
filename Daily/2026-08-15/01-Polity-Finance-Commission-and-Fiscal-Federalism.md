# Finance Commission and Fiscal Federalism

## Introduction to Fiscal Federalism

Fiscal federalism refers to the division of financial powers and responsibilities between different levels of government in a federal system. In India, the Constitution establishes a clear framework for the distribution of taxation powers, expenditure responsibilities, and mechanisms for intergovernmental transfers. This arrangement ensures that both the Union and the States have adequate resources to perform their assigned functions while maintaining the unity and integrity of the nation.

The Indian model of fiscal federalism is characterised by a strong centralising tendency in taxation powers combined with significant expenditure responsibilities at the state level. This vertical imbalance is corrected through a system of transfers recommended by the Finance Commission and other institutional mechanisms.

## Constitutional Provisions Related to Finance

Part XII of the Constitution deals with finance, property, contracts and suits. Key articles include:

- Article 268: Duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States.
- Article 269: Taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to the States.
- Article 270: Taxes levied and collected by the Union and distributed between the Union and the States.
- Article 275: Grants from the Union to certain States.
- Article 280: Finance Commission.
- Article 282: Expenditure defrayable by the Union or a State out of its revenues.

The Seventh Schedule divides subjects into Union List, State List and Concurrent List. Most high-yielding taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs, GST) fall under the Union List or are shared, while states have primary responsibility for subjects like agriculture, police, public health and education that require substantial expenditure.

## Vertical and Horizontal Imbalances

Vertical imbalance arises because the Union has greater capacity to raise resources while States have greater expenditure responsibilities, especially in social sectors. Horizontal imbalance refers to differences in fiscal capacity and needs among different States arising from variations in population, area, terrain, economic development and demographic characteristics.

The Finance Commission addresses both these imbalances through its recommendations on tax devolution and grants-in-aid.

## Finance Commission: Composition and Functions

Article 280 provides for a Finance Commission to be constituted by the President every five years or earlier if necessary. The Commission consists of a Chairman and four other members appointed by the President. The Chairman is usually a person with experience in public affairs, while members are selected from among persons with specialised knowledge of finance, economics, administration or law.

The core functions of the Finance Commission are:

1. Distribution of the net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, and allocation of the respective shares among the States.
2. Principles governing grants-in-aid of the revenues of the States out of the Consolidated Fund of India.
3. Measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities on the basis of recommendations made by the State Finance Commissions.
4. Any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of sound finance.

The recommendations of the Finance Commission are made to the President, who lays them before both Houses of Parliament along with an explanatory memorandum on the action taken.

## Evolution of Tax Devolution

Over successive Finance Commissions, the share of States in the divisible pool of central taxes has generally increased. The Fourteenth Finance Commission recommended a sharp increase in the States' share to 42 per cent of the divisible pool. The Fifteenth Finance Commission maintained a high share while adjusting for the changed circumstances after the creation of the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, recommending 41 per cent for the remaining period.

The divisible pool consists of the net proceeds of all Union taxes except those specifically excluded (such as cesses and surcharges in certain contexts, and taxes on Union territories). The horizontal distribution among States is based on a formula that typically includes criteria such as population, area, income distance, demographic performance, tax effort and forest cover.

## Grants-in-Aid

Apart from tax devolution, the Finance Commission recommends grants-in-aid under Article 275. These may be general purpose grants to cover revenue deficits or specific purpose grants for sectors such as health, education, justice delivery, statistics or local body functioning. Grants provide flexibility to address specific needs that cannot be adequately captured through the formula-based tax devolution.

## State Finance Commissions

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments mandated the constitution of State Finance Commissions every five years to review the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities and to recommend the principles governing distribution of taxes, duties, tolls and fees between the State and the local bodies, as well as grants-in-aid. The Central Finance Commission takes into account the recommendations of State Finance Commissions while recommending measures to augment State Consolidated Funds for local bodies.

## GST and Fiscal Federalism

The introduction of the Goods and Services Tax through the 101st Constitutional Amendment fundamentally altered the fiscal landscape. GST is a concurrent tax with both Union and States having the power to levy it on a common base. The GST Council, a constitutional body, makes recommendations on tax rates, exemptions, model laws and other matters. The compensation mechanism for States for revenue shortfall during the transition period was an important feature that addressed federal concerns.

GST has increased the interdependence of Union and State finances and strengthened the role of cooperative federalism in tax policy.

## Challenges in Indian Fiscal Federalism

Several challenges persist:

- Growing use of cesses and surcharges that remain outside the divisible pool reduces the effective share of States.
- Conditional grants and centrally sponsored schemes sometimes constrain State autonomy.
- Differences in fiscal capacity among States create persistent horizontal imbalances.
- Issues related to debt sustainability and fiscal responsibility at both levels.
- The need to strengthen local government finances remains significant.
- Coordination between Finance Commission recommendations and Planning or NITI Aayog processes.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- Vertical fiscal imbalance is inherent in the Indian assignment of functions and resources.
- Finance Commission is a constitutional body under Article 280.
- Tax devolution is the primary channel of resource transfer.
- Grants-in-aid under Article 275 supplement formula-based transfers.
- GST represents a major shift towards concurrent taxation and cooperative federalism.
- State Finance Commissions are essential for strengthening local self-government finances.

## Summary

Fiscal federalism in India balances the need for national unity with the diversity of regional needs. The Finance Commission remains the principal institutional mechanism for ensuring equitable distribution of resources. Understanding its composition, mandate, criteria for distribution and the broader framework of tax assignment and expenditure responsibilities is essential for grasping the working of Indian federalism.
