# Electoral System and Representation in India

## Constitutional Framework of Elections

Part XV of the Constitution (Articles 324 to 329) deals with elections. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President in the Election Commission of India. The Commission is an independent constitutional body.

Article 325 provides that there shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency and that no person shall be ineligible for inclusion on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 lays down adult suffrage: every citizen who is not less than 18 years of age (reduced from 21 by the 61st Amendment) and is not otherwise disqualified is entitled to be registered as a voter.

Parliament is empowered to make laws relating to elections (Representation of the People Acts, 1950 and 1951). Delimitation of constituencies is governed by Delimitation Acts passed after each census (with the freeze on seat allocation between states extended by constitutional amendments).

## First-Past-the-Post System

India primarily uses the First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) or plurality system for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies. Under this system:

- The country/state is divided into single-member territorial constituencies.
- Each voter casts one vote for a candidate.
- The candidate who secures the highest number of votes in a constituency is declared elected, even if the candidate does not obtain an absolute majority of votes.

Advantages of FPTP:
- Simplicity and ease of understanding for voters.
- Provides a clear link between the representative and the constituency.
- Tends to produce stable governments by favouring larger parties and discouraging fragmentation.
- Facilitates accountability of the individual representative to the local electorate.

Disadvantages:
- Can lead to significant disproportionality between votes obtained by a party and seats won.
- Smaller parties and parties with geographically dispersed support find it difficult to win seats commensurate with their vote share.
- Wasted votes (votes cast for losing candidates) are high.
- Encourages focus on local rather than national issues in some contexts.

## Proportional Representation in India

Although FPTP is the dominant system, proportional representation is used in certain cases:

- Election of the President: proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
- Election of the Vice-President: same method.
- Election of members of the Rajya Sabha by the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies: proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
- Election of members of State Legislative Councils (where they exist) from certain constituencies also uses aspects of proportional representation.

The single transferable vote system allows voters to rank candidates in order of preference. Surplus votes of elected candidates and votes of eliminated candidates are transferred according to preferences until the required number of candidates is elected.

## Reservation of Seats

To ensure representation of historically disadvantaged groups, the Constitution provides for reservation of seats:

- In the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population (Articles 330 and 332). These reservations were originally for a limited period but have been extended by successive amendments.
- Nomination of not more than two members of the Anglo-Indian community to the Lok Sabha by the President if the community is not adequately represented (the provision for nomination has been discontinued by the 104th Amendment with effect from 2020).
- Reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in local bodies (Panchayats and Municipalities) along with reservation for women (not less than one-third of seats, including those reserved for SC/ST).

The 73rd and 74th Amendments made reservation for women in local bodies a constitutional mandate, significantly increasing women’s political participation at the grassroots.

## Role of the Election Commission

The Election Commission of India (ECI) is a permanent constitutional body. Originally a single-member body, it has been a multi-member body (Chief Election Commissioner and two Election Commissioners) since 1993. All have equal powers and the decisions are by majority.

Functions include:
- Preparation and revision of electoral rolls.
- Notification of election schedules.
- Recognition of political parties and allotment of symbols.
- Monitoring election expenditure and enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct.
- Conducting free and fair elections, including use of Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) and Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT).
- Advising the President/Governor on disqualification of members under the Tenth Schedule (anti-defection) in certain contexts and on other electoral matters.

The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only through a process similar to that of a Supreme Court Judge. Election Commissioners can be removed on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner. This provides a measure of independence.

## Electoral Reforms and Issues

Over the decades several reforms have been introduced:
- Lowering of voting age to 18.
- Introduction of EVMs to reduce booth capturing and invalid votes.
- VVPAT for verifiability.
- Disclosure of criminal antecedents, assets and educational qualifications of candidates (following Supreme Court directives).
- Restrictions on exit polls.
- None of the Above (NOTA) option.
- Ceiling on election expenditure (though enforcement remains challenging).
- Partial state funding in the form of free time on public broadcasters and certain facilities.

Persistent issues include:
- Criminalisation of politics (candidates with serious criminal cases).
- Role of money power and the challenge of regulating anonymous donations and electoral bonds (the electoral bond scheme was struck down by the Supreme Court).
- Misuse of religion and caste in campaigning.
- Need for inner-party democracy.
- Simultaneous elections debate (pros and cons of synchronising Lok Sabha and State Assembly elections).
- Strengthening the independence and capacity of the Election Commission further.

## Representation and Democratic Legitimacy

The electoral system determines how votes are translated into seats and therefore shapes the nature of representation. FPTP produces a direct constituency link and usually single-party or stable coalition governments, but at the cost of proportionality. Proportional systems used for the Rajya Sabha and presidential elections ensure that minority viewpoints within the electoral college obtain representation.

Reservation policies attempt to correct historical under-representation and make the political system more inclusive. The overall design reflects a balance between the values of effective government, local accountability, national integration and social justice.

## Key Points

- Article 324: Superintendence of elections by the Election Commission.
- Adult suffrage under Article 326 (voting age 18).
- FPTP for Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
- Proportional representation (single transferable vote) for President, Vice-President and Rajya Sabha.
- Reservation of seats for SC/ST in Lok Sabha and Assemblies; reservation for women in local bodies.
- Election Commission is a multi-member body; CEC removable like a Supreme Court Judge.
- Important reforms: EVMs, VVPAT, NOTA, disclosure requirements.

A sound understanding of the electoral system is essential for analysing the working of Indian democracy, the behaviour of political parties, and the quality of representation that citizens receive.
