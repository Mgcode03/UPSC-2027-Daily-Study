# Quit India Movement to Independence (1942–1947)

## Background to the Quit India Movement

By 1942 the Second World War had entered a critical phase. Japan had occupied large parts of Southeast Asia and was advancing towards the Indian border. The British government, under pressure from the United States and faced with the need for Indian cooperation in the war effort, sent the Cripps Mission in March 1942.

The Cripps Mission offered Dominion Status after the war, with the right of provinces to secede, and immediate participation of Indians in the Viceroy's Executive Council. The proposals were rejected by the Congress because they did not grant immediate transfer of power and because the provision for provincial secession was seen as a threat to unity. The Muslim League also rejected them because the idea of Pakistan was not explicitly accepted. The failure of the Cripps Mission left the Indian political leadership frustrated.

Simultaneously, Mahatma Gandhi had come to the conclusion that the British presence in India was a provocation to Japanese aggression and that the only way to defend the country was for the British to leave. He articulated the demand for immediate British withdrawal.

## Launch of the Quit India Movement

On 8 August 1942 the All-India Congress Committee met at Gowalia Tank Maidan (now August Kranti Maidan) in Bombay and passed the Quit India Resolution. The resolution demanded the immediate end of British rule in India. Gandhi gave the call of “Do or Die” in his speech, urging people to act as if they were already free and to use non-violent means to achieve freedom.

The British response was swift and severe. Within hours of the resolution being passed, almost the entire top leadership of the Congress, including Gandhi, Nehru, Patel and Azad, was arrested. The Congress was declared an unlawful organisation. With the leaders in jail, the movement passed into the hands of younger leaders, students, peasants and workers.

## Nature and Course of the Movement

The Quit India Movement was the most widespread and intense mass movement of the freedom struggle. It had several distinctive features:

- It was largely spontaneous after the initial arrests.
- Leadership was decentralised; underground organisations and local leaders directed activities.
- Methods included strikes, hartals, processions, sabotage of government property (especially communications – railways, telegraph lines, post offices), and establishment of parallel governments in some areas.
- Parallel governments functioned for varying periods in places such as Ballia (Uttar Pradesh), Tamluk (Midnapore, Bengal), Satara (Maharashtra) and parts of Bihar and Orissa.
- Students played a particularly active role; many left colleges to join the movement.
- Women participated in large numbers, organising processions and providing shelter to underground activists.

The British used unprecedented force. Thousands were killed in firings, villages were burnt, and collective fines were imposed. Over 60,000 people were arrested. The movement was suppressed by early 1944, but it had demonstrated that the British could no longer rule India without the consent of the Indian people.

## Significance of Quit India

The movement marked a decisive shift. It showed that the Congress still commanded mass support despite the absence of its leaders. It also revealed the depth of anti-British feeling across different sections of society. Internationally, it strengthened the argument that India could not be held by force. Although the movement itself was crushed, it left the British government convinced that a political settlement after the war was inevitable.

## Developments after 1942

While the Congress leadership remained in jail, other political forces were active. The Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah expanded its organisational base and popularised the demand for Pakistan. The Communist Party of India, which had supported the war effort after the Soviet Union was attacked, remained outside the Quit India framework. Subhas Chandra Bose organised the Indian National Army (INA) with Japanese support and tried to liberate India from the eastern front. Although the INA campaigns did not succeed militarily, the subsequent INA trials in 1945–46 generated massive public sympathy and further eroded British authority.

## Towards Transfer of Power

The end of the Second World War in 1945 changed the situation dramatically. A Labour government came to power in Britain under Clement Attlee. It was more sympathetic to Indian aspirations and recognised that Britain no longer had the resources or the will to hold India by force.

In 1945 the British government announced that elections would be held to the central and provincial legislatures and that a constitution-making body would be set up. The elections of 1945–46 showed a polarisation: the Congress won overwhelmingly in general constituencies while the Muslim League swept the Muslim seats. This strengthened the League’s claim to speak for Muslims and made the demand for Pakistan more difficult to ignore.

The Cabinet Mission of 1946 proposed a federal structure with a weak centre and grouping of provinces. Both Congress and League initially accepted the plan with reservations, but differences soon arose over the interpretation of the grouping clause. The League withdrew its acceptance and observed Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946, leading to large-scale communal violence, especially in Calcutta.

An Interim Government headed by Jawaharlal Nehru was formed in September 1946 with League participation later. The atmosphere remained charged with communal tension. In February 1947 Attlee announced that power would be transferred by June 1948. Lord Mountbatten was appointed Viceroy with the mandate to arrange an early transfer.

## Partition and Independence

Mountbatten advanced the date of transfer to 15 August 1947. The Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 provided for the partition of British India into two dominions – India and Pakistan – and the partition of Punjab and Bengal. Princely states were given the choice to accede to either dominion. The Indian Independence Act was passed by the British Parliament in July 1947.

On 14–15 August 1947 power was transferred. Pakistan came into existence on 14 August; India became independent on 15 August. The partition was accompanied by one of the largest migrations in human history and large-scale communal violence, particularly in Punjab and Bengal.

## Key Themes for Understanding

The period 1942–1947 illustrates several important historical processes:

- The transformation of the freedom struggle into a mass upsurge that made colonial rule untenable.
- The role of international factors (World War, change of government in Britain) in accelerating decolonisation.
- The failure of political leadership on both sides to prevent the communal partition despite decades of shared struggle against colonialism.
- The emergence of a new political geography of the subcontinent.

The Quit India Movement remains a landmark because it represented the final, most intense assertion of the demand for complete independence and demonstrated that the Indian people were prepared to make enormous sacrifices for freedom. The subsequent negotiations, though resulting in partition, brought the long colonial chapter to a close and opened the era of independent nation-building.

## Important Facts

- Quit India Resolution: 8 August 1942, Bombay.
- Gandhi’s slogan: “Do or Die”.
- Parallel governments: Ballia, Tamluk, Satara.
- Cripps Mission: March 1942.
- Cabinet Mission: 1946.
- Direct Action Day: 16 August 1946.
- Mountbatten Plan: 3 June 1947.
- Indian Independence Act: July 1947.
- Independence: 15 August 1947 (India), 14 August 1947 (Pakistan).

This phase of the freedom struggle combines mass mobilisation, high politics, communal polarisation and the final transfer of power. Mastery of its sequence, causes and consequences is essential for understanding the birth of modern India.
