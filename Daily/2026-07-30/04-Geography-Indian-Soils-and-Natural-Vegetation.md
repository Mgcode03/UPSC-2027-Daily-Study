# Geography: Indian Soils and Natural Vegetation

## Indian Soils

Soil is the thin layer of loose material on the Earth’s surface that supports plant life. Indian soils are classified on the basis of colour, texture, structure, chemical properties, and formation processes. The major soil types are:

### Alluvial Soils

Alluvial soils are the most widespread and important soils of India, covering about 40 per cent of the total area. They are formed by the deposition of sediments brought by rivers. They occur extensively in the Northern Plains (Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra), the deltas of the east coast, and parts of the river valleys of peninsular India.

Characteristics:
- Vary from sandy loam to clay.
- Rich in potash, phosphoric acid and lime; deficient in nitrogen and humus in many areas.
- Highly fertile and support intensive agriculture.
- Two subtypes: Khadar (new alluvium – finer, more fertile, deposited in floodplains) and Bhangar (old alluvium – higher upland, contains calcareous concretions called kankar).

Crops: Rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute, cotton, oilseeds, and a wide variety of other crops.

### Black Soils (Regur or Black Cotton Soils)

Black soils cover large parts of Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. They are formed from the weathering of basaltic rocks of the Deccan Trap.

Characteristics:
- Dark grey to black colour.
- High clay content; become sticky when wet and develop cracks when dry (self-ploughing property).
- Rich in iron, lime, calcium, magnesium, carbonates and alumina; poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter.
- Moisture-retentive; suitable for dry farming.

Crops: Cotton (hence the name), sugarcane, jowar, wheat, linseed, and tobacco.

### Red and Yellow Soils

These soils develop on crystalline igneous rocks in areas of low rainfall. They are found in large parts of Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and the eastern parts of Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra.

Characteristics:
- Reddish colour due to the presence of iron oxides; yellow when hydrated.
- Generally poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus.
- More fertile in the lower slopes and valleys where finer particles accumulate.

Crops: Millets, pulses, tobacco, oilseeds, potatoes, and rice (in irrigated areas).

### Laterite Soils

Laterite soils are formed under conditions of high temperature and high rainfall with alternating wet and dry periods. Intense leaching removes silica and leaves behind iron and aluminium oxides. They are found in the Western Ghats, Eastern Ghats, parts of Odisha, West Bengal, Assam, and the hills of the North-East.

Characteristics:
- Rich in iron and aluminium; poor in nitrogen, potash, lime and organic matter.
- Become soft when wet and hard when dry.
- Suitable for tree crops after proper treatment.

Crops: Tea, coffee, rubber, cashew, and tapioca. In some areas, laterite is cut and used as building material.

### Arid and Desert Soils

These soils are found in western Rajasthan and parts of Haryana, Punjab and Gujarat. They are sandy to gravelly, low in organic matter and nitrogen, and often saline or alkaline. Phosphate content may be adequate. With irrigation (as in the Indira Gandhi Canal command area), these soils can become productive.

### Forest and Mountain Soils

These soils are found in the hilly and mountainous regions of the Himalayas, Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats. They are heterogeneous in nature, vary with altitude and slope, and are generally thin and less fertile on steep slopes. In the valleys and lower slopes they support forests and agriculture. They are rich in humus in forested areas but may be acidic.

### Saline and Alkaline Soils

Also known as usar, reh, kallar or thur, these soils contain high proportions of sodium, potassium and magnesium. They occur in areas of high evaporation and poor drainage, especially in the irrigated tracts of the northern plains and in arid regions. Reclamation requires proper drainage, application of gypsum, and salt-tolerant crops.

### Peaty and Organic Soils

These soils are found in areas of heavy rainfall and high humidity with good growth of vegetation, such as parts of Kerala (Kari soils), the Sundarbans, and the coastal areas of Odisha and West Bengal. They are heavy, black, and highly acidic, with large amounts of organic matter.

## Soil Degradation and Conservation

Soil degradation in India results from deforestation, overgrazing, faulty agricultural practices, excessive irrigation leading to salinity and waterlogging, and industrial pollution. Soil conservation measures include contour ploughing, terracing, strip cropping, afforestation, controlled grazing, proper drainage, and the use of organic manures and bio-fertilisers.

## Natural Vegetation of India

Natural vegetation refers to plant life that grows naturally without human aid and has been left undisturbed for a long time. India’s vegetation is influenced by rainfall, temperature, soil and topography. The major types are:

### Tropical Evergreen and Semi-Evergreen Forests

Found in areas of heavy rainfall (more than 200 cm) and high temperatures – Western Ghats, North-Eastern hills, Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and upper parts of Assam.

Characteristics: Dense, multi-layered canopy; trees do not shed leaves simultaneously; valuable timber species such as rosewood, mahogany, ebony, aini, and bamboo. Semi-evergreen forests occur on the less rainy margins and contain a mixture of evergreen and deciduous species.

### Tropical Deciduous Forests (Monsoon Forests)

These are the most widespread forests of India. They occur in areas with rainfall between 70 cm and 200 cm.

- **Moist Deciduous**: Rainfall 100-200 cm. Found in the north-eastern states, along the foothills of the Himalayas, Jharkhand, West Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and the eastern slopes of the Western Ghats. Teak is the most important species; others include bamboo, sal, shisham, sandalwood, and kusum.

- **Dry Deciduous**: Rainfall 70-100 cm. Found in the plains of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and large parts of peninsular India. Trees shed leaves during the dry season. Species include teak, axlewood, tendu, bijasal, and palas. In many areas these forests have been cleared for agriculture and are now open scrub.

### Tropical Thorn Forests and Scrubs

Found in areas with rainfall less than 70 cm – north-western India (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh) and the rain-shadow regions of the Deccan. Vegetation consists of thorny trees and bushes such as acacias, palms, euphorbias and cacti. Plants have long roots, fleshy stems and small leaves to conserve water.

### Montane Forests

Vegetation changes with altitude in the Himalayas and other mountain ranges.

- **Wet Temperate Forests** (1000-2000 m): Evergreen broad-leaf trees such as oaks and chestnuts.
- **Temperate Forests** (1500-3000 m): Coniferous species – pine, deodar, silver fir, spruce, cedar. Also found in the higher hills of South India (Nilgiris, Anaimalai, Palani).
- **Alpine Vegetation** (above 3600 m): Silver fir, juniper, birch, and rhododendron, giving way to alpine pastures (bugyals) and ultimately to permanent snow.

### Littoral and Swamp Forests

Found along the coasts and in deltas. Mangrove (tidal) forests are particularly important in the Sundarbans (West Bengal and Bangladesh), the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri deltas, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. The Sundari tree is characteristic of the Sundarbans. These forests protect the coastline from erosion and storm surges and support rich biodiversity.

## Forest Cover and Conservation

According to the India State of Forest Report, forest and tree cover together account for roughly one-fourth of the geographical area. Dense forests, moderately dense forests and open forests are distinguished on the basis of canopy density. Conservation measures include the Forest Conservation Act, Wildlife Protection Act, Project Tiger, Project Elephant, Joint Forest Management, and the establishment of National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries and Biosphere Reserves.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Alluvial soils: most extensive, fertile, khadar and bhangar.
- Black soils: Deccan Trap, cotton, self-ploughing cracks.
- Red soils: crystalline rocks, iron oxides.
- Laterite: leaching, iron and aluminium, tree crops.
- Tropical evergreen: >200 cm rainfall, dense, multi-layered.
- Moist deciduous: teak dominant, 100-200 cm.
- Dry deciduous: 70-100 cm, leaf-shedding.
- Thorn forests: <70 cm, acacias and scrub.
- Mangroves: Sundarbans, coastal protection.
- Montane vegetation zones change with altitude.

## Conceptual Notes

Soil formation (pedogenesis) depends on parent rock, climate, vegetation, topography and time. In India, the monsoon climate with its distinct wet and dry seasons plays a major role in determining both soil characteristics and natural vegetation. The close relationship between rainfall and vegetation type is a classic example of climatic control on plant geography. Human interference through agriculture, grazing, urbanisation and industrialisation has significantly altered the original vegetation cover and has led to soil degradation in many regions. Sustainable management of soil and forest resources is essential for ecological balance, agricultural productivity and climate resilience.
