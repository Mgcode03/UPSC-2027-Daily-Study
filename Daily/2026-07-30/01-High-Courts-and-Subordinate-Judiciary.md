# High Courts and Subordinate Judiciary

## Introduction to the Judicial System at State Level

The Constitution of India establishes a unified and integrated judicial system. At the apex is the Supreme Court. Below it stand the High Courts, and further down operate the subordinate courts. This structure ensures that justice is accessible, hierarchical, and consistent across the country while allowing states a significant role in the administration of justice.

Article 214 of the Constitution provides that there shall be a High Court for each State. Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States or for a State and a Union Territory. Currently, India has 25 High Courts. Some High Courts have jurisdiction over more than one State or Union Territory. For example, the Guwahati High Court serves Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh. The Bombay High Court has jurisdiction over Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu. The Madras High Court covers Tamil Nadu and Puducherry.

## Composition and Appointment of High Court Judges

Every High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary. There is no fixed maximum number of judges for a High Court; the strength is determined by the President after consultation with the Chief Justice of India and the Governor of the concerned State.

The appointment of judges follows the collegium system. The Chief Justice of a High Court is appointed by the President after consultation with the Chief Justice of India and the Governor of the State. In the case of appointment of other judges, the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court is also consulted. The collegium for High Court appointments consists of the Chief Justice of India and two senior-most judges of the Supreme Court. For the appointment of a High Court Chief Justice, the collegium recommends names, often involving inter-state transfers to maintain institutional integrity and prevent local influences.

A person is qualified for appointment as a High Court judge if he or she is a citizen of India and has held a judicial office in India for at least ten years or has been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years. The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age, but by convention judges are appointed after substantial experience.

Judges of High Courts hold office until they attain the age of 62 years. They can resign by writing to the President. They can be removed only by the President on an address by both Houses of Parliament, supported by a special majority, on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The procedure is the same as for Supreme Court judges and is deliberately made difficult to protect judicial independence.

## Jurisdiction and Powers of High Courts

High Courts possess a wide range of jurisdiction: original, appellate, writ, and supervisory.

**Original Jurisdiction**: High Courts have original jurisdiction in certain matters such as admiralty, probate, matrimonial causes, and company law cases. The High Courts of Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, and Delhi also exercise original civil jurisdiction in higher-value suits. High Courts can also issue writs under Article 226.

**Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226)**: This is one of the most powerful features of High Courts. Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue directions, orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, to any person or authority, including the government, for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose. The phrase "for any other purpose" makes the writ jurisdiction of High Courts wider than that of the Supreme Court under Article 32, which is confined to Fundamental Rights. High Courts can issue writs against private persons or bodies if a public duty is involved or if Fundamental Rights are affected.

**Appellate Jurisdiction**: High Courts hear appeals from subordinate courts in both civil and criminal matters. In civil cases, appeals lie against decrees and certain orders of District Courts and lower courts. In criminal cases, appeals can be filed against convictions and sentences. High Courts also have revisional jurisdiction to examine the legality and propriety of orders passed by subordinate courts.

**Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227)**: Every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction (except those constituted under any law relating to the Armed Forces). This power allows the High Court to call for returns, make and issue general rules, prescribe forms, and settle tables of fees. It can also withdraw cases from subordinate courts and dispose of them itself or transfer them.

**Control over Subordinate Courts**: Under Article 235, the control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto, including the posting, promotion and grant of leave of persons belonging to the judicial service and holding any post inferior to the post of District Judge, is vested in the High Court. This is a key provision ensuring independence of the lower judiciary from the executive.

## Subordinate Judiciary

The subordinate courts form the base of the judicial pyramid and handle the bulk of litigation. The structure typically includes:

- District and Sessions Courts
- Civil Courts (Senior Civil Judge, Junior Civil Judge / Munsiff)
- Criminal Courts (Chief Judicial Magistrate, Judicial Magistrates of First and Second Class)
- Special Courts and Tribunals for specific matters (family courts, consumer courts, etc.)

**District Judge**: The District Judge is the highest judicial authority in a district. He or she exercises both civil and criminal jurisdiction (as Sessions Judge). Appointment of District Judges is made by the Governor in consultation with the High Court. A person must have been an advocate or pleader for at least seven years, or must be in the service of the Union or the State. The High Court has the power of posting, promotion and grant of leave of District Judges.

**Appointment of other Judicial Officers**: Recruitment to the subordinate judiciary is generally through competitive examinations conducted by State Public Service Commissions or High Courts, followed by interviews. The High Court exercises control over these officers under Article 235.

## Independence of the Judiciary

Several constitutional provisions safeguard the independence of High Courts and subordinate courts:

- Security of tenure
- Fixed service conditions that cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment
- Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State
- Prohibition on discussion of the conduct of judges in the State Legislature (except on a motion for removal)
- Control of the High Court over subordinate judiciary rather than the executive
- Separation of judiciary from executive in the public services of the State (Article 50, Directive Principle)

## Important Constitutional Articles Related to High Courts

- Article 214: High Courts for States
- Article 215: High Courts to be courts of record
- Article 216: Constitution of High Courts

- Article 222: Transfer of a Judge from one High Court to another
- Article 226: Power of High Courts to issue certain writs
- Article 227: Power of superintendence over all courts by the High Court
- Article 228: Transfer of certain cases to High Court
- Article 233: Appointment of District Judges
- Article 234: Recruitment of persons other than District Judges to the judicial service
- Article 235: Control over subordinate courts
- Article 236: Interpretation of terms "district judge" and "judicial service"

## Key Points for Memorisation

- High Court judges retire at 62; Supreme Court judges at 65.
- Writ jurisdiction of High Court under Article 226 is wider than Supreme Court’s under Article 32.
- Control over subordinate courts vests in the High Court (Article 235), not the State Government.
- High Courts are courts of record and can punish for their own contempt.
- A common High Court can be established for two or more States.
- The collegium system governs appointments and transfers.
- District Judges are appointed by the Governor in consultation with the High Court.
- Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

## Conceptual Clarity Notes

The integrated judicial system means that the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts (Article 141), and the law declared by a High Court is binding on all subordinate courts within its jurisdiction. This ensures uniformity while allowing High Courts to develop local jurisprudence suited to the conditions of their States.

The dual role of the High Court – as a court of appeal and as a constitutional court with writ powers – makes it a powerful institution for protecting rights and ensuring good governance at the State level. The supervisory power under Article 227 is administrative as well as judicial in nature and is frequently invoked to correct jurisdictional errors of tribunals and lower courts.

Understanding the distinction between Article 32 and Article 226 is crucial. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and can be suspended only during Emergency under specific conditions. Article 226 is a constitutional right but not a Fundamental Right; however, its wider scope makes High Courts the first port of call for most writ petitions.

The independence of the subordinate judiciary is as important as that of the higher judiciary because the majority of citizens interact only with the lower courts. The constitutional vesting of control in the High Court is designed to insulate these courts from executive interference in postings, transfers and disciplinary matters.
