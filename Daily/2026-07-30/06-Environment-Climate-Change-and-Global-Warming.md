# Environment: Climate Change and Global Warming

## Understanding Climate Change and Global Warming

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperatures and weather patterns. These shifts may be natural, but since the mid-twentieth century they have been primarily driven by human activities, particularly the burning of fossil fuels. Global warming is the long-term heating of the Earth’s climate system observed since the pre-industrial period due to human activities. It is the most prominent aspect of current climate change.

The Earth’s climate is regulated by the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) in the atmosphere trap some of the outgoing radiation, keeping the planet warm enough to support life. This natural greenhouse effect is essential. The problem arises when the concentration of GHGs increases significantly due to human activities, enhancing the greenhouse effect and leading to additional warming.

## Major Greenhouse Gases

- **Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)**: The most important anthropogenic GHG. Released primarily by burning of coal, oil and natural gas, and by deforestation and land-use change. It has a long atmospheric lifetime.

- **Methane (CH₄)**: More potent than CO₂ over a 20-year period but shorter-lived. Sources include livestock (enteric fermentation), rice paddies, landfills, and fossil fuel extraction.

- **Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)**: Released from agricultural soils (fertiliser use), industrial processes, and combustion. Very long-lived and highly potent.

- **Fluorinated Gases**: Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) and others. Synthetic gases used in refrigeration, air conditioning and industry. Extremely potent and long-lived.

Water vapour is also a greenhouse gas, but its concentration is largely determined by temperature feedbacks rather than direct human emissions.

## Evidence of Climate Change

Multiple lines of evidence confirm that the climate is changing:

- Rising global average surface temperatures (approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels as of recent assessments).
- Warming of the oceans and rising sea levels due to thermal expansion and melting of ice.
- Shrinking of glaciers and ice sheets (Greenland, Antarctica, Himalayan glaciers).
- Changes in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events (heatwaves, heavy precipitation, droughts, tropical cyclones).
- Shifts in the distribution of plant and animal species and changes in phenology (timing of biological events).
- Ocean acidification due to absorption of excess CO₂.

## Impacts of Climate Change

**Global Impacts**:
- Sea-level rise threatens coastal cities, small island states and low-lying deltas.
- Changes in precipitation patterns affect water availability and agriculture.
- Increased frequency of extreme events causes loss of life, livelihoods and infrastructure.
- Risks to biodiversity and ecosystems, including coral bleaching and forest dieback.
- Potential for large-scale, irreversible changes (tipping points) such as collapse of ice sheets or disruption of ocean circulation.

**Impacts on India**:
- Increased intensity of the monsoon and greater variability, leading to more frequent floods and droughts.
- Rapid retreat of Himalayan glaciers, affecting river flows in the Indo-Gangetic plains.
- Sea-level rise and increased cyclone intensity threatening the coasts of Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, Gujarat and the island territories.
- Heat stress affecting human health, labour productivity and agriculture.
- Threats to food security due to impacts on crop yields (wheat, rice, and others).
- Stress on water resources and increased competition among sectors and regions.

## International Response to Climate Change

**United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)**: Adopted at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992. It provides the overall framework for intergovernmental efforts. The principle of “common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities” (CBDR-RC) recognises that developed countries have historically contributed more to the problem and have greater capacity to address it.

**Kyoto Protocol (1997)**: Set binding emission reduction targets for developed countries (Annex I parties) for the period 2008-2012 (and later extended). Introduced flexible mechanisms: Emissions Trading, Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), and Joint Implementation.

**Paris Agreement (2015)**: A landmark agreement under the UNFCCC. Key features include:
- Goal of holding the increase in global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.
- Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs): each country sets its own targets.
- Global stocktake every five years to assess collective progress.
- Climate finance from developed to developing countries.
- Emphasis on adaptation as well as mitigation.
- The Agreement is legally binding in terms of process but the content of NDCs is nationally determined.

**Conference of the Parties (COP)**: The annual meetings of the UNFCCC. Important recent COPs have advanced rules for the Paris Agreement, climate finance, loss and damage, and sectoral initiatives.

## India’s Climate Policy and Actions

India is the third-largest emitter of GHGs in absolute terms but has low per capita emissions. Its stance has emphasised equity, the right to development, and the need for developed countries to take the lead and provide finance and technology.

Key elements of India’s approach:
- National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC, 2008) with eight missions: Solar, Enhanced Energy Efficiency, Sustainable Habitat, Water, Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem, Green India, Sustainable Agriculture, and Strategic Knowledge.
- Updated NDCs under the Paris Agreement: commitments on reducing emissions intensity of GDP, increasing non-fossil energy capacity, creating additional carbon sinks through forests, and adapting to climate impacts.
- Significant expansion of renewable energy, especially solar and wind, under schemes such as the National Solar Mission.
- Participation in international initiatives such as the International Solar Alliance (co-founded by India and France).
- Domestic policies on energy efficiency, electric mobility, and sustainable urban development.

## Mitigation and Adaptation

**Mitigation**: Actions to reduce GHG emissions or enhance sinks. Examples include shifting to renewable energy, improving energy efficiency, afforestation, sustainable agriculture, and reducing methane from livestock and waste.

**Adaptation**: Adjustments in natural or human systems in response to actual or expected climatic stimuli. Examples include drought-resistant crops, improved irrigation, early warning systems, coastal protection, and climate-resilient infrastructure.

Both mitigation and adaptation are necessary. Mitigation reduces the long-term severity of the problem; adaptation addresses impacts that are already occurring or are unavoidable.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, fluorinated gases.
- Enhanced greenhouse effect causes global warming.
- UNFCCC 1992; Kyoto Protocol 1997; Paris Agreement 2015.
- Paris goals: well below 2°C, pursue 1.5°C; NDCs; global stocktake.
- CBDR-RC principle.
- NAPCC 2008 with eight national missions.
- India: low per capita emissions, high absolute emissions, focus on equity and renewable energy.
- Mitigation vs Adaptation.
- International Solar Alliance.

## Conceptual Notes

Climate change is a classic example of a global commons problem: the atmosphere is a shared resource, and the benefits of emitting GHGs are local while the costs are global and long-term. This creates incentives for free-riding and makes international cooperation both essential and difficult. The science of climate change is robust; the challenges are primarily political, economic and ethical – involving questions of intergenerational equity, historical responsibility, and the distribution of the costs of transition. For a country like India, the dual challenge is to pursue rapid development and poverty eradication while contributing to global mitigation efforts and building resilience to impacts that disproportionately affect the poor and vulnerable.
