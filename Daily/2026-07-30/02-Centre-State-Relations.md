# Centre-State Relations

## Introduction

India is a Union of States. The Constitution establishes a federal system with a strong central bias. The distribution of powers between the Union and the States is a defining feature of Indian federalism. Centre-State relations can be studied under three broad heads: Legislative Relations, Administrative Relations, and Financial Relations. In addition, there are mechanisms for coordination and the role of institutions such as the Inter-State Council and Finance Commission.

## Legislative Relations

Articles 245 to 255 deal with legislative relations.

**Territorial Jurisdiction**: Parliament can make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India. A State Legislature can make laws for the whole or any part of the State. Parliament’s laws can have extraterritorial operation; State laws cannot.

**Distribution of Legislative Subjects**: The Seventh Schedule contains three lists:

- **Union List (List I)**: 100 subjects (originally 97). Parliament has exclusive power. Subjects include defence, foreign affairs, banking, currency, atomic energy, railways, airways, posts and telegraphs, citizenship, and inter-state trade.

- **State List (List II)**: 61 subjects (originally 66). State Legislatures have exclusive power in normal times. Subjects include public order, police, public health, agriculture, local government, and fisheries.

- **Concurrent List (List III)**: 52 subjects (originally 47). Both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate. In case of conflict, the Union law prevails (Article 254). Subjects include criminal law, criminal procedure, marriage and divorce, forests, education, population control, and economic and social planning.

**Residuary Powers**: Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List vest residuary powers of legislation in Parliament. This is a major centralising feature.

**Parliamentary Legislation in the State Field**:

- Article 249: If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by two-thirds majority that it is necessary in the national interest for Parliament to legislate on a State List subject, Parliament can do so for a period of one year (extendable).

- Article 250: During a Proclamation of Emergency, Parliament can legislate on any subject in the State List. Such laws cease to have effect six months after the Emergency ends.

- Article 252: If two or more States request Parliament to legislate on a State List subject, Parliament can do so. The law applies only to the consenting States and others that adopt it later.

- Article 253: Parliament can make laws for implementing international treaties, agreements and conventions, even on State List subjects.

**Inconsistency between Union and State Laws**: Article 254 provides that if a State law on a Concurrent subject is repugnant to a Union law, the Union law prevails. However, if the State law has received the President’s assent, it prevails in that State unless Parliament later enacts a law overriding it.

## Administrative Relations

Articles 256 to 263 govern administrative relations.

**Obligation of States and the Union**: Article 256 requires the executive power of every State to be so exercised as to ensure compliance with the laws made by Parliament. Article 257 provides that the executive power of every State shall be so exercised as not to impede or prejudice the exercise of the executive power of the Union. The Union may give directions to States for these purposes.

**Control of the Union over States in certain cases**: The Union can issue directions to States regarding construction and maintenance of means of communication of national or military importance, and protection of railways within the State.

**Delegation of Union functions**: With the consent of the State Government, the President may entrust to the State Government or its officers functions in relation to any matter to which the executive power of the Union extends (Article 258). Conversely, a State can entrust functions to the Union with the consent of the Government of India (Article 258A).

**All-India Services**: Article 312 provides for All-India Services (IAS, IPS, IFS). Officers of these services serve both the Union and the States and act as a unifying force. The dual control (Union and State) is an important feature of Indian federalism.

**Inter-State Council**: Article 263 provides for the establishment of an Inter-State Council to inquire into and advise upon disputes between States, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination. The Council was established in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. It is a recommendatory body chaired by the Prime Minister.

**Zonal Councils**: Created by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, these are statutory (not constitutional) bodies for promoting cooperation among States in a zone. There are five Zonal Councils plus the North-Eastern Council.

## Financial Relations

Articles 268 to 293 deal with financial relations.

**Distribution of Taxing Powers**:

- Taxes levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States (Article 268): stamp duties on certain documents, duties of excise on medicinal and toilet preparations.

- Taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to the States (Article 269): taxes on inter-state trade and commerce (now largely subsumed under GST).

- Taxes levied and collected by the Union and distributed between the Union and the States (Article 270): the most important category, covering income tax (other than agricultural income) and, after the 101st Amendment, the Central share of GST.

- Taxes levied and collected and retained by the States: land revenue, agricultural income tax, taxes on lands and buildings, excise on alcohol, etc.

**Grants-in-Aid**: Article 275 provides for grants-in-aid from the Union to certain States (especially those needing assistance) charged on the Consolidated Fund of India. Article 282 allows the Union or a State to make grants for any public purpose, even if outside their legislative competence. This has been used extensively for Centrally Sponsored Schemes.

**Finance Commission**: Article 280 provides for a Finance Commission every five years (or earlier) to recommend the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, the allocation among States, principles governing grants-in-aid, and measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities. The recommendations of the Finance Commission are placed before Parliament; the government generally accepts them.

**Goods and Services Tax (GST)**: The 101st Constitutional Amendment, 2016, introduced GST, a concurrent tax on supply of goods and services. It created the GST Council (Article 279A) as a constitutional body for joint decision-making on GST rates, exemptions, and model laws. GST has significantly altered the financial landscape by subsuming many earlier taxes and creating a dual GST structure (CGST + SGST/UTGST) with IGST for inter-state supplies.

## Important Commissions on Centre-State Relations

- **Sarkaria Commission (1983-88)**: Examined Centre-State relations comprehensively. Recommended greater consultation, strengthening of the Inter-State Council, restraint in the use of Article 356, and proper use of the office of Governor.

- **Punchhi Commission (2007-10)**: Reviewed Centre-State relations in the light of changes since Sarkaria. Made recommendations on the role of Governors, Article 356, inter-state river disputes, and the need for a more balanced federal structure.

## Emergency Provisions and Federalism

During a National Emergency (Article 352), the federal structure can become unitary. Parliament can legislate on State subjects, and the executive power of the Union extends to giving directions to States on any matter. During President’s Rule (Article 356), the President can assume the functions of the State Government and declare that the powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by Parliament. Financial Emergency (Article 360) allows the Union to issue directions on financial matters to States.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Three Lists in the Seventh Schedule: Union, State, Concurrent.
- Residuary powers with the Union.
- Union law prevails over State law on Concurrent List subjects in case of conflict (with limited exception if State law has Presidential assent).
- Article 249 allows Parliament to legislate on State subjects in national interest with Rajya Sabha resolution.
- Inter-State Council under Article 263.
- Finance Commission under Article 280 – quasi-judicial, recommendatory.
- GST Council under Article 279A – constitutional body for cooperative federalism in taxation.
- All-India Services under Article 312 act as steel frame linking Centre and States.
- Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions are landmark reports on Centre-State relations.

## Conceptual Notes

Indian federalism is often described as “quasi-federal” or “federal with a unitary bias.” The strong Centre was a deliberate choice of the Constituent Assembly in light of Partition, the need for national unity, and the requirements of planned economic development. Over time, political and economic changes, the rise of regional parties, and judicial interpretation have introduced greater elements of cooperative and competitive federalism.

The GST Council represents a significant institutional innovation in cooperative federalism. Decisions are taken by a three-fourths majority, with the Centre having one-third weightage and States collectively two-thirds. This forces consensus-building.

The office of the Governor remains a sensitive point in Centre-State relations. The Governor is appointed by the President and holds office during the pleasure of the President, yet is expected to act as a constitutional head of the State. Controversies regarding the use of Article 356, reservation of bills, and the role of the Governor in hung assemblies continue to test the federal balance.
