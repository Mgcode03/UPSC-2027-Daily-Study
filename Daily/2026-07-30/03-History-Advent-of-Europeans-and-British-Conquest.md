# History: Advent of Europeans and the British Conquest of India

## The Coming of the Europeans

The decline of the Mughal Empire in the eighteenth century coincided with the increasing commercial and political ambitions of European trading companies in India. The Portuguese were the first to arrive, followed by the Dutch, English, French, and others. Ultimately, the English East India Company emerged as the dominant power and laid the foundations of British rule.

### The Portuguese

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. The Portuguese established trading posts and fortified settlements along the western coast. Important centres included Goa (captured in 1510 by Afonso de Albuquerque), Diu, Daman, Cochin, and Hooghly. Albuquerque’s policies included the establishment of a Portuguese empire in the East based on control of the seas (cartaz system), encouragement of mixed marriages, and suppression of Arab trade. The Portuguese introduced the cultivation of tobacco, cashew, and potato, and brought the printing press and new architectural styles. Their power declined after the sixteenth century due to competition from the Dutch and English, internal corruption, and the union of the Portuguese and Spanish crowns.

### The Dutch

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was formed in 1602. They established factories at Masulipatnam, Pulicat, Surat, Chinsura, and other places. Their main interest was in the spice trade of the Indonesian archipelago. They defeated the Portuguese in several encounters and briefly challenged the English. After the Battle of Bedara (1759), Dutch power in India declined sharply. They eventually focused on the East Indies.

### The English East India Company

The English East India Company received its charter from Queen Elizabeth I in 1600. The first factory was established at Surat in 1613 after Captain Hawkins’s visit to Jahangir’s court. In 1615, Sir Thomas Roe secured important trading privileges. The Company established Madras (Fort St. George, 1639), Bombay (received from the Portuguese as part of Catherine of Braganza’s dowry in 1661 and given to the Company in 1668), and Calcutta (Fort William, 1690s). These three Presidency towns became the nuclei of British power.

### The French

The French East India Company was founded in 1664 under Colbert. Pondicherry became their main settlement (1674). Other centres included Chandernagore, Mahe, Yanam, and Karaikal. Under governors such as Dupleix, the French pursued an aggressive policy of political intervention in Indian states, using trained Indian troops (sepoys) and European military techniques. This policy brought them into direct conflict with the English.

## Anglo-French Rivalry and the Carnatic Wars

The struggle for supremacy between the English and the French was played out mainly in the Carnatic region of South India and was linked to the wider European conflicts (War of Austrian Succession, Seven Years’ War).

**First Carnatic War (1746-48)**: Linked to the War of Austrian Succession. The French captured Madras. The war ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle; Madras was restored to the English. The war demonstrated the superiority of European military discipline and the effectiveness of naval power.

**Second Carnatic War (1749-54)**: Although Europe was at peace, the two companies continued the struggle through Indian proxies. Dupleix supported Chanda Sahib for the Nawabship of Carnatic and Muzaffar Jang for the Subahdari of Deccan. The English supported rival claimants. Robert Clive’s capture of Arcot in 1751 was a turning point. The war ended with the recall of Dupleix. The English candidate Muhammad Ali became Nawab of Carnatic.

**Third Carnatic War (1758-63)**: Linked to the Seven Years’ War. The French, under Count de Lally, captured Fort St. David but failed to take Madras. The decisive Battle of Wandiwash (1760) was won by the English under Eyre Coote. Pondicherry surrendered in 1761. The Treaty of Paris (1763) restored the French settlements but forbade fortifications and armies. French political ambitions in India were effectively ended.

The Carnatic Wars established the military superiority of the English East India Company and demonstrated that control of the sea and efficient use of Indian resources were decisive.

## British Conquest of Bengal

Bengal was the richest province of the Mughal Empire. The Company’s trade was substantial, and conflicts over privileges, dastaks (trade permits), and private trade of Company servants created tensions.

**Siraj-ud-Daulah and the Black Hole**: Siraj-ud-Daulah became Nawab in 1756. He was alarmed by the Company’s fortification of Calcutta and misuse of trading privileges. He captured Calcutta. The incident of the Black Hole of Calcutta (June 1756), in which a number of English prisoners died, became a powerful propaganda tool, though its exact details remain debated by historians.

**Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)**: Robert Clive, with a small force and the support of conspirators including Mir Jafar (commander of the Nawab’s army), Rai Durlabh, and Jagat Seth (the banker), defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah. The battle itself was short; the Nawab’s forces largely melted away due to treachery. Siraj was later captured and killed. Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab. The Company received large sums of money, the zamindari of the 24 Parganas, and enhanced trading privileges.

Plassey marked the beginning of the political transformation of the Company from trader to ruler. It was less a military triumph than a successful conspiracy backed by force.

**Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764)**: Mir Jafar proved unsatisfactory. His successor Mir Qasim tried to assert independence, abolished dastaks, and formed an alliance with Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh) and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. The combined forces were defeated by the English under Major Hector Munro at Buxar. The victory was more decisive than Plassey because it was a proper military contest.

**Treaty of Allahabad (1765)**: Clive (who returned as Governor) concluded treaties. The Mughal Emperor granted the Diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the Company. The Nawab of Awadh had to pay a large indemnity and surrender Allahabad and Kara. Shah Alam II was given a pension and resided at Allahabad under Company “protection.” The dual system of government was introduced in Bengal: the Company controlled revenue and the Nawab retained responsibility for administration and justice (nizamat). This system led to administrative chaos and the plunder of Bengal until it was ended by Warren Hastings in 1772.

## Expansion under Warren Hastings, Cornwallis, Wellesley and Others

**Warren Hastings (1772-85)**: Ended the dual system, shifted the treasury to Calcutta, and appointed British Collectors of revenue. He faced the Rohilla War, the First Anglo-Maratha War, and the Second Anglo-Mysore War. The Regulating Act of 1773 and Pitt’s India Act of 1784 began the process of parliamentary control over the Company.

**Lord Cornwallis (1786-93)**: Introduced the Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793), which created a class of hereditary zamindars. He also reformed the judiciary and civil services (Cornwallis Code). The Third Anglo-Mysore War ended with the Treaty of Seringapatam (1792); Tipu Sultan surrendered half his territory.

**Lord Wellesley (1798-1805)**: Followed an aggressive policy of expansion through the Subsidiary Alliance system. Under this system, Indian rulers were forced to accept a British force in their territory, pay for its maintenance, dismiss other European officers, and conduct foreign relations only through the British. In return, the British promised protection. The system was applied to Hyderabad, Awadh, Mysore (after Tipu’s death in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799), the Maratha Peshwa, and others. It expanded British influence without immediate annexation and drained the resources of the Indian states.

**Lord Hastings (1813-23)**: Completed the supremacy of the British. The Gurkha War (1814-16) led to the acquisition of Kumaon, Garhwal and other areas. The Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817-18) destroyed the Maratha Confederacy. The Pindari campaigns cleared central India. By 1818, the British were the paramount power in India.

**Lord Dalhousie (1848-56)**: Used the Doctrine of Lapse (annexation of states without natural heirs) to annex Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, and others. He also annexed Awadh in 1856 on grounds of misgovernance. His period saw the introduction of railways, telegraph, and postal reforms, and the Second Anglo-Sikh War leading to the annexation of Punjab (1849).

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Portuguese: Vasco da Gama 1498; Goa 1510; Albuquerque.
- English Company charter: 1600; Surat factory 1613; Madras 1639; Bombay 1668; Calcutta 1690s.
- Carnatic Wars: three wars; Wandiwash 1760 decisive.
- Plassey 1757: conspiracy + force; Mir Jafar.
- Buxar 1764: military victory; Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa 1765.
- Dual system: Company (diwani) + Nawab (nizamat); ended 1772.
- Subsidiary Alliance: Wellesley; military protection in exchange for subordination.
- Doctrine of Lapse: Dalhousie; annexation of states without natural heirs.
- Paramountcy established by 1818 after Third Anglo-Maratha War.

## Conceptual Understanding

The British conquest was not a single event but a long process driven by commercial ambition, military superiority (discipline, artillery, naval power), political fragmentation of India, and the ability of the Company to recruit and train Indian soldiers. The transition from trade to territory was gradual and often justified by the need to protect trade. Once territorial revenues became available (especially after the Diwani), the Company could maintain large armies without depending solely on profits from trade. The subsidiary alliance and doctrine of lapse were instruments of expansion that minimised the need for direct administration in the initial stages while maximising control.

The economic impact of early British rule included the drain of wealth, destruction of traditional industries (especially textiles), and the creation of a new land revenue system that prioritised the interests of the Company and its collaborators over those of cultivators.
