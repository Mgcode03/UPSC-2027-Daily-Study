# Ethics: Public Service Values and Integrity

## Meaning of Public Service Values

Public service values are the principles and standards that guide the behaviour of civil servants and public officials in the performance of their duties. They define what is considered right, proper and desirable in the conduct of public administration. These values are rooted in the Constitution, laws, rules, and the broader ethical traditions of society.

In a democracy, public servants are trustees of public resources and power. Their legitimacy depends not only on legal authority but also on public trust. Values such as integrity, impartiality, objectivity, accountability and dedication to public service form the foundation of that trust.

## Core Public Service Values

**Integrity**: Integrity means honesty, consistency between words and actions, and the refusal to be corrupted or compromised. A public servant of integrity does not use official position for personal gain, does not accept bribes or favours, and maintains financial and moral probity. Integrity is both personal and institutional. Personal integrity refers to the character of the individual; institutional integrity refers to systems, procedures and culture that make corrupt behaviour difficult and honest behaviour the norm.

**Impartiality and Non-partisanship**: Civil servants must serve the government of the day with equal commitment regardless of which political party is in power. They must not allow political preferences, personal relationships or sectional interests to influence their advice or decisions. Impartiality ensures that the permanent civil service remains a stable and professional instrument of governance across changes of government.

**Objectivity**: Decisions and advice should be based on evidence, facts and rational analysis rather than emotion, prejudice or extraneous considerations. Objectivity requires the courage to present inconvenient facts and the discipline to separate personal views from professional assessment.

**Accountability**: Public servants are accountable for their actions and decisions to the political executive, to the legislature, to the courts, and ultimately to the public. Accountability mechanisms include hierarchical control, legislative oversight, audit, judicial review, and the Right to Information. Accountability is not merely about answering for mistakes; it is about being answerable for the use of public power and resources.

**Dedication to Public Service**: This value emphasises commitment to the welfare of the people, especially the vulnerable and marginalised. It involves a sense of duty that goes beyond the minimum requirements of the job and a willingness to work in difficult conditions when required.

**Transparency**: Openness in decision-making and access to information enable citizens to understand how and why decisions are made. Transparency reduces the scope for arbitrary action and corruption and strengthens accountability.

**Compassion and Empathy**: Especially important in the delivery of public services to the poor, the elderly, women, children and persons with disabilities. Compassion humanises administration and prevents the mechanical application of rules from causing injustice.

## Integrity in Public Life

Integrity is often described as the most fundamental of all values. Without integrity, other values lose their meaning. A brilliant but dishonest officer can cause greater harm than a less capable but honest one.

Forms of integrity failure include:
- Corruption (bribery, embezzlement, favouritism, nepotism)
- Conflict of interest (using official position to benefit private interests)
- Misuse of official information or resources
- Failure to declare assets or interests where required
- Compromising professional standards under pressure

Preventive measures include strong legal frameworks (Prevention of Corruption Act, Lokpal and Lokayuktas), codes of conduct, asset declaration systems, whistle-blower protection, and an organisational culture that rewards integrity and punishes dishonesty.

## Foundational Values for Civil Services (as identified in various reports)

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission and other bodies have highlighted a set of foundational values:

- Integrity
- Impartiality and non-partisanship
- Objectivity
- Dedication to public service
- Empathy and compassion towards the weaker sections
- Tolerance and equanimity
- Accountability and transparency

These values are not abstract ideals; they have practical implications for everyday decision-making – from awarding contracts and making postings to handling public grievances and implementing welfare schemes.

## Ethical Dilemmas in Public Service

Public servants frequently face situations in which values conflict or in which the right course of action is not obvious. Examples include:

- Pressure from political superiors to favour a particular party or individual
- Conflict between rule-bound administration and the need for compassionate flexibility
- Balancing transparency with confidentiality in sensitive matters
- Choosing between short-term political expediency and long-term public interest
- Personal career considerations versus professional duty

Resolving such dilemmas requires ethical reasoning, clarity about one’s primary obligations (to the Constitution and the public), and sometimes the courage to dissent or to seek higher guidance.

## Code of Conduct and Code of Ethics

A Code of Conduct typically lays down specific do’s and don’ts (e.g., restrictions on acceptance of gifts, political activities, private employment). A Code of Ethics articulates the broader values and principles that should guide behaviour. Both are necessary. Rules without values can lead to mechanical compliance; values without enforceable rules can remain aspirational.

In India, the Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules and analogous State rules provide the formal framework. Various recommendations have been made for a more comprehensive Code of Ethics for civil servants.

## Building an Ethical Organisation

Individual integrity is necessary but not sufficient. Organisations must create systems that support ethical behaviour:

- Clear rules and procedures that reduce discretion where it can be abused
- Transparent decision-making processes
- Effective grievance redressal and whistle-blower mechanisms
- Leadership that models ethical behaviour
- Training and sensitisation in ethics
- Fair and prompt disciplinary action against misconduct
- Recognition and reward for exemplary ethical conduct

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Core values: integrity, impartiality, objectivity, accountability, dedication, transparency, compassion.
- Integrity: honesty + consistency + resistance to corruption.
- Impartiality: serving governments of different parties with equal commitment.
- Accountability: answerability for the use of public power.
- Foundational values emphasised by ARC and other reports.
- Ethical dilemmas arise from conflicting values or pressures.
- Code of Conduct (specific rules) vs Code of Ethics (principles).
- Organisational culture and systems are as important as individual character.

## Conceptual Understanding

Public service ethics is not merely about avoiding wrongdoing; it is about actively promoting the public good. In a diverse, unequal and complex society such as India, public servants exercise significant discretion. The quality of that discretion depends on their values. Integrity ensures that power is not converted into private gain. Impartiality and objectivity ensure that decisions are fair. Accountability ensures that power remains answerable. Compassion ensures that the human consequences of administrative action are never forgotten. Together, these values sustain the legitimacy of the administrative state and the trust of citizens in democratic institutions.
