# Economy: Planning and Economic Development in India

## Concept of Economic Planning

Economic planning is a deliberate and conscious attempt by the state to influence, direct, and in some cases control the growth and development of an economy over a period of time. It involves setting targets, allocating resources, and coordinating the activities of different sectors to achieve desired socio-economic objectives.

In the Indian context, planning was adopted after Independence as a means to overcome the colonial legacy of underdevelopment, poverty, low productivity, and regional imbalances. The Constitution’s Directive Principles of State Policy provided the ideological foundation for a mixed economy with a significant role for the public sector.

## Historical Background of Planning in India

Even before Independence, several efforts at planning had been made. The National Planning Committee under the chairmanship of Jawaharlal Nehru was set up by the Indian National Congress in 1938. The Bombay Plan (1944) was prepared by a group of industrialists. The People’s Plan was formulated by M.N. Roy, and the Gandhian Plan by S.N. Agarwal. These reflected different visions of India’s economic future.

After Independence, the Planning Commission was established in March 1950 by a resolution of the Government of India. It was an advisory body chaired by the Prime Minister. Its functions included assessment of resources, formulation of plans, determination of priorities, and appraisal of progress. The National Development Council (NDC), created in 1952, served as the highest decision-making body for planning and included the Prime Minister, Chief Ministers, and members of the Planning Commission.

## The Five-Year Plans: An Overview

India followed a system of Five-Year Plans from 1951 to 2017, with some Annual Plans and Plan Holidays in between.

**First Five-Year Plan (1951-56)**: Based on the Harrod-Domar model. Priority was given to agriculture, irrigation and power. The plan was successful in exceeding its growth target. Community Development Programme was launched.

**Second Five-Year Plan (1956-61)**: Based on the Mahalanobis model. Emphasis shifted to rapid industrialisation with a focus on heavy and basic industries. The public sector was given a leading role. The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 classified industries into three schedules and reserved the “commanding heights” for the public sector.

**Third Five-Year Plan (1961-66)**: Aimed at self-sustaining growth. Agriculture and industry both received attention. The plan was disrupted by the wars of 1962 and 1965 and by severe drought. It failed to achieve targets. A Plan Holiday (1966-69) followed with three Annual Plans.

**Fourth Five-Year Plan (1969-74)**: Emphasis on growth with stability and progressive achievement of self-reliance. Nationalisation of banks (1969) took place during this period. The plan faced challenges from inflation and the 1971 war.

**Fifth Five-Year Plan (1974-79)**: Focused on poverty alleviation (Garibi Hatao) and self-reliance. The concept of the Minimum Needs Programme was introduced. The plan was terminated a year early by the Janata Government, which introduced a Sixth Plan of its own (later abandoned).

**Sixth Five-Year Plan (1980-85)**: Aimed at direct attack on poverty through programmes such as NREP and RLEGP (later merged into Jawahar Rozgar Yojana). Liberalisation of industrial licensing began modestly.

**Seventh Five-Year Plan (1985-90)**: Emphasis on food, work and productivity. Growth accelerated. The period saw the beginning of significant fiscal imbalances.

**Eighth Five-Year Plan (1992-97)**: Formulated in the context of the 1991 economic crisis and reforms. Indicative planning was emphasised. Human development indicators received greater attention. The plan period coincided with the early years of liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation (LPG).

**Ninth to Twelfth Plans**: Continued emphasis on growth, equity, and sustainability. The Eleventh Plan (2007-12) targeted faster and more inclusive growth. The Twelfth Plan (2012-17) aimed at faster, more inclusive and sustainable growth. After the Twelfth Plan, the Planning Commission was replaced.

## NITI Aayog and the End of the Planning Commission

In 2015, the Planning Commission was replaced by the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog). NITI Aayog is a policy think-tank with the Prime Minister as Chairperson. It focuses on cooperative federalism, bottom-up planning, monitoring and evaluation, and knowledge and innovation. Unlike the Planning Commission, it does not allocate resources; financial allocation is handled by the Finance Ministry. The change reflected a shift from centralised planning to a more facilitative and cooperative model suited to a liberalised economy and a multi-party federal polity.

## Models of Growth and Development Strategies

**Nehru-Mahalanobis Strategy**: Emphasis on heavy industry, public sector dominance, import substitution, and a closed economy. The goal was to build a strong industrial base and achieve self-reliance over time.

**Agriculture-First vs Industry-First Debate**: Early plans prioritised industry after the First Plan. The neglect of agriculture contributed to food shortages in the 1960s and the need for the Green Revolution.

**Green Revolution**: Beginning in the mid-1960s, the introduction of high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, chemical fertilisers, irrigation, and credit transformed agriculture in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of other states. It made India self-sufficient in food grains but also created regional and social disparities and environmental concerns.

**Economic Reforms of 1991**: Triggered by a balance-of-payments crisis, the reforms included devaluation of the rupee, reduction of fiscal deficit, industrial delicensing, opening to foreign investment, trade liberalisation, and financial sector reforms. The reforms marked a decisive shift from the earlier inward-looking strategy to greater integration with the global economy.

## Key Concepts in Development

**Economic Growth vs Economic Development**: Growth refers to the increase in real national income or per capita income. Development is a broader concept that includes structural changes, improvement in living standards, reduction of poverty and inequality, and expansion of human capabilities (as emphasised by Amartya Sen).

**Inclusive Growth**: Growth that provides opportunities for all sections of society and reduces disparities. It has been a central theme of recent plans and policy documents.

**Sustainable Development**: Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It integrates economic, social and environmental dimensions.

**Human Development Index (HDI)**: Developed by the UNDP, it measures development in terms of life expectancy, education and per capita income. India has improved its HDI over the decades but still faces significant challenges in health and education outcomes.

## Challenges of Indian Planning and Development

- Persistence of poverty and malnutrition despite growth.
- Regional disparities and the concentration of industrial development.
- Jobless growth and the challenge of employing a large young population.
- Agricultural distress and the need for a second green revolution that is sustainable.
- Environmental degradation and climate change risks.
- Quality of public services in health and education.
- Fiscal constraints and the need for efficient public expenditure.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Planning Commission established 1950; replaced by NITI Aayog 2015.
- First Plan: agriculture and irrigation (Harrod-Domar).
- Second Plan: heavy industry (Mahalanobis).
- Industrial Policy Resolution 1956: commanding heights of public sector.
- Green Revolution: mid-1960s onwards; HYV seeds, wheat and rice.
- 1991 reforms: LPG – liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation.
- NITI Aayog: cooperative federalism, no resource allocation role.
- Growth vs Development: quantitative vs qualitative and structural change.
- HDI: health, education, income.

## Conceptual Clarity

Indian planning evolved from a highly centralised, public-sector-driven model to a more indicative, market-oriented and cooperative federal model. The early emphasis on heavy industry created an industrial base but at the cost of efficiency and consumer goods. The reforms of 1991 unlocked growth potential but also raised questions about equity and the role of the state in providing public goods. Contemporary development strategy seeks to combine high growth with inclusion, sustainability and human development. Understanding this historical trajectory is essential for analysing current economic policies and their outcomes.
