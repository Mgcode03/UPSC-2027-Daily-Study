# Fundamental Duties

## Constitutional Position and Origin

Fundamental Duties are contained in Part IVA of the Constitution, specifically under Article 51A. They were not part of the original Constitution of 1950. They were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, on the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee. The Committee had suggested the incorporation of certain duties that citizens should observe, drawing inspiration from the Constitution of the erstwhile Soviet Union and other socialist constitutions that emphasised citizens’ obligations alongside rights.

Initially, ten Fundamental Duties were listed. An eleventh duty was added by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002. Unlike Fundamental Rights, which are enforceable through courts, Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable. There is no direct legal sanction for their violation. However, Parliament can make laws to enforce them, and courts have referred to them while interpreting laws and constitutional provisions.

The inclusion of Fundamental Duties was intended to remind citizens that rights and duties are correlative. While the Constitution guarantees rights, citizens also have obligations towards the nation, society, and fellow citizens. The duties aim to promote a sense of discipline, commitment, and patriotism, and to counteract tendencies that undermine the unity and integrity of the country.

## The Eleven Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

It shall be the duty of every citizen of India —

(a) to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem;

(b) to cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom;

(c) to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India;

(d) to defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so;

(e) to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women;

(f) to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture;

(g) to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wild life, and to have compassion for living creatures;

(h) to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;

(i) to safeguard public property and to abjure violence;

(j) to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement;

(k) who is a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years. (Added by the 86th Amendment, 2002)

These duties cover a wide spectrum — constitutional loyalty, patriotism, national security, social harmony, cultural preservation, environmental protection, scientific outlook, non-violence, excellence, and parental responsibility towards education.

## Detailed Understanding of Each Duty

### Duty to Abide by the Constitution and Respect National Symbols

This duty requires citizens to respect the Constitution as the supreme law and to honour the National Flag and National Anthem. Disrespect to these symbols can attract legal consequences under laws such as the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971. The duty reinforces constitutional morality and national identity.

### Cherishing the Ideals of the Freedom Struggle

Citizens are expected to remember and uphold the values of liberty, equality, justice, non-violence, and sacrifice that characterised the national movement. This duty links the present generation with the historical struggle that created independent India.

### Upholding Sovereignty, Unity and Integrity

This is a core duty that prohibits actions that threaten the territorial integrity or the political unity of the nation. It supports laws dealing with secessionist activities, terrorism, and communal violence that endanger national cohesion.

### Defending the Country and National Service

When required, citizens must be prepared to defend the nation. This duty provides the moral and constitutional foundation for compulsory military service if Parliament were to legislate for it, though India follows a voluntary system at present.

### Promoting Harmony and Brotherhood; Renouncing Practices Derogatory to Women

This duty directly addresses communalism, casteism, regionalism, and gender discrimination. It places a positive obligation on citizens to foster fraternity and to reject practices that demean women. It complements the Fundamental Rights relating to equality and non-discrimination.

### Valuing and Preserving Composite Culture

India’s strength lies in its diversity of languages, religions, traditions, and art forms. This duty requires citizens to respect and protect this composite heritage rather than promote cultural exclusivism.

### Protecting the Natural Environment and Compassion for Living Creatures

This duty aligns with Article 48A (Directive Principle) and has been given practical shape through environmental legislation such as the Environment (Protection) Act, Wildlife Protection Act, and Forest Conservation Act. Courts have frequently invoked this duty while delivering judgments on environmental matters.

### Developing Scientific Temper, Humanism and Spirit of Inquiry

This duty seeks to promote rationality, critical thinking, and a progressive outlook. It discourages superstition, dogma, and blind faith, and encourages reformist attitudes in social and personal life.

### Safeguarding Public Property and Abjuring Violence

Citizens must protect public assets — roads, buildings, transport, utilities — and reject violence as a means of protest or expression. This duty is particularly relevant in the context of strikes, bandhs, and public demonstrations that sometimes lead to destruction of property.

### Striving for Excellence

This duty is aspirational. It calls upon every citizen to pursue excellence in personal and collective endeavours so that the nation as a whole progresses. It underpins the culture of achievement and continuous improvement.

### Parental Duty Regarding Education

The eleventh duty, added in 2002, makes it obligatory for parents and guardians to provide educational opportunities to children in the age group of six to fourteen years. This duty supports the Fundamental Right to Education under Article 21A and the corresponding legislation.

## Legal Status and Enforceability

Fundamental Duties are not directly enforceable by courts. However:

- Parliament may enact laws to give effect to any of these duties.
- Courts can take them into account while determining the reasonableness of restrictions on Fundamental Rights.
- In several judgments, the Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Duties are as important as Fundamental Rights and that citizens should be conscious of them.
- Some duties have been indirectly enforced through specific statutes (for example, environmental duties through environmental laws, respect for national symbols through the National Honour Act).

The Verma Committee (1999) examined ways to operationalise Fundamental Duties and recommended that they be taught in educational institutions and that certain duties be given statutory backing.

## Significance in the Constitutional Scheme

Fundamental Duties serve several important purposes:

- They balance rights with responsibilities.
- They promote a sense of national unity and discipline.
- They provide a moral compass for citizens.
- They help in the interpretation of laws and constitutional provisions.
- They reinforce the values of the freedom struggle and the constitutional vision.

In a diverse and large democracy, the conscious observance of these duties by citizens strengthens the democratic fabric and supports the effective enjoyment of rights by all.

## Key Points for Retention

- Part IVA, Article 51A
- Added by 42nd Amendment, 1976 (ten duties); eleventh by 86th Amendment, 2002
- Non-justiciable but can be enforced through legislation
- Inspired by Swaran Singh Committee recommendations
- Cover constitutional loyalty, national integrity, social harmony, environment, scientific temper, excellence, and parental educational responsibility
- Complement Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
- Courts have given them interpretive value

Fundamental Duties remind every citizen that the Constitution is not merely a charter of rights but also a document of responsibilities essential for the survival and progress of the Republic.
