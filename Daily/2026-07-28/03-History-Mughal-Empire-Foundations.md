# History: Foundations of the Mughal Empire

## Introduction to the Mughal Period

The Mughal Empire, which dominated large parts of the Indian subcontinent from the early sixteenth century until the mid-eighteenth century (with nominal continuity thereafter), represents one of the most significant political, cultural, and administrative formations in Indian history. The empire was founded by Babur in 1526 after his victory at the First Battle of Panipat. Although the Mughals were of Turko-Mongol origin, tracing their ancestry to both Timur (on the paternal side) and Genghis Khan (on the maternal side), they developed a distinctly Indo-Persian political culture that deeply influenced the subcontinent.

The early phase of the empire — from Babur to Akbar — laid the military, administrative, and ideological foundations that enabled the Mughals to consolidate power over a vast and diverse territory. Understanding this foundational period is essential for grasping later Mughal institutions, the nature of state formation in medieval India, and the cultural synthesis that characterised the era.

## Babur (1526–1530): The Founder

Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur was born in 1483 in the Fergana Valley (present-day Uzbekistan). He inherited a small principality but faced continuous pressure from the Uzbeks under Shaibani Khan. After losing his Central Asian possessions, Babur turned his attention towards India. He had already conducted several raids into the Punjab region.

The political situation in North India in the early sixteenth century was favourable for an ambitious outsider. The Delhi Sultanate under the Lodi dynasty was weakened by internal rebellions and the independent ambitions of Afghan nobles. Rana Sanga of Mewar was a powerful Rajput ruler who also sought influence over the declining Sultanate.

### First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526)

Babur faced Ibrahim Lodi, the last Lodi Sultan, at Panipat. Despite being heavily outnumbered, Babur achieved a decisive victory through superior military technology and tactics. Key factors in his success included:

- Use of field artillery and matchlock guns, which were relatively new to Indian warfare on this scale.
- The tulughma tactic (dividing the army into flanking units to encircle the enemy).
- Effective use of the araba (cart) system to protect artillery and create defensive positions.
- High mobility of his cavalry and disciplined command structure.

Ibrahim Lodi was killed in the battle, and the Lodi dynasty came to an end. Babur occupied Delhi and Agra. However, his position remained precarious because he had to face the challenge of Rana Sanga and residual Afghan resistance.

### Battle of Khanwa (1527)

Rana Sanga of Mewar, who had expected Babur to withdraw after defeating the Lodis (as earlier Central Asian invaders had done), confronted Babur at Khanwa near Agra. Babur again used artillery and tactical skill to defeat the Rajput confederacy. After this victory, Babur assumed the title of Ghazi. The Battle of Khanwa was more decisive than Panipat in establishing Mughal power in North India because it broke the most powerful indigenous challenge of the time.

Babur also defeated the Afghans at the Battle of Ghagra in 1529. He died in 1530 at Agra. His memoirs, the Baburnama (written in Chagatai Turkish), provide valuable insights into his personality, military campaigns, and observations of the Indian environment and society.

Babur’s short reign established the Mughal presence but did not create a stable administrative system. That task fell to his successors.

## Humayun (1530–1540 and 1555–1556): Struggle and Restoration

Nasir-ud-din Muhammad Humayun inherited a fragile empire. He faced multiple challenges: rebellious brothers (especially Kamran, Askari, and Hindal), the powerful Afghan leader Sher Khan (later Sher Shah Suri), and the residual power of the Rajputs and other regional chiefs.

Humayun’s early years were marked by military setbacks. He lost the Battle of Chausa (1539) and the Battle of Kannauj (1540) to Sher Shah Suri. As a result, he was forced into exile, spending nearly fifteen years outside India, mainly in Persia and Sindh. During his stay at the Safavid court in Persia, Humayun received military assistance in return for certain concessions, including the temporary adoption of Shia practices.

Sher Shah Suri established the Sur Empire (1540–1555), which, though short-lived, introduced important administrative and revenue reforms that later influenced the Mughals. After Sher Shah’s death and the subsequent weakening of the Sur dynasty, Humayun was able to recover Delhi and Agra in 1555 with Persian help. He died accidentally in 1556, shortly after regaining the throne, leaving the empire to his thirteen-year-old son Akbar.

Humayun’s reign is significant for two reasons: first, it demonstrated the resilience of the Mughal claim; second, his exposure to Persian culture deepened the Persianate character of the Mughal court.

## Akbar (1556–1605): Consolidation and Imperial Foundations

Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar is regarded as the real consolidator of the Mughal Empire. When he ascended the throne in 1556 under the regency of Bairam Khan, the Mughal position was still insecure. The Second Battle of Panipat (1556) against Hemu, a general of the Sur remnants, secured Akbar’s throne. Thereafter, a long process of territorial expansion, administrative innovation, and ideological experimentation followed.

### Territorial Expansion

Under Akbar, the empire expanded significantly:

- Malwa, Gondwana, Rajasthan (through a combination of warfare and matrimonial alliances), Gujarat, Bengal, and parts of the Deccan were brought under Mughal control.
- The conquest of Gujarat (1572–73) gave the empire access to important ports and overseas trade.
- The submission of many Rajput states through a policy of alliance rather than pure conquest was a distinctive feature.

### Administrative and Revenue Systems

Akbar’s reign saw the crystallisation of key Mughal institutions:

- The mansabdari system: a unique system of ranking (mansab) that combined military and civil responsibilities. Mansabdars were assigned ranks (zat and sawar) that determined their status, salary, and military contingent.
- The jagirdari system: land revenue assignments (jagirs) given to mansabdars in lieu of salary. The system was carefully regulated to prevent the emergence of hereditary local power.
- Land revenue reforms: building upon Sher Shah’s system, Akbar’s administration under Raja Todar Mal refined the measurement of land, classification of soils, and assessment of revenue (the zabt system in many areas). The revenue demand was generally fixed as a share of the produce, payable in cash.
- Centralised bureaucracy with specialised departments (diwan, mir bakshi, mir saman, sadr, etc.).

### Religious and Ideological Policies

Akbar’s religious policy evolved over time. Early in his reign he followed conventional Islamic practices, but later he adopted a more inclusive approach:

- Abolition of the jizya (pilgrim tax on non-Muslims) and the pilgrimage tax.
- Matrimonial alliances with Rajput houses and appointment of Rajputs and other non-Muslims to high mansabs.
- Establishment of the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) at Fatehpur Sikri for inter-religious discussions.
- Promulgation of the Din-i-Ilahi (though its actual following was limited and it was more an ethical and imperial cult than a new religion).
- Policy of sulh-i-kul (peace with all), which sought to treat all subjects equally regardless of religion.

These measures helped in broadening the social base of the empire and reducing religious conflict as a source of political instability.

### Cultural and Architectural Contributions

Akbar’s period saw the flowering of Mughal culture:

- Construction of Fatehpur Sikri as a new capital (later abandoned).
- Development of Mughal painting through the imperial atelier.
- Patronage of literature in Persian and translation of Sanskrit works.
- Synthesis of Persian, Indian, and Central Asian architectural elements.

## Significance of the Foundational Phase

The period from Babur to Akbar transformed a Central Asian conquest state into a durable Indo-Persian empire with deep roots in the subcontinent. Military technology, flexible alliance strategies, sophisticated revenue and ranking systems, and an inclusive imperial ideology were the key pillars. Later Mughal rulers built upon these foundations, though the empire eventually faced structural challenges of succession, fiscal strain, and regional assertion in the eighteenth century.

## Key Facts and Concepts for Memory

- Babur: 1526–1530; Battles of Panipat (1526), Khanwa (1527), Ghagra (1529); Baburnama
- Humayun: two reigns; defeated by Sher Shah; exile in Persia; restoration 1555
- Sher Shah Suri: important administrative precursor (roads, currency, revenue)
- Akbar: 1556–1605; Second Battle of Panipat (1556); mansabdari and jagirdari systems; zabt revenue; sulh-i-kul; Ibadat Khana; Din-i-Ilahi
- Core institutions: mansab (zat and sawar), jagir, central departments
- Policy towards Rajputs: alliance and incorporation rather than pure subjugation

The foundations laid in this period explain both the strength and the eventual complexities of Mughal rule in India.
