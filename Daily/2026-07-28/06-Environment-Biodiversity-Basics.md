# Environment: Biodiversity — Concepts, Levels and Importance

## Meaning of Biodiversity

Biodiversity, or biological diversity, refers to the variety and variability of life on Earth. It encompasses the diversity within species, between species, and of ecosystems. The term was popularised in the 1980s and became a central concept in conservation biology and environmental policy following the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) adopted at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992.

Biodiversity is not merely a count of species; it includes genetic differences within populations, the variety of species in a given area, and the range of ecosystems and ecological processes that support life. It is the foundation of ecosystem services that sustain human societies — food, fibre, fuel, clean water, climate regulation, pollination, soil formation, and cultural values.

## Levels of Biodiversity

Biodiversity is conventionally understood at three hierarchical levels:

### Genetic Diversity

Genetic diversity refers to the variation of genes within a species. It is the raw material for evolution and for the adaptation of populations to changing environments. High genetic diversity within a species increases its resilience to diseases, climate change, and other stresses. Examples include the large number of traditional rice varieties in India, the diversity of mango cultivars, or the genetic variation in wild populations of tigers.

Loss of genetic diversity (genetic erosion) can occur through selective breeding that narrows the gene pool, habitat fragmentation that isolates populations, or the dominance of a few commercial varieties in agriculture.

### Species Diversity

Species diversity refers to the variety of species within a particular region or ecosystem. It is measured by species richness (the number of species) and species evenness (the relative abundance of different species). Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are among the most species-rich ecosystems on the planet.

India is recognised as one of the 17 megadiverse countries in the world. It harbours a high number of species of plants, animals, and microorganisms, many of which are endemic (found nowhere else).

### Ecosystem Diversity

Ecosystem diversity refers to the variety of ecosystems in a given area or on the planet. Ecosystems differ in their species composition, physical structure, and ecological processes. Examples in India include tropical rainforests of the Western Ghats and Northeast, dry deciduous forests of the Deccan, alpine meadows of the Himalayas, mangrove ecosystems of the Sundarbans, coral reefs of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the Gulf of Mannar, wetlands, grasslands, and desert ecosystems.

Different ecosystems provide different services and support different assemblages of species. The loss of an entire ecosystem type represents a profound loss of biodiversity.

## Biodiversity Hotspots

Biodiversity hotspots are regions that are both rich in endemic species and under significant threat of habitat loss. The concept was developed by Norman Myers. To qualify as a hotspot, a region must have at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and must have lost at least 70 per cent of its original habitat.

India has four major biodiversity hotspots (or parts of them):

1. The Western Ghats and Sri Lanka
2. The Himalayas
3. Indo-Burma (covering the northeastern states and adjoining areas)
4. Sundaland (including the Nicobar Islands)

These regions contain a disproportionately high share of India’s endemic species and are priority areas for conservation.

## Value of Biodiversity

Biodiversity provides multiple values that can be categorised as follows:

### Direct Use Values

- Food: crops, livestock, fish, wild edible plants and animals.
- Medicine: a large proportion of modern drugs have origins in natural compounds; traditional systems such as Ayurveda rely heavily on plant diversity.
- Raw materials: timber, fibres, gums, resins, oils.
- Ornamental and aesthetic uses.

### Indirect Use Values (Ecosystem Services)

- Provisioning services: food, water, genetic resources.
- Regulating services: climate regulation, flood control, water purification, pollination, pest control.
- Supporting services: soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production.
- Cultural services: spiritual, recreational, educational, and aesthetic benefits.

### Option Value and Existence Value

- Option value: the potential future benefits that may arise from biodiversity that is not currently used (for example, future medicines from unstudied species).
- Existence value: the value people derive from simply knowing that a species or ecosystem continues to exist, irrespective of any direct use.

## Threats to Biodiversity

The major causes of biodiversity loss (often summarised as the acronym HIPPO or more expanded lists) include:

- Habitat loss and fragmentation: conversion of forests, wetlands, and grasslands for agriculture, infrastructure, and urbanisation is the single largest driver.
- Over-exploitation: over-hunting, over-fishing, and over-harvesting of plants.
- Invasive alien species: non-native species that outcompete native organisms and alter ecosystems.
- Pollution: chemical pollution, plastic pollution, nutrient loading, and noise and light pollution.
- Climate change: shifting temperature and precipitation patterns, ocean acidification, and extreme events that alter habitats and species distributions.
- Co-extinctions: loss of one species leading to the loss of others that depend on it.

In India, additional pressures include population density, developmental projects in ecologically sensitive areas, and conflicts between wildlife and human communities.

## Conservation Approaches

Conservation of biodiversity is pursued through two broad strategies:

### In-situ Conservation

Conservation of species in their natural habitats. This includes:

- Protected areas: National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, Conservation Reserves, Community Reserves.
- Biosphere Reserves: large areas that combine conservation with sustainable use and research (India has many Biosphere Reserves under the UNESCO Man and the Biosphere Programme).
- Sacred groves: traditional community-protected forest patches.
- Biodiversity Heritage Sites.

In-situ conservation maintains evolutionary processes and ecological interactions.

### Ex-situ Conservation

Conservation of components of biodiversity outside their natural habitats. This includes:

- Botanical gardens, zoological parks, and aquaria.
- Seed banks, gene banks, and cryopreservation facilities.
- Captive breeding programmes for endangered species followed by reintroduction when possible.

Ex-situ methods serve as an insurance policy and support research and education, but they cannot fully replace the conservation of natural ecosystems.

## India as a Megadiverse Country

India’s high biodiversity is the result of its varied climate, topography, and geological history. The country accounts for a significant percentage of the world’s species of plants, mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish relative to its land area. Endemism is particularly high in the Western Ghats, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and the northeastern region.

Constitutional and legal frameworks for biodiversity conservation in India include the Wildlife (Protection) Act, the Forest (Conservation) Act, the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, and the Environment (Protection) Act, along with various international commitments under the Convention on Biological Diversity and related agreements.

## Key Points for Conceptual Clarity

- Biodiversity: variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels
- Genetic diversity: variation within species; basis for adaptation
- Species diversity: richness and evenness of species
- Ecosystem diversity: variety of habitats and ecological processes
- Biodiversity hotspots: high endemism + high threat; four in/near India
- Values: direct use, ecosystem services, option value, existence value
- Major threats: habitat loss, over-exploitation, invasive species, pollution, climate change
- In-situ vs ex-situ conservation
- India: megadiverse country with rich endemism

Biodiversity is not a luxury; it is the living infrastructure that supports human well-being and the resilience of the biosphere. Understanding its dimensions, values, and threats is foundational for environmental governance and sustainable development.
