# Geography: Climate of India and the Monsoon System

## Overview of India’s Climate

India’s climate is described as a tropical monsoon type, though this broad characterisation encompasses significant regional variations. The country experiences four distinct seasons according to the traditional classification used by the India Meteorological Department: the cold weather season (winter), the hot weather season (summer), the southwest monsoon season (rainy season), and the retreating monsoon season (autumn). The climate is influenced by India’s latitudinal extent, the Himalayan barrier in the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, the Thar Desert, and the complex interplay of pressure systems and wind patterns.

The most distinctive and economically crucial feature of India’s climate is the monsoon. The word “monsoon” is derived from the Arabic word “mausim,” meaning season. It refers to the seasonal reversal of winds that brings rainfall to the Indian subcontinent.

## Factors Influencing India’s Climate

Several physical and geographical factors shape the climate:

- Latitude: The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of India. Areas south of it have a tropical climate; areas to the north experience a subtropical climate.
- Himalayas: They act as a climatic barrier, protecting the subcontinent from the cold winds of Central Asia in winter and forcing the monsoon winds to shed their moisture within India.
- Altitude: Temperature decreases with height. Hill stations remain cooler throughout the year.
- Distance from the sea (continentality): Coastal areas have equable climates; interior areas experience greater temperature extremes.
- Western disturbances: Temperate cyclones originating over the Mediterranean that bring winter rainfall to northwestern India.
- Jet streams: Upper air westerly jet streams influence the onset and withdrawal of the monsoon and the movement of western disturbances.
- El Niño and Southern Oscillation (ENSO): Ocean-atmosphere phenomena that can disrupt the normal monsoon pattern.
- Relief: The Western Ghats cause orographic rainfall on their windward side and create a rain-shadow effect on the leeward side (Deccan Plateau).

## The Mechanism of the Monsoon

The traditional explanation of the monsoon is based on the differential heating of land and sea. In summer, the Indian landmass heats up rapidly, creating a low-pressure area over northwestern India and the Tibetan Plateau. The Indian Ocean remains relatively cooler, generating a high-pressure area. Moisture-laden winds blow from the high-pressure ocean towards the low-pressure land, bringing rainfall.

Modern understanding incorporates additional elements:

- The shift of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) northwards over the Indian subcontinent in summer.
- The role of the Tibetan Plateau as a heat source that intensifies the low-pressure system.
- The Somali Jet and the Mascarene High in the southern Indian Ocean that influence the strength of the monsoon current.
- The subtropical westerly jet stream that must shift north of the Himalayas before the monsoon can advance over India.

### Onset of the Southwest Monsoon

The southwest monsoon normally sets in over the Kerala coast around 1 June. It advances in two branches:

1. The Arabian Sea branch: This branch strikes the Western Ghats and causes heavy orographic rainfall on the windward side (Konkan and Malabar coasts). Part of it moves into the interior, and another part curves towards Rajasthan and further north.
2. The Bay of Bengal branch: This branch moves towards Myanmar and then curves westward into the Indian subcontinent, bringing rainfall to the northeastern states, West Bengal, and the Gangetic plains. It eventually merges with the Arabian Sea branch.

The monsoon normally covers the entire country by mid-July. The advance is not continuous; it occurs in pulses with periods of active rainfall and breaks (weak monsoon phases).

### Rainfall Distribution

Rainfall in India is highly uneven:

- Highest rainfall: Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya (orographic rainfall due to the Khasi Hills).
- Heavy rainfall (over 200 cm): Western Ghats, northeastern states, and parts of the Himalayan foothills.
- Moderate rainfall (100–200 cm): Most of the Gangetic plains, eastern Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and parts of the Deccan.
- Low rainfall (50–100 cm): Large parts of the Deccan Plateau, western Madhya Pradesh, and parts of Gujarat and Rajasthan.
- Scanty rainfall (less than 50 cm): Western Rajasthan, parts of Gujarat, and the rain-shadow regions of the Western Ghats.

The variability of rainfall is also high in the low-rainfall regions, making them more vulnerable to drought.

### Breaks in the Monsoon

During the monsoon season, there are periods when rainfall activity weakens or ceases over large parts of the country. These are called monsoon breaks. They are associated with the shifting of the monsoon trough (the low-pressure area) towards the foothills of the Himalayas. Prolonged breaks can cause agricultural stress even in a year of normal overall rainfall.

### Retreating Monsoon

From September onwards, the southwest monsoon begins to withdraw. The retreating monsoon season lasts from October to November. During this period, the low-pressure area weakens and moves southwards. The retreating monsoon winds are dry over most of northern India, but they pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal and cause rainfall along the Coromandel Coast (Tamil Nadu), which receives a significant portion of its annual rainfall during this season. Cyclonic storms frequently form in the Bay of Bengal during October–November and can cause heavy rainfall and damage along the eastern coast.

## Seasons of India in Detail

### Cold Weather Season (November to February/March)

- Clear skies, low temperatures, and low humidity in most of North India.
- Temperature decreases from south to north.
- Western disturbances bring light rainfall and snowfall to the northwestern plains and the Himalayas.
- The northeast trade winds prevail; they are dry over most of India but cause some rainfall on the southeastern coast.

### Hot Weather Season (March to May/June)

- Rapid rise in temperature, especially in the northwestern plains (temperatures can exceed 45°C).
- Formation of a low-pressure area over northwestern India.
- Dust storms (loo) in the north, and pre-monsoon showers (mango showers in Kerala, nor’westers or kalbaisakhi in West Bengal and Assam).

### Southwest Monsoon Season (June to September)

- The principal rainy season for most of India.
- Accounts for 75–90 per cent of annual rainfall in large parts of the country.

### Retreating Monsoon Season (October to November)

- Transition period with clear skies in the north and rainfall on the east coast.
- High humidity and temperatures in some regions.

## Climatic Regions of India (Broad Classification)

Although India has a monsoon climate overall, regional variations allow for a more detailed classification:

- Tropical rainy climate in parts of the west coast and northeast.
- Tropical savanna climate over large parts of the peninsula.
- Humid subtropical climate in the Gangetic plains.
- Mountain climate in the Himalayas.
- Desert and steppe climates in western Rajasthan and adjoining areas.
- Tropical semi-arid climate in parts of the Deccan.

## Importance of the Monsoon

The monsoon is often called the economic lifeline of India because:

- Agriculture, which still supports a large part of the population, depends heavily on monsoon rainfall.
- Reservoirs, groundwater recharge, and river flow are largely determined by the monsoon.
- Hydroelectric power generation is linked to monsoon performance.
- Droughts and floods associated with monsoon variability have major socio-economic consequences.

Understanding the monsoon system, its variability, and the factors that influence it remains central to Indian geography, agriculture, and disaster management.

## Key Points for Memory

- Climate type: Tropical monsoon with regional variations
- Four seasons: Winter, Summer, Southwest Monsoon, Retreating Monsoon
- Onset over Kerala: around 1 June
- Two branches: Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal
- Orographic rainfall on Western Ghats and northeastern hills
- Rain-shadow effect on the Deccan
- Highest rainfall: Mawsynram/Cherrapunji
- Western disturbances: winter rainfall in northwest
- ITCZ, Tibetan heat source, jet streams: modern explanations
- Retreating monsoon rains on Coromandel Coast
- High variability in low-rainfall regions

The monsoon system is both a unifying climatic feature and a source of significant spatial and temporal diversity across the Indian subcontinent.
