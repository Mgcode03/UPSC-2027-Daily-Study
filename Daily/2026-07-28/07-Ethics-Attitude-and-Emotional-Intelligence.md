# Ethics: Attitude and Emotional Intelligence — Foundational Concepts

## Understanding Attitude

Attitude refers to a psychological tendency that is expressed by evaluating a particular entity with some degree of favour or disfavour. In simpler terms, an attitude is a learned predisposition to respond in a consistently favourable or unfavourable manner with respect to a given object, person, idea, or situation.

Attitudes have three classical components (the ABC model):

1. Affective component: the emotional or feeling aspect (like or dislike, positive or negative emotion).
2. Behavioural component: the tendency to act or behave in a certain way towards the object of the attitude.
3. Cognitive component: the beliefs, thoughts, and knowledge about the object.

Attitudes are formed through experience, socialisation, education, observation of others (social learning), and direct exposure. They can be explicit (consciously held and reported) or implicit (operating outside conscious awareness).

### Functions of Attitudes

Attitudes serve several psychological functions:

- Knowledge function: they help organise and make sense of the world.
- Utilitarian (or instrumental) function: they help obtain rewards and avoid punishments.
- Ego-defensive function: they protect self-esteem and justify actions.
- Value-expressive function: they allow individuals to express central values and self-identity.

### Attitude Change

Attitudes can change through persuasion, new information, dissonance reduction, and social influence. Key theories relevant to attitude change include:

- Cognitive Dissonance Theory (Festinger): when there is inconsistency between attitudes and behaviour, individuals experience discomfort and are motivated to reduce it, often by changing the attitude.
- Elaboration Likelihood Model: persuasion can occur through a central route (careful consideration of arguments) or a peripheral route (superficial cues such as attractiveness of the source).
- Social Learning Theory: attitudes are acquired by observing and imitating others, particularly significant role models.

In the context of public service and ethics, the formation of positive attitudes towards integrity, impartiality, empathy, and public welfare is crucial. Negative attitudes such as prejudice, cynicism, or indifference can undermine ethical conduct and institutional performance.

## Content, Structure and Function of Attitudes in Administration

For civil servants and public officials, attitudes towards the following are particularly important:

- Attitude towards the public: respect, responsiveness, and empathy versus arrogance or indifference.
- Attitude towards rules and procedures: commitment to legality and fairness versus rigid formalism or manipulative evasion.
- Attitude towards colleagues and subordinates: collegiality, fairness, and mentorship versus favouritism or authoritarianism.
- Attitude towards change and innovation: openness versus resistance.
- Attitude towards ethical dilemmas: willingness to confront and resolve them versus avoidance or rationalisation.

Training, organisational culture, leadership example, and institutional incentives all shape these attitudes. Ethical codes and conduct rules attempt to reinforce desirable attitudes, but internalisation through personal conviction is more durable than mere compliance.

## Emotional Intelligence: Concept and Components

Emotional Intelligence (EI) refers to the ability to recognise, understand, manage, and reason with emotions in oneself and in others. The concept was popularised by psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer, and later brought to wider attention by Daniel Goleman.

Goleman’s widely used model identifies five main components:

1. Self-awareness: recognising one’s own emotions, strengths, weaknesses, values, and motives, and understanding their impact on others.
2. Self-regulation (or self-management): the ability to control or redirect disruptive impulses and moods, and to suspend judgement and think before acting. It includes trustworthiness, conscientiousness, adaptability, and innovativeness.
3. Motivation: a passion to work for reasons that go beyond money or status, pursuing goals with energy and persistence. It includes achievement drive, commitment, initiative, and optimism.
4. Empathy: the ability to understand the emotional makeup of other people and to treat them according to their emotional reactions. It includes service orientation, developing others, leveraging diversity, and political awareness.
5. Social skills: proficiency in managing relationships and building networks; an ability to find common ground and build rapport. It includes influence, communication, conflict management, leadership, change catalyst, building bonds, collaboration, and team capabilities.

Other models (such as the ability model of Mayer and Salovey) emphasise four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thinking, understanding emotions, and managing emotions.

## Importance of Emotional Intelligence in Public Life and Administration

Emotional intelligence is particularly relevant for civil servants, leaders, and professionals who work with people and make decisions under pressure:

- Better self-awareness reduces the likelihood of decisions driven by unrecognised bias, anger, or insecurity.
- Self-regulation helps maintain composure in crises, resist unethical pressures, and act consistently with values.
- Empathy improves public service delivery, citizen-centric governance, and the handling of grievances.
- Social skills facilitate coordination across departments, negotiation, and the building of trust with stakeholders.
- Motivation aligned with public purpose sustains integrity and effort even when external rewards are limited.

Research and practical experience suggest that technical competence alone is insufficient for effective and ethical public administration. The capacity to manage one’s own emotions and to respond constructively to the emotions of others is a critical complement to knowledge and skills.

## Relationship between Attitude and Emotional Intelligence

Attitudes and emotional intelligence interact in several ways:

- Emotional intelligence influences how attitudes are formed and expressed. A person with high self-awareness is more likely to recognise when an attitude is based on prejudice rather than evidence.
- Self-regulation helps individuals act in accordance with professed values even when emotional impulses pull in another direction.
- Empathy can soften rigid or hostile attitudes by enabling perspective-taking.
- Conversely, deeply held attitudes (for example, strong prejudices) can limit the exercise of empathy and distort emotional perception.

Developing emotional intelligence can therefore support the cultivation of more ethical, flexible, and constructive attitudes.

## Practical Implications for Ethical Conduct

- Regular self-reflection helps in identifying one’s dominant attitudes and emotional triggers.
- Seeking feedback from trusted colleagues can reveal blind spots in self-awareness.
- Exposure to diverse perspectives and experiences can challenge stereotypical attitudes.
- Mindfulness and stress-management practices support self-regulation.
- Deliberate perspective-taking exercises strengthen empathy.
- Organisational cultures that reward emotional maturity and ethical behaviour reinforce these capacities.

Attitude and emotional intelligence are not fixed traits; they can be developed through conscious effort, experience, and learning. For individuals preparing for public service, cultivating positive attitudes towards constitutional values, integrity, and public welfare, together with the emotional capacities to enact those attitudes under pressure, forms an essential part of ethical competence.

## Key Points for Retention

- Attitude: learned predisposition to evaluate an object favourably or unfavourably
- ABC components: Affective, Behavioural, Cognitive
- Functions of attitudes: knowledge, utilitarian, ego-defensive, value-expressive
- Attitude change: dissonance, persuasion routes, social learning
- Emotional Intelligence: recognising, understanding, managing, and using emotions
- Goleman’s five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills
- Relevance to administration: better decisions, citizen-centric service, integrity under pressure, coordination
- Interaction: EI supports healthier attitude formation and ethical behaviour

These concepts bridge psychology and ethics and provide tools for both self-development and the analysis of behaviour in organisational and public contexts.
