# CSAT: Basic Numeracy — Foundational Concepts

## Introduction to Basic Numeracy for CSAT

The Civil Services Aptitude Test (CSAT) paper includes questions on basic numeracy, which test quantitative aptitude at a level corresponding roughly to Class X mathematics. The emphasis is on conceptual understanding, logical application, and speed rather than advanced mathematical theory. Topics typically include numbers and their properties, percentages, ratios and proportions, averages, simple and compound interest, time and work, time and distance, basic algebra, geometry, and data interpretation.

This note focuses on core foundational concepts that underpin most quantitative questions.

## Number System Basics

### Types of Numbers

- Natural numbers: 1, 2, 3, … (counting numbers)
- Whole numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3, …
- Integers: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …
- Rational numbers: numbers that can be expressed as p/q where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0
- Irrational numbers: numbers that cannot be expressed as a simple fraction (e.g., √2, π)
- Real numbers: all rational and irrational numbers

### Divisibility Rules (Quick Reference)

- A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is even.
- Divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
- Divisible by 4 if the last two digits form a number divisible by 4.
- Divisible by 5 if the last digit is 0 or 5.
- Divisible by 6 if divisible by both 2 and 3.
- Divisible by 8 if the last three digits form a number divisible by 8.
- Divisible by 9 if the sum of digits is divisible by 9.
- Divisible by 11 if the difference between the sum of digits in odd positions and even positions is a multiple of 11 (including 0).

### Prime Numbers and Factors

A prime number has exactly two distinct positive divisors: 1 and itself. Composite numbers have more than two positive divisors. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Prime factorisation expresses a number as a product of primes. The Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two or more numbers is the largest number that divides all of them. The Least Common Multiple (LCM) is the smallest number that is a multiple of each of them. For two numbers a and b, HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b.

## Percentages

Percentage means “per hundred.” x% of a quantity is (x/100) × quantity.

Key relationships:

- If A is increased by x%, the new value = A × (1 + x/100)
- If A is decreased by x%, the new value = A × (1 − x/100)
- Successive percentage changes are multiplicative, not additive. An increase of x% followed by an increase of y% results in a net change of [x + y + (xy)/100]%.
- To find what percentage A is of B: (A/B) × 100
- Percentage change = [(New − Old)/Old] × 100

Percentages are widely used in questions involving profit and loss, population growth, election results, and data interpretation.

## Ratio and Proportion

A ratio is a comparison of two quantities by division. The ratio of a to b is a : b or a/b.

A proportion is an equality of two ratios: a/b = c/d, which can also be written as a : b :: c : d. In a proportion, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means (a × d = b × c).

If a quantity is divided in the ratio m : n, the actual parts are [m/(m+n)] and [n/(m+n)] of the total.

Direct proportion: two quantities increase or decrease together. Inverse proportion: one increases as the other decreases.

## Averages

The arithmetic mean (average) of a set of numbers is the sum of the numbers divided by the count of numbers.

Average = (Sum of observations) / (Number of observations)

If a new number is added or an existing number is removed or replaced, the average changes according to the difference between the new/removed value and the old average, distributed over the new number of observations.

Weighted average is used when different groups have different sizes or importance: Weighted Average = (Σ (value × weight)) / (Σ weights).

## Simple and Compound Interest

Simple Interest (SI) = (Principal × Rate × Time) / 100

Amount under simple interest = Principal + SI

Compound Interest is calculated on the principal plus the interest accumulated in previous periods.

Amount under compound interest (annual compounding) = Principal × (1 + Rate/100)^Time

Compound Interest = Amount − Principal

When interest is compounded more frequently (half-yearly, quarterly), the rate and time are adjusted accordingly (rate divided by the number of periods per year; time multiplied by the same factor).

The difference between compound interest and simple interest for two years is given by Principal × (Rate/100)^2.

## Time, Work and Time-Distance Basics

### Time and Work

If A can complete a work in n days, A’s one-day work = 1/n.

If A and B work together, their combined one-day work = 1/n + 1/m (where m is B’s time).

Total time taken working together = 1 / (combined one-day work).

If A is twice as efficient as B, A takes half the time that B takes.

Work is often treated as a constant; the relationship is Work = Rate × Time.

### Time and Distance

Distance = Speed × Time

Units must be consistent (km/h and hours, or m/s and seconds). Conversion: x km/h = x × (5/18) m/s; y m/s = y × (18/5) km/h.

Average speed for a journey with equal distances at speeds u and v is the harmonic mean: 2uv/(u+v).

For equal times, average speed is the arithmetic mean.

Relative speed: when two objects move in the same direction, relative speed = difference of speeds; when in opposite directions, relative speed = sum of speeds.

## Basic Algebraic Identities and Equations

Useful identities:

- (a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2
- (a − b)^2 = a^2 − 2ab + b^2
- (a + b)(a − b) = a^2 − b^2
- (a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca

Linear equations in one variable can be solved by isolating the variable. For two variables, methods include substitution, elimination, and cross-multiplication.

## Geometry Essentials

- Sum of angles in a triangle = 180°
- Pythagoras theorem: in a right-angled triangle, (hypotenuse)^2 = (side1)^2 + (side2)^2
- Area of triangle = (1/2) × base × height
- Area of rectangle = length × breadth
- Area of circle = π r^2; circumference = 2 π r
- Perimeter concepts for polygons

Understanding basic properties of triangles, quadrilaterals, and circles is sufficient for most CSAT geometry questions.

## Approach to Quantitative Questions

- Read the question carefully and identify what is asked.
- Note the given data and the units.
- Choose the appropriate concept or formula.
- Perform calculations systematically; avoid unnecessary complexity.
- Check whether the answer is reasonable in magnitude and units.
- For data interpretation, first understand the table, chart, or graph before attempting calculations.

Speed and accuracy both matter. Regular practice with timed sets improves both.

## Key Formulas and Concepts for Quick Recall

- Percentage change and successive changes
- HCF × LCM = product of two numbers
- Average = sum / number of observations
- SI = PRT/100
- CI Amount = P(1 + R/100)^T
- Distance = Speed × Time
- Work = Rate × Time
- Average speed (equal distance) = 2uv/(u+v)
- Pythagorean relation in right triangles

Basic numeracy in CSAT tests the ability to apply elementary mathematical ideas to practical and logical situations. Mastery of these foundations enables confident handling of the quantitative section.
