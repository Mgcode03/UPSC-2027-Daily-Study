# Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)

## Introduction and Constitutional Placement

The Directive Principles of State Policy are enshrined in Part IV of the Indian Constitution, covering Articles 36 to 51. They represent the ideals and objectives that the State should strive to achieve in the governance of the country. While Fundamental Rights (Part III) are justiciable and enforceable by courts, the Directive Principles are non-justiciable. This means that no citizen can approach a court of law to compel the government to implement a particular Directive Principle. However, Article 37 makes it clear that these principles are fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

The inspiration for Directive Principles came primarily from the Irish Constitution of 1937, which itself drew from the Spanish Constitution and the ideas of social justice prevalent in the early 20th century. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described the Directive Principles as a novel feature of the Constitution and called them the instrument of instructions to the legislature and the executive. He emphasised that while they may not be enforceable in courts, they are nevertheless binding on the conscience of the government.

The Directive Principles aim to establish a welfare state rather than a mere police state. They seek to secure social and economic justice, minimise inequalities, and promote the ideals of democracy, socialism, and secularism in substantive terms. Together with the Fundamental Rights and the Preamble, they form the core of the constitutional vision of India.

## Classification of Directive Principles

Although the Constitution itself does not formally classify the Directive Principles, scholars and commentators conventionally group them into three broad categories based on their content and ideological orientation: Socialist Principles, Gandhian Principles, and Liberal-Intellectual Principles.

### Socialist Principles

These principles reflect the influence of socialist thought and aim at establishing a social and economic democracy. Key provisions include:

- Article 38: The State shall strive to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order in which justice — social, economic and political — shall inform all the institutions of national life. The State shall, in particular, strive to minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.

- Article 39: The State shall direct its policy towards securing that citizens have the right to an adequate means of livelihood; that the ownership and control of the material resources of the community are so distributed as best to subserve the common good; that the operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment; that there is equal pay for equal work for both men and women; that the health and strength of workers, men and women, and the tender age of children are not abused; and that children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a healthy manner and in conditions of freedom and dignity.

- Article 39A: The State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, and shall provide free legal aid to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities. This was added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.

- Article 41: The State shall, within the limits of its economic capacity and development, make effective provision for securing the right to work, to education and to public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, and in other cases of undeserved want.

- Article 42: The State shall make provision for securing just and humane conditions of work and for maternity relief.

- Article 43: The State shall endeavour to secure, by suitable legislation or economic organisation or in any other way, to all workers, agricultural, industrial or otherwise, work, a living wage, conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life and full enjoyment of leisure and social and cultural opportunities. The State shall also endeavour to promote cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.

- Article 43A: The State shall take steps, by suitable legislation or in any other way, to secure the participation of workers in the management of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in any industry. Added by the 42nd Amendment.

- Article 47: The State shall regard the raising of the level of nutrition and the standard of living of its people and the improvement of public health as among its primary duties. In particular, the State shall endeavour to bring about prohibition of the consumption except for medicinal purposes of intoxicating drinks and of drugs which are injurious to health.

These provisions collectively seek to reduce economic disparities, protect the weaker sections, ensure social security, and promote a more egalitarian economic order.

### Gandhian Principles

These principles embody the ideals of Mahatma Gandhi and reflect his vision of a decentralised, village-based, and morally upright society. They include:

- Article 40: The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government. This forms the constitutional basis for the Panchayati Raj system, later strengthened by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment.

- Article 43: (already mentioned under socialist principles) also contains the Gandhian element of promoting cottage industries.

- Article 46: The State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, and, in particular, of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, and shall protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.

- Article 47: (already noted) contains the Gandhian emphasis on prohibition.

- Article 48: The State shall endeavour to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and shall, in particular, take steps for preserving and improving the breeds, and prohibiting the slaughter, of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle.

- Article 48A: The State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard the forests and wild life of the country. Added by the 42nd Amendment.

These principles emphasise decentralisation, protection of the vulnerable, prohibition, cow protection, and environmental care — themes close to Gandhian thought.

### Liberal-Intellectual Principles

These principles reflect liberal and modern intellectual values such as individual liberty in the broader sense, international peace, and scientific temper. Key articles are:

- Article 44: The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India. This remains one of the most debated Directive Principles.

- Article 45: The State shall endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years. Originally this article provided for free and compulsory education for children until the age of fourteen; after the 86th Amendment, the right to education for children aged 6–14 became a Fundamental Right (Article 21A), and Article 45 was modified accordingly.

- Article 49: It shall be the obligation of the State to protect every monument or place or object of artistic or historic interest declared by or under law made by Parliament to be of national importance from spoliation, disfigurement, destruction, removal, disposal or export, as the case may be.

- Article 50: The State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State. This principle has been substantially implemented through various laws and judicial pronouncements.

- Article 51: The State shall endeavour to promote international peace and security; maintain just and honourable relations between nations; foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organised peoples with one another; and encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

These principles promote a modern, progressive, and internationally responsible outlook.

## Amendments and Evolution

Several important changes have been made to the Directive Principles through constitutional amendments:

- The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 added Articles 39A, 43A and 48A, and also inserted the words “socialist” and “secular” in the Preamble (though the Preamble change is not part of DPSP itself).

- The 44th Amendment Act, 1978 inserted a new clause in Article 38 requiring the State to minimise inequalities in income and eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities.

- The 86th Amendment Act, 2002 modified Article 45 and introduced Article 21A as a Fundamental Right.

Over the decades, many Directive Principles have been given effect through legislation and policy. Examples include the enactment of the Minimum Wages Act, various social security laws, the establishment of Panchayati Raj institutions, the Right to Education Act, environmental protection laws, and legal aid schemes.

## Relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

The relationship between Part III and Part IV has been a subject of judicial evolution. In the early years, the Supreme Court held that Fundamental Rights would prevail over Directive Principles in case of conflict (Champakam Dorairajan case, 1951). Later, the Court adopted a harmonious construction approach, holding that both are complementary and neither is superior to the other (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973; Minerva Mills, 1980). The Court has also held that Directive Principles can be used to interpret and expand the scope of Fundamental Rights, particularly the right to life under Article 21.

Thus, while Directive Principles remain non-justiciable, they have acquired significant constitutional weight through judicial interpretation and legislative action. They serve as a constant reminder of the socio-economic goals that the Indian State is expected to pursue.

## Key Points for Conceptual Clarity and Memory

- Part IV, Articles 36–51
- Non-justiciable but fundamental in governance (Article 37)
- Inspired by Irish Constitution
- Aim: Welfare State
- Three broad groups: Socialist, Gandhian, Liberal-Intellectual
- Important additions by 42nd Amendment: 39A, 43A, 48A
- Article 38: Social order based on justice; minimise inequalities
- Article 39: Livelihood, distribution of resources, equal pay, protection of workers and children
- Article 40: Village panchayats
- Article 44: Uniform Civil Code
- Article 45: Early childhood care and education (modified)
- Article 48A: Environment and wildlife
- Article 51: International peace and security
- Relationship with Fundamental Rights: harmonious construction and mutual complementarity

The Directive Principles continue to guide policy-making and remain a vital part of India’s constitutional philosophy of transforming political democracy into social and economic democracy.
