# Economy: Money, Banking and Inflation — Foundational Concepts

## Nature and Functions of Money

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, a measure of value, a store of value, and a standard of deferred payment. In modern economies, money takes the form of currency (notes and coins) issued by the central authority and demand deposits held with commercial banks that can be withdrawn by cheque or electronic transfer.

The four classical functions of money are:

1. Medium of exchange: Money eliminates the inefficiencies of barter by providing a common intermediary in transactions.
2. Measure of value (unit of account): It provides a common denominator in which the value of goods and services can be expressed.
3. Store of value: Money allows purchasing power to be saved and used later, though inflation can erode this function.
4. Standard of deferred payment: It facilitates contracts that involve future payments, such as loans and salaries.

In addition, modern analysis emphasises money’s role in the transmission of monetary policy and as a liquid asset in portfolio decisions.

## Money Supply Measures in India

The Reserve Bank of India publishes several monetary aggregates:

- M0 (Reserve Money): Currency in circulation + bankers’ deposits with RBI + other deposits with RBI. It is also called high-powered money or base money.
- M1 (Narrow Money): Currency with the public + demand deposits with banks + other deposits with RBI.
- M2: M1 + savings deposits with post office savings banks.
- M3 (Broad Money): M1 + time deposits with banks. M3 is the most commonly used measure of money supply in policy discussions.
- M4: M3 + all deposits with post office savings banks (excluding National Savings Certificates).

Understanding these measures is important because changes in money supply influence interest rates, inflation, and economic activity.

## Commercial Banking: Basic Structure and Functions

Commercial banks are financial intermediaries that accept deposits from the public and provide loans and advances. Their primary functions include:

- Accepting deposits (demand, savings, and time deposits).
- Providing loans and advances (cash credit, overdraft, term loans, etc.).
- Creating credit: Through the process of fractional reserve banking, banks can expand the money supply beyond the initial deposit base.
- Agency functions: Collection of cheques, payment of bills, acting as trustees, etc.
- General utility functions: Providing lockers, issuing letters of credit, merchant banking services, etc.

The ability of banks to create credit depends on the cash reserve ratio (CRR) and the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) prescribed by the Reserve Bank of India, as well as on the demand for credit and the willingness of banks to lend.

## Central Banking and the Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), established in 1935 and nationalised in 1949, is the central bank of India. Its main functions are:

- Issuer of currency notes (except one-rupee notes and coins, which are issued by the Government of India).
- Banker to the government: manages public debt, provides ways and means advances, and acts as agent for government transactions.
- Banker to banks: maintains accounts of scheduled banks, provides refinance, and acts as lender of last resort.
- Supervisor and regulator of the banking system.
- Manager of foreign exchange reserves and the exchange rate regime (in coordination with the government).
- Formulator and implementer of monetary policy.

Monetary policy refers to the actions of the central bank to influence the availability, cost, and use of money and credit in order to achieve macroeconomic objectives such as price stability, growth, and financial stability.

### Instruments of Monetary Policy

- Repo rate: The rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds to commercial banks against government securities. An increase in repo rate makes borrowing costlier and is used to control inflation.
- Reverse repo rate: The rate at which the RBI absorbs liquidity from banks.
- Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): The percentage of net demand and time liabilities that banks must keep as cash with the RBI. Higher CRR reduces the funds available for lending.
- Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): The percentage of liabilities that banks must maintain in the form of specified liquid assets (mainly government securities).
- Open Market Operations (OMO): Buying and selling of government securities by the RBI to inject or absorb liquidity.
- Bank Rate: The longer-term lending rate of the RBI (less frequently used now as the primary tool).
- Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) and other liquidity adjustment facilities.

In recent years, the RBI has followed a flexible inflation targeting framework, with a target of 4 per cent CPI inflation (±2 per cent band), decided in consultation with the government.

## Inflation: Meaning, Types and Measurement

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time. It reduces the purchasing power of money.

### Types of Inflation (based on causes)

- Demand-pull inflation: Occurs when aggregate demand exceeds aggregate supply at the full employment level. Too much money chasing too few goods.
- Cost-push inflation: Arises from an increase in the cost of production (wages, raw materials, energy) that is passed on to consumers in the form of higher prices.
- Structural inflation: Results from structural rigidities in the economy, such as supply bottlenecks in agriculture or infrastructure.
- Hyperinflation: Extremely rapid and out-of-control inflation, often associated with the collapse of the currency.
- Stagflation: Combination of stagnant growth and high inflation.

### Measurement of Inflation in India

- Consumer Price Index (CPI): Measures the change in prices of a basket of goods and services consumed by households. Different CPI series exist (CPI for Industrial Workers, Agricultural Labourers, Rural Labourers, and the all-India CPI). The RBI uses CPI (Combined) as the nominal anchor for monetary policy.
- Wholesale Price Index (WPI): Measures price changes at the wholesale level. It has a higher weightage of manufactured products and primary articles.
- GDP Deflator: Ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP; covers the entire economy but is available with a lag.

Inflation rate is usually expressed as the percentage change in the index over a twelve-month period (year-on-year inflation).

### Effects of Inflation

Moderate inflation may accompany growth, but high and volatile inflation has several adverse effects:

- Erosion of real income, especially for fixed-income groups and the poor.
- Uncertainty that discourages long-term investment and savings.
- Distortion of relative prices and resource allocation.
- Redistribution of income from creditors to debtors if inflation is unanticipated.
- Possible wage-price spirals.
- Loss of international competitiveness if domestic inflation is higher than that of trading partners.

Deflation (sustained fall in prices) is also harmful because it can lead to postponed consumption, lower investment, and debt deflation.

## Interlinkages

Money supply growth, credit expansion, fiscal deficits, supply shocks, and external factors (oil prices, global commodity prices, exchange rate movements) all influence inflation. The central bank attempts to manage these pressures through monetary policy instruments while coordinating with fiscal authorities. Understanding the basic relationships between money, banking, and prices is essential for analysing macroeconomic developments and policy responses in the Indian economy.

## Key Concepts for Retention

- Functions of money: medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, standard of deferred payment
- Monetary aggregates: M0, M1, M3 (most commonly used)
- Commercial banks: deposit acceptance, credit creation, intermediation
- RBI functions: currency issue, banker to government and banks, monetary policy, regulation
- Policy instruments: repo rate, CRR, SLR, OMO
- Inflation types: demand-pull, cost-push, structural
- Measurement: CPI (policy anchor), WPI, GDP deflator
- Inflation targeting: 4% ± 2% CPI

These foundational concepts form the basis for understanding more advanced topics in monetary economics, banking regulation, and macroeconomic management.
