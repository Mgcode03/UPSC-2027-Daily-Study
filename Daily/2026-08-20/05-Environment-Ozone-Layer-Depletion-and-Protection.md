# Ozone Layer Depletion, Montreal Protocol and Global Protection Measures

The ozone layer is a region of the Earth’s stratosphere that contains a relatively high concentration of ozone (O₃) molecules. It plays a critical role in absorbing the major part of the Sun’s harmful ultraviolet-B radiation. Depletion of this layer increases the amount of UV-B radiation reaching the Earth’s surface, with adverse effects on human health, terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, and materials. Understanding the science of ozone depletion, the international response and the current status of recovery is essential for environmental geography and policy studies.

## Structure and Function of the Ozone Layer

Ozone is formed in the stratosphere by the action of ultraviolet radiation on oxygen molecules. The bulk of the ozone layer lies between 15 and 35 kilometres above the Earth’s surface, with maximum concentration at around 20–25 kilometres. Although ozone is a pollutant at ground level, in the stratosphere it acts as a protective shield. It absorbs UV-B radiation (wavelength 280–315 nm) and converts it into heat, thereby protecting living organisms from DNA damage, skin cancer, cataracts and suppression of the immune system. UV radiation also affects phytoplankton, the base of the marine food chain, and can damage crops and forests.

## Causes of Ozone Depletion

The primary cause of anthropogenic ozone depletion is the release of ozone-depleting substances (ODS), principally chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform and methyl bromide. These chemicals were widely used as refrigerants, propellants in aerosol sprays, solvents, foam-blowing agents and fire extinguishers. When released into the atmosphere they eventually reach the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation breaks them down, releasing chlorine or bromine atoms. A single chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules through catalytic cycles before it is removed from the stratosphere.

Polar stratospheric clouds that form in the extremely cold Antarctic winter provide surfaces for chemical reactions that convert inactive chlorine reservoirs into active forms. When sunlight returns in spring, rapid ozone destruction occurs, producing the Antarctic ozone hole. A similar but less severe phenomenon occurs over the Arctic.

## The Antarctic Ozone Hole

The term “ozone hole” refers to the severe depletion of ozone over Antarctica during the Southern Hemisphere spring (September–November). It was first reported in 1985 by scientists of the British Antarctic Survey. The hole is defined as the area where ozone concentration falls below 220 Dobson Units. The size and depth of the hole vary from year to year depending on temperature, dynamics and the residual amount of ODS. The discovery of the ozone hole provided dramatic evidence of the impact of human activities on the global atmosphere and galvanised international action.

## Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer

The Montreal Protocol, adopted in 1987 under the auspices of the United Nations Environment Programme, is the principal international treaty designed to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the production and consumption of ODS. It has been ratified by every country in the world, making it the most successful environmental treaty in history. The Protocol has been strengthened through successive amendments (London 1990, Copenhagen 1992, Montreal 1997, Beijing 1999, Kigali 2016) that accelerated phase-out schedules and added new substances.

Key features of the Protocol include:

- Binding phase-out schedules for different groups of ODS, with differentiated responsibilities for developed and developing countries.
- A Multilateral Fund to provide financial and technical assistance to developing countries to enable them to comply with their obligations.
- A system of reporting, verification and non-compliance procedures.
- Provisions for trade restrictions with non-parties to prevent leakage of controlled substances.

The Kigali Amendment (2016) added hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) to the list of controlled substances. HFCs do not deplete ozone but are potent greenhouse gases. Their phase-down contributes significantly to climate mitigation.

## Effectiveness and Current Status

Atmospheric concentrations of major CFCs have been declining. Global emissions of ODS have fallen dramatically. Scientific assessments indicate that the ozone layer is healing. The Antarctic ozone hole has shown signs of recovery, although full recovery to 1980 levels is not expected until the middle of the twenty-first century because of the long atmospheric lifetime of residual ODS. Recovery is also influenced by climate change, which affects stratospheric temperatures and circulation.

The success of the Montreal Protocol is attributed to clear scientific evidence, the availability of technological alternatives, the financial mechanism for developing countries, flexible adjustment procedures, and strong compliance.

## India’s Role and Domestic Measures

India is a party to the Montreal Protocol and has met its phase-out obligations. Production and consumption of CFCs, carbon tetrachloride and other major ODS have been phased out according to the agreed schedules. India has also ratified the Kigali Amendment. Domestic measures include regulations under the Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules, promotion of ozone-friendly technologies, and awareness programmes. The transition to alternatives has involved challenges of technology transfer, cost and the need to ensure that new substances have low global-warming potential.

## Linkages with Climate Change

Ozone depletion and climate change are interconnected. Many ODS are also greenhouse gases. The phase-out of ODS has therefore conferred a substantial climate benefit. Conversely, climate change affects the rate of ozone recovery through changes in stratospheric temperature and circulation patterns. The Kigali Amendment explicitly links ozone protection with climate mitigation by controlling HFCs. Sustainable management of the cold chain, air-conditioning and foam sectors requires integrated consideration of both ozone and climate impacts.

## Remaining Challenges

Despite overall success, challenges remain. Illegal trade in ODS, the presence of banks of ODS in existing equipment, the need to manage the transition to low-global-warming-potential alternatives, and the uncertain effects of climate change on ozone recovery require continued vigilance. Scientific monitoring, policy enforcement and international cooperation remain essential.

## Key Points for Retention

- Ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful UV-B radiation.
- Main ODS: CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform, methyl bromide.
- Chlorine and bromine atoms catalytically destroy ozone; polar stratospheric clouds enhance depletion over Antarctica.
- Montreal Protocol (1987) is the universal treaty for phasing out ODS; Multilateral Fund assists developing countries.
- Kigali Amendment (2016) controls HFCs for climate benefits.
- Ozone layer is healing; full recovery expected around mid-century.
- India has complied with phase-out schedules and ratified the Kigali Amendment.
- Ozone protection and climate mitigation are linked through the control of ODS and HFCs.
