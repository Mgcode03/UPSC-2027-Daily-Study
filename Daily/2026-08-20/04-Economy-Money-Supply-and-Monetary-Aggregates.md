# Money Supply Measures and Monetary Aggregates in India

Money supply refers to the total stock of money available in an economy at a particular point of time. In modern economies money serves as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value and a standard of deferred payment. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the monetary authority responsible for regulating the supply of money in India. Understanding the different measures of money supply is essential for analysing monetary policy, inflation, liquidity and the transmission mechanism of policy rates.

## Concept of Money and Near-Money

Money in the narrow sense consists of currency and demand deposits that are immediately available for transactions. Near-money or quasi-money includes assets that can be quickly converted into money with little loss of value, such as time deposits, savings deposits and certain money-market instruments. The distinction is important because the broader the definition of money, the greater the volume of liquidity captured.

## Monetary Aggregates in India

The RBI publishes four standard measures of money supply, designated M0, M1, M2 and M3. These measures progress from the narrowest to the broadest definition.

### Reserve Money (M0)

Reserve money, also called base money or high-powered money, consists of:

- Currency in circulation (notes and coins with the public and in bank tills)
- Bankers’ deposits with the RBI
- Other deposits with the RBI

Reserve money is the liability of the RBI and forms the base on which the banking system creates credit. Changes in reserve money directly affect the capacity of banks to expand deposits and loans. The RBI controls reserve money through open market operations, changes in the cash reserve ratio, and the management of foreign exchange reserves.

### Narrow Money (M1)

M1 is defined as:

- Currency with the public
- Demand deposits with the banking system
- Other deposits with the RBI

Demand deposits include current-account deposits and the demand-liability portion of savings deposits. M1 represents the most liquid form of money available for immediate transactions. It is closely watched as an indicator of transaction demand for money.

### Intermediate Aggregate (M2)

M2 equals M1 plus savings deposits with post office savings banks. This measure is less frequently emphasised in recent policy discussions but remains part of the official statistical framework.

### Broad Money (M3)

M3 is the most commonly used measure of money supply in India. It is defined as:

- M1
- Time deposits with the banking system

Time deposits include fixed deposits, recurring deposits and the time-liability portion of savings deposits. M3 captures the overall liquidity in the economy and is the key aggregate targeted or monitored by the RBI in its monetary policy framework. Growth rates of M3 are regularly reported and analysed in relation to inflation and economic activity.

## New Monetary Aggregates

In addition to the traditional measures, the RBI has introduced liquidity aggregates that incorporate a wider range of instruments. These include:

- L1 = M3 + all deposits with post office savings banks (excluding National Savings Certificates)
- L2 = L1 + term deposits with term-lending institutions and refinancing institutions + certificates of deposit issued by financial institutions
- L3 = L2 + public deposits of non-banking financial companies

These broader aggregates help assess overall liquidity conditions beyond the banking system.

## Determinants of Money Supply

The money supply is determined by the interaction of the central bank, commercial banks and the public. The volume of reserve money created by the RBI sets the outer limit. The credit multiplier (or money multiplier) determines how much broad money can be created from a given base. The money multiplier depends on the cash reserve ratio, the statutory liquidity ratio, the currency-deposit ratio preferred by the public, and the excess reserves maintained by banks.

Factors that increase money supply include:

- RBI’s purchase of government securities (open market operations)
- Reduction in reserve requirements
- Inflow of foreign exchange leading to increase in net foreign assets of the RBI
- Increase in government borrowing from the RBI (though this is now restricted)

Factors that decrease money supply include the reverse of the above operations.

## Money Multiplier

The money multiplier is the ratio of broad money (M3) to reserve money (M0). It indicates the extent of credit creation by the banking system. A higher multiplier implies greater expansion of money supply for a given increase in reserve money. The multiplier is influenced by policy variables (CRR, SLR) and behavioural variables (currency preference of the public, excess reserve preference of banks).

## Velocity of Money

Velocity of money is the rate at which money circulates in the economy. It is measured as the ratio of nominal GDP to the stock of money. Higher velocity means that a given stock of money supports a larger volume of transactions. Velocity is influenced by payment habits, availability of credit substitutes, interest rates and financial innovation. In the quantity theory of money framework, changes in money supply, velocity and output together determine the price level.

## Monetary Policy and Money Supply

The RBI’s monetary policy aims to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. Under the flexible inflation-targeting framework, the primary objective is to keep consumer price inflation at 4 per cent with a tolerance band of ±2 per cent. The policy repo rate is the main instrument. Changes in the repo rate influence short-term interest rates, which in turn affect credit demand, deposit rates and overall liquidity.

The RBI also uses quantitative instruments such as open market operations, the cash reserve ratio and the statutory liquidity ratio, and qualitative instruments such as margin requirements and selective credit controls. Liquidity management is conducted through the Liquidity Adjustment Facility, Standing Deposit Facility and other fine-tuning operations.

## Importance for Analysis

Monitoring money supply growth helps in assessing inflationary pressures, the stance of monetary policy and the availability of credit to the productive sectors. Excess growth of money supply relative to the growth of real output is often associated with inflationary tendencies. Conversely, very slow growth of money supply can constrain economic activity. The composition of money supply — the relative shares of currency, demand deposits and time deposits — also provides insights into the liquidity preference of the public and the stage of financial development.

## Key Points for Retention

- Reserve money (M0) = currency in circulation + bankers’ deposits with RBI + other deposits with RBI.
- Narrow money (M1) = currency with public + demand deposits + other deposits with RBI.
- Broad money (M3) = M1 + time deposits with banks; most widely used measure.
- Money multiplier = M3 / M0; depends on CRR, SLR, currency-deposit ratio and excess reserves.
- RBI controls reserve money through open market operations, reserve ratios and foreign exchange management.
- Velocity of money = nominal GDP / money stock.
- Flexible inflation targeting is the current monetary policy framework with CPI inflation target of 4 ± 2 per cent.
- Money supply analysis is central to understanding inflation, liquidity and policy transmission.
