# India’s Nuclear Doctrine and the Concept of Strategic Autonomy

India’s nuclear doctrine and the broader pursuit of strategic autonomy are central elements of its national security policy and foreign-policy orientation. Understanding the evolution, content and implications of the nuclear doctrine, together with the meaning of strategic autonomy in a changing international order, is important for the study of international relations and security studies in the Indian context.

## Historical Background of India’s Nuclear Programme

India’s nuclear programme began in the early years after independence with a focus on peaceful uses of atomic energy. The first nuclear test in 1974 (described as a peaceful nuclear explosion) demonstrated technical capability. After a period of ambiguity, India conducted a series of nuclear tests in May 1998 and declared itself a nuclear-weapon state. The tests were followed by the articulation of a formal nuclear doctrine.

The decision to weaponise was influenced by the regional security environment, particularly the nuclear capability of China and the nuclear and missile programmes of Pakistan, as well as by the desire to preserve strategic autonomy in a world where nuclear weapons remained central to the security policies of major powers.

## Key Elements of India’s Nuclear Doctrine

India’s nuclear doctrine, as publicly articulated, rests on several core principles.

**Credible minimum deterrence** is the central organising concept. India seeks to maintain a nuclear force sufficient to deter the use or threat of use of nuclear weapons against it, but does not pursue open-ended expansion or numerical parity with other nuclear powers. The size and composition of the arsenal are determined by the requirements of credibility rather than by an arms-race logic.

**No-first-use** is a declared policy. India has stated that it will not be the first to use nuclear weapons. Nuclear weapons are intended for retaliation against a nuclear attack on Indian territory or on Indian forces anywhere. The no-first-use posture is linked to the idea that nuclear weapons are political instruments of last resort rather than war-fighting tools.

**Retaliation will be massive and designed to inflict unacceptable damage.** The doctrine emphasises that any nuclear attack will be met with a response that is punitive and capable of causing unacceptable damage to the adversary. This formulation is intended to strengthen deterrence by removing ambiguity about the scale of retaliation.

**Civilian control** is firmly established. Nuclear command authority rests with the political leadership. The Nuclear Command Authority comprises a Political Council, headed by the Prime Minister, and an Executive Council. Decisions regarding the use of nuclear weapons are political decisions.

**Non-use against non-nuclear weapon states** is another declared element. India has stated that it will not use nuclear weapons against states that do not possess nuclear weapons and are not aligned with nuclear-weapon powers.

**Commitment to global nuclear disarmament** remains part of the official position. India continues to advocate the goal of a nuclear-weapon-free world through universal, non-discriminatory and verifiable disarmament, while maintaining its deterrent as long as other nuclear-weapon states retain their arsenals.

## Operational Aspects and Command Structure

The credibility of deterrence depends on survivability, reliability and the ability to communicate resolve. India has developed a triad of delivery systems — land-based missiles, aircraft and sea-based platforms — to enhance survivability. The sea-based leg, in particular, is considered important for a secure second-strike capability. Command and control arrangements are designed to ensure that nuclear weapons remain under firm political control while allowing for timely response if required.

## Strategic Autonomy — Meaning and Evolution

Strategic autonomy refers to the capacity of a state to make independent decisions on matters of security, foreign policy and economic engagement without being constrained by the preferences of other powers. For India, strategic autonomy has been a consistent theme since independence, evolving in form with changes in the international system.

In the early decades strategic autonomy was expressed through non-alignment — the refusal to join military alliances of the Cold War blocs while retaining the freedom to take positions on international issues according to India’s own assessment of its interests. Non-alignment did not mean neutrality or isolation; it meant the preservation of decision-making independence.

After the end of the Cold War the concept adapted to a more fluid international environment. India expanded its engagement with all major powers, deepened partnerships, and participated in multilateral and plurilateral groupings, while continuing to avoid exclusive military alliances that would limit its freedom of action. Strategic autonomy in the contemporary period involves the ability to maintain multiple partnerships, to cooperate on shared interests without being drawn into the conflicts of others, and to retain the capacity for independent judgement on issues of war, peace and economic policy.

## Linkages between Nuclear Doctrine and Strategic Autonomy

The nuclear doctrine supports strategic autonomy by reducing the vulnerability of India to nuclear coercion and by ensuring that decisions about the use of force remain under national control. A credible deterrent allows India to engage with other powers from a position of greater security and reduces the pressure to seek protection under another state’s nuclear umbrella. At the same time, the doctrine’s emphasis on minimum deterrence and no-first-use signals that India’s nuclear capability is defensive and stabilising rather than aggressive, thereby facilitating diplomatic engagement.

## Contemporary Challenges and Debates

Debates continue on several aspects of the doctrine. Some analysts question whether the no-first-use posture remains optimal in the face of evolving threats, including the possibility of tactical nuclear weapons or chemical and biological attacks. Others argue that the existing posture continues to serve India’s interests by reducing the risk of nuclear escalation and by reinforcing the country’s image as a responsible nuclear power. Questions also arise about the requirements of credible minimum deterrence in the light of technological change, missile defence developments and the modernisation of other nuclear arsenals.

Strategic autonomy faces challenges from intensifying great-power competition, the demands of technology partnerships, supply-chain vulnerabilities, and the pressure to take sides in geopolitical contests. Maintaining autonomy requires continuous investment in indigenous capabilities, diversification of partnerships, and clarity about core national interests.

## Significance for India’s International Role

India’s nuclear doctrine and its pursuit of strategic autonomy shape its role in global nuclear governance, its participation in export-control regimes, and its bilateral security relationships. The doctrine has enabled India to gain a degree of acceptance as a responsible nuclear power, reflected in civil nuclear cooperation agreements and membership of certain multilateral export-control regimes. Strategic autonomy allows India to contribute to regional and global stability on its own terms and to balance relations among major powers in a manner consistent with its long-term interests.

## Key Points for Retention

- India’s nuclear doctrine is based on credible minimum deterrence, no-first-use, massive retaliation to inflict unacceptable damage, civilian control, and non-use against non-nuclear weapon states.
- Nuclear Command Authority is headed by the Prime Minister; decisions on use are political.
- Strategic autonomy means the capacity for independent decision-making on security and foreign policy.
- It evolved from non-alignment during the Cold War to multi-alignment and diversified partnerships in the post-Cold War period.
- Nuclear capability supports strategic autonomy by reducing vulnerability to coercion.
- Contemporary debates concern the adequacy of no-first-use, the requirements of minimum deterrence, and the pressures of great-power competition on autonomy.
- The doctrine and the autonomy orientation together shape India’s role as a responsible nuclear power and an independent actor in international affairs.
