# The Mauryan Empire — Administration, Economy and Ashoka’s Dhamma

The Mauryan Empire (c. 321 BCE – 185 BCE) represents the first large-scale political unification of the Indian subcontinent. Founded by Chandragupta Maurya with the guidance of Chanakya (Kautilya), the empire expanded under Bindusara and reached its territorial zenith under Ashoka. The administrative system, economic organisation and the ideological experiment of Ashoka’s Dhamma form the core of Mauryan historical significance for the study of ancient Indian polity and society.

## Foundation and Expansion

Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 321 BCE. With the assistance of Chanakya he built a centralised state that extended from the north-western frontiers (after the defeat of Seleucus Nicator) to the eastern and southern regions of the subcontinent. Bindusara, his successor, consolidated the empire and maintained diplomatic contacts with the Hellenistic world. Ashoka, after a period of provincial governorship, ascended the throne around 268 BCE. The Kalinga war (c. 261 BCE) marked a turning point in his reign and in the ideological orientation of the empire.

## Sources of Information

Knowledge of the Mauryan period is derived from multiple sources. The Arthashastra attributed to Kautilya provides a detailed theoretical treatise on statecraft, economy and administration, though its exact date and authorship remain subjects of scholarly discussion. Megasthenes, the Seleucid ambassador, left an account (Indica) that survives only in fragments quoted by later classical writers. Ashokan edicts — rock edicts, pillar edicts and minor edicts — constitute the most authentic contemporary evidence. Buddhist and Jain literary traditions, Puranic genealogies, and archaeological remains including punch-marked coins, northern black polished ware and architectural fragments further enrich the picture.

## Central Administration

The Mauryan state was highly centralised. The king stood at the apex of the administrative hierarchy. He was the supreme commander of the army, the highest judicial authority, and the chief source of law and policy. The king was assisted by a council of ministers (mantriparishad). The Arthashastra describes various categories of officials — the mantrins, the amatyas and specialised departmental heads.

Key central officials included the Samaharta (chief collector of revenue), the Sannidhata (treasury officer), the Senapati (commander-in-chief), the Durgapala (in charge of forts), and the Antapala (frontier officer). The empire maintained an elaborate espionage system. Spies (gudhapurushas) operated in various disguises and reported directly to the king or his trusted officers. This system of intelligence was considered essential for the maintenance of internal security and for keeping provincial governors under check.

## Provincial and Local Administration

The empire was divided into provinces. Major provinces were governed by princes of the royal blood or by high officials designated as kumara or aryaputra. Important provincial centres included Taxila (north-west), Ujjain (west), Tosali (east) and Suvarnagiri (south). Provinces were further divided into districts (aharas or vishayas) administered by officials such as the Pradeshika, Rajuka and Yukta. The Rajukas combined revenue and judicial functions and were given considerable autonomy under Ashoka.

At the local level, villages formed the basic unit of administration. Village headmen and assemblies managed day-to-day affairs. Cities had their own administrative machinery. Megasthenes describes the municipal administration of Pataliputra as being managed by six boards, each consisting of five members, dealing with industrial arts, foreign residents, birth and death registration, trade and commerce, manufacturers, and collection of tithes on sales.

## Military Organisation

The Mauryan army was large and well organised. According to classical accounts it consisted of infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants. A separate navy is also mentioned. The army was maintained through regular recruitment and was paid from the state treasury. Forts and strategic garrisons protected the frontiers and important routes. The Kalinga campaign demonstrated the capacity of the Mauryan military machine, even though the human cost led Ashoka to renounce aggressive warfare.

## Revenue System and Economy

Agriculture formed the backbone of the Mauryan economy. The state claimed a share of the produce, commonly one-sixth, though the rate could vary. Land was classified according to fertility and irrigation. The state undertook irrigation works, including canals and reservoirs. Waste land was brought under cultivation through state initiative. The Arthashastra emphasises the importance of agriculture and prescribes measures for its promotion.

Trade and industry were regulated. The state maintained monopolies over certain commodities such as salt, mining and forest produce. Weights and measures were standardised. Roads were constructed and maintained to facilitate the movement of goods and troops. The royal highway from Pataliputra to Taxila is particularly noted. Punch-marked coins of silver and copper served as the medium of exchange. Urban centres flourished, and craft specialisation increased.

The state derived revenue from land tax, customs duties, tolls, taxes on professions, and fines. The treasury was carefully managed. The Arthashastra lays down detailed rules for accounting, auditing and prevention of embezzlement.

## Social Structure and Slavery

Mauryan society continued the varna framework, though occupational mobility and the presence of numerous jatis complicated the picture. Megasthenes’ account of seven classes of Indian society (philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans, soldiers, overseers and councillors) reflects an outsider’s attempt to understand social organisation rather than the indigenous varna-jati system. Slavery existed, but the conditions of dasas varied. The Arthashastra distinguishes different categories of slaves and lays down rules for their treatment and manumission.

## Ashoka’s Transformation after Kalinga

The Kalinga war resulted in massive loss of life and suffering. Ashoka’s thirteenth major rock edict records his remorse and his decision to abandon the path of military conquest in favour of Dhamma-vijaya — conquest through righteousness. This ideological shift did not mean the abandonment of the empire or of administrative control. Rather, it introduced a new moral dimension into the exercise of political power.

## Content of Ashoka’s Dhamma

Dhamma, as propounded by Ashoka, was not identical with any single religious doctrine. It drew upon ethical principles common to several traditions of the time. The principal elements included:

- Non-violence (ahimsa) towards living beings, including restrictions on animal sacrifice and festivals involving slaughter.
- Obedience to parents and elders.
- Respect for teachers and proper treatment of servants and dependents.
- Liberality towards friends, relatives, Brahmins and Shramanas.
- Truthfulness and purity of conduct.
- Moderation in expenditure and possession.
- Religious tolerance and the avoidance of sectarian discord.
- Humane treatment of prisoners and provision for their welfare.
- Planting of trees, digging of wells and construction of rest-houses along roads for the benefit of humans and animals.

Ashoka appointed special officers called Dhamma-mahamatras to propagate these principles and to look after the welfare of various social groups, including women, the aged and the distressed. He also ordered that his edicts be inscribed on rocks and pillars across the empire so that the message of Dhamma could reach the people.

## Nature and Purpose of Dhamma

Scholars have interpreted Ashoka’s Dhamma in different ways. Some view it as a personal moral code that the emperor sought to universalise. Others see it as a political strategy to unify a vast and culturally diverse empire under a common ethical umbrella. Still others regard it as an early form of social welfare policy combined with religious tolerance. The edicts themselves emphasise practical ethics rather than metaphysical speculation. Ashoka repeatedly stresses that the purpose of Dhamma is the happiness and well-being of all beings in this world and the next.

## Administrative Continuity under Ashoka

Despite the ideological change, the administrative machinery remained strong. Ashoka continued to maintain a large army for defensive purposes. Provincial administration functioned as before. The Rajukas were given independent authority in judicial matters so that they could dispense justice promptly. The emperor himself undertook tours of inspection (dhamma-yatras) in place of earlier pleasure tours. Communication between the centre and the provinces was maintained through a network of officials and messengers.

## Decline of the Mauryan Empire

After Ashoka the empire gradually weakened. Successors lacked his stature and administrative capacity. Economic pressures, possible over-centralisation, the growth of local powers, and external invasions contributed to the decline. By 185 BCE the last Mauryan ruler was overthrown by Pushyamitra Shunga, marking the end of the dynasty.

## Historical Significance

The Mauryan Empire demonstrated that a large territorial state could be sustained in the Indian subcontinent through centralised administration, a professional bureaucracy, a standing army and a systematic revenue system. Ashoka’s experiment with Dhamma introduced the idea that political power carries moral responsibility and that the welfare of subjects is a primary duty of the ruler. The edicts provide the earliest extensive corpus of royal inscriptions in India and remain a unique source for understanding the relationship between ideology and governance in ancient times. The administrative and economic institutions of the Mauryas influenced later Indian political thought and practice for centuries.

## Key Points for Retention

- Mauryan Empire founded by Chandragupta Maurya with Chanakya’s guidance; expanded under Bindusara and Ashoka.
- Highly centralised administration with king at the apex, council of ministers, specialised officials and an elaborate spy system.
- Provinces governed by royal princes or high officials; districts under Rajukas and Pradeshikas; villages as basic units.
- Economy based on agriculture with state share of produce; trade regulated; standardised weights, roads and coinage.
- Ashoka’s Dhamma after Kalinga war emphasised non-violence, tolerance, social welfare and ethical conduct.
- Dhamma-mahamatras appointed to propagate ethical principles and ensure welfare.
- Decline after Ashoka due to weak successors, economic strain and rise of regional powers.
- Significance lies in political unification, administrative sophistication and the moralisation of political authority.
