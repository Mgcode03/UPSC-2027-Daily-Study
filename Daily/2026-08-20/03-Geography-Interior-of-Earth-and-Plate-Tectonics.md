# Interior of the Earth and Fundamentals of Plate Tectonics

Understanding the internal structure of the Earth and the theory of plate tectonics is essential for explaining earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building, continental drift and the distribution of continents and oceans. These topics form a core part of physical geography for both Prelims and Mains.

## Sources of Information about the Earth’s Interior

Direct observation of the Earth’s interior is limited to a few kilometres through mining and deep drilling. Most knowledge is derived from indirect methods. Seismic waves generated by earthquakes provide the primary evidence. The behaviour of primary (P) waves and secondary (S) waves as they travel through the Earth reveals changes in density, rigidity and state of material at different depths. Other sources include the study of volcanic materials, meteorites (considered analogous to planetary interiors), gravity and magnetic anomalies, and laboratory experiments that simulate high pressure and temperature conditions.

## Layered Structure of the Earth

The Earth is differentiated into three major concentric layers — the crust, the mantle and the core — further subdivided on the basis of physical and chemical properties.

### Crust

The crust is the outermost solid layer. It is discontinuous and relatively thin. Continental crust is thicker (average 30–40 km, up to 70 km under young fold mountains) and less dense, composed predominantly of granitic rocks rich in silica and aluminium (sial). Oceanic crust is thinner (average 5–10 km) and denser, composed mainly of basaltic rocks rich in silica and magnesium (sima). The boundary between the crust and the mantle is marked by the Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho), where seismic wave velocity increases sharply.

### Mantle

The mantle extends from the Moho to a depth of approximately 2900 km. It constitutes about 83 per cent of the Earth’s volume and 67 per cent of its mass. The upper mantle includes the asthenosphere, a partially molten, ductile zone that lies beneath the rigid lithosphere. The lithosphere comprises the crust and the uppermost solid mantle and is broken into tectonic plates. The lower mantle is more rigid due to increasing pressure. The mantle is composed mainly of silicate minerals rich in iron and magnesium. Convection currents within the mantle are the primary driving force of plate movements.

### Core

The core extends from 2900 km to the centre of the Earth (6371 km). It is divided into the outer core and the inner core. The outer core is liquid and composed mainly of iron and nickel with lighter elements. The movement of this liquid metal generates the Earth’s magnetic field through the dynamo effect. The inner core is solid despite extremely high temperatures because of immense pressure. The boundary between the mantle and the core is the Gutenberg discontinuity. The boundary between the outer and inner core is the Lehmann discontinuity.

## Seismic Wave Evidence

P-waves are compressional waves that can travel through solids, liquids and gases. S-waves are shear waves that travel only through solids. The disappearance of S-waves beyond 103 degrees angular distance from the earthquake epicentre indicates a liquid outer core. The shadow zone of P-waves between 103 and 142 degrees further confirms the presence of a liquid outer core that refracts the waves. Sudden changes in velocity at the Moho, Gutenberg and Lehmann discontinuities allow precise mapping of internal boundaries.

## Theory of Plate Tectonics

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of modern geology. It states that the Earth’s lithosphere is divided into a number of rigid plates that move relative to one another over the ductile asthenosphere. The theory incorporates and supersedes earlier ideas of continental drift (Wegener) and sea-floor spreading (Hess and Dietz).

### Major and Minor Plates

There are seven major plates: African, Antarctic, Eurasian, Indo-Australian, North American, Pacific and South American. Several minor plates such as the Arabian, Caribbean, Cocos, Juan de Fuca, Nazca, Philippine and Scotia plates also exist. Plate boundaries are the sites of most tectonic activity.

### Types of Plate Boundaries

Three principal types of plate boundaries are recognised.

**Divergent boundaries** occur where plates move apart. New crust is created by upwelling of magma. Mid-oceanic ridges such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge are classic examples. Continental rifting, as in the East African Rift Valley, can eventually lead to the formation of new ocean basins.

**Convergent boundaries** occur where plates move towards each other. Three subtypes exist. Ocean–ocean convergence produces island arcs and deep-sea trenches (e.g., Mariana Trench and Mariana Islands). Ocean–continent convergence produces volcanic mountain chains and trenches (e.g., Andes and Peru–Chile Trench). Continent–continent convergence produces high fold mountains with little volcanic activity (e.g., Himalayas formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates).

**Transform boundaries** occur where plates slide past each other horizontally. Crust is neither created nor destroyed. The San Andreas Fault in California is a well-known example. Earthquakes are common along transform faults.

## Driving Mechanisms

Plate movement is driven primarily by convection currents in the mantle. Hot material rises at divergent boundaries, cools, and sinks at convergent boundaries. Ridge push (gravitational sliding of plates away from elevated mid-ocean ridges) and slab pull (the weight of the cold, dense subducting slab pulling the rest of the plate) are important forces. The relative contribution of each mechanism is still debated, but slab pull is widely regarded as the dominant force for many plates.

## Evidence Supporting Plate Tectonics

Multiple lines of evidence support the theory. The fit of continental margins, especially the coasts of South America and Africa, was noted by early observers. Matching geological formations, fossil assemblages (Glossopteris flora, Mesosaurus) and glacial deposits across continents now separated by oceans provide strong support for continental drift. Palaeomagnetic data show that continents have moved relative to the magnetic poles. Magnetic striping on either side of mid-ocean ridges records repeated reversals of the Earth’s magnetic field and confirms sea-floor spreading. Direct measurement of plate movements using GPS and other geodetic techniques shows rates of a few centimetres per year, consistent with geological estimates.

## Implications for Landforms and Hazards

Plate tectonics explains the global distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain belts. The Pacific Ring of Fire coincides with convergent and transform boundaries around the Pacific Plate. Fold mountains such as the Himalayas, Alps and Andes are products of convergence. Rift valleys and mid-ocean ridges result from divergence. Hotspots, such as the Hawaiian Islands, are explained by mantle plumes that remain relatively fixed while plates move over them.

Understanding plate boundaries is crucial for assessing seismic and volcanic risk, for the exploration of mineral and hydrocarbon resources (often concentrated along ancient or active plate margins), and for reconstructing the geological history of continents and ocean basins.

## Key Points for Retention

- Earth has three main layers: crust (continental and oceanic), mantle (including asthenosphere), and core (liquid outer, solid inner).
- Discontinuities: Moho (crust–mantle), Gutenberg (mantle–core), Lehmann (outer–inner core).
- Lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates that move over the asthenosphere.
- Three boundary types: divergent (constructive), convergent (destructive), transform (conservative).
- Driving forces: mantle convection, ridge push, slab pull.
- Evidence includes continental fit, fossils, palaeomagnetism, magnetic striping, and GPS measurements.
- Plate tectonics explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building and the distribution of continents and oceans.
