# Local Self-Government: Panchayati Raj Institutions and Urban Local Bodies

## Introduction to Local Self-Government

Local self-government forms the third tier of governance in India, below the Union and the States. It brings democracy and administration closer to the people. The idea rests on the principle that local problems are best understood and solved by local people. The Constitution of India originally did not give detailed provisions for local bodies. They existed as creatures of state legislation. A major transformation came through the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments in 1992, which conferred constitutional status on rural and urban local bodies respectively.

These amendments added Part IX (The Panchayats) and Part IX-A (The Municipalities) to the Constitution, along with the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules listing the functional subjects. The objective was to create institutions of self-government that enable people’s participation in planning and implementation of development programmes, ensure accountability, and promote social justice.

## Historical Background

Village panchayats have deep roots in Indian history. Ancient texts refer to sabhas and samitis. In the medieval period, village communities managed local affairs with a degree of autonomy. During British rule, local bodies were introduced primarily for administrative convenience and revenue collection. Lord Mayo’s Resolution of 1870 and Lord Ripon’s Resolution of 1882 are landmarks. Ripon’s resolution is often called the Magna Carta of local self-government because it emphasised elected representatives, financial autonomy, and non-official majorities.

After independence, the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended a three-tier Panchayati Raj system: Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the block level, and Zila Parishad at the district level. The Ashok Mehta Committee (1978) suggested a two-tier system and greater political status. The G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985) and L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986) further stressed the need for constitutional recognition. These recommendations culminated in the 73rd and 74th Amendments.

## The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The 73rd Amendment gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs). Key features include:

- **Three-tier structure**: Gram Panchayat at village level, intermediate level (block or taluka), and district level (Zila Parishad). States with population below 20 lakh may not have the intermediate level.
- **Gram Sabha**: The foundation of the system. It consists of all registered voters in a village or group of villages. It is a deliberative body that discusses plans, identifies beneficiaries, and reviews the work of the Gram Panchayat.
- **Direct elections**: All members at all three levels are directly elected by the people. The chairperson of the intermediate and district levels may be elected indirectly as provided by state law.
- **Reservation of seats**: Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population. Not less than one-third of the total seats (including those reserved for SC/ST) are reserved for women. States may provide reservation for Other Backward Classes.
- **Duration**: Five years. If dissolved earlier, elections must be held within six months.
- **State Election Commission**: Conducts elections to PRIs. The Governor appoints the State Election Commissioner.
- **State Finance Commission**: Constituted every five years to review the financial position of PRIs and recommend principles for distribution of taxes, duties, and grants.
- **Eleventh Schedule**: Lists 29 subjects over which Panchayats may exercise powers, including agriculture, land improvement, minor irrigation, animal husbandry, fisheries, social forestry, small-scale industries, rural housing, drinking water, roads, education, health, and poverty alleviation programmes.

The Amendment does not mandate that all 29 subjects must be transferred. States decide the extent of devolution through legislation. This has led to variation across states in the actual powers and resources available to PRIs.

## Structure and Functions of Panchayati Raj Institutions

### Gram Sabha
The Gram Sabha is the basic unit of democracy. It meets at least twice a year (many states prescribe more frequent meetings). Its functions include approving annual plans and budgets, identifying beneficiaries for schemes, reviewing the reports of the Gram Panchayat, and promoting social harmony. In some states, the Gram Sabha has been empowered to conduct social audits of government programmes.

### Gram Panchayat
It is the executive body at the village level. It consists of elected members (Panchs) and a Sarpanch (president). The Sarpanch is usually elected directly by voters or by the members. The Gram Panchayat prepares plans for economic development and social justice, implements schemes related to the subjects in the Eleventh Schedule, maintains records, and collects certain local taxes and fees. It is responsible for basic services such as sanitation, street lighting, drinking water, and maintenance of village roads.

### Intermediate Level (Panchayat Samiti / Block Panchayat)
It coordinates the activities of Gram Panchayats within a block and links them with the Zila Parishad. It prepares block-level plans, supervises the implementation of schemes, and provides technical support. The chairperson is often elected by the members.

### Zila Parishad
It is the apex body at the district level. It coordinates the plans of the intermediate bodies, prepares district plans, and advises the state government on matters of rural development. It has an important role in integrating rural development programmes.

## Financial Resources of PRIs

PRIs suffer from inadequate financial resources. Their sources include:

- Own taxes and fees (property tax, profession tax, market fees, water charges, etc.) – often limited in yield.
- Share of state taxes as recommended by the State Finance Commission.
- Grants from the Central and State governments, including those under centrally sponsored schemes.
- Funds under the recommendations of the Central Finance Commission (which now also recommends grants to local bodies).

The lack of adequate taxation powers, weak collection machinery, and dependence on grants constrain their autonomy. Many PRIs function more as implementing agencies of higher-level schemes than as true institutions of self-government.

## Challenges Facing Panchayati Raj

Several issues limit the effectiveness of PRIs:

- Incomplete devolution of functions, functionaries, and funds (the three Fs).
- Parallel bodies and schemes that bypass PRIs.
- Capacity deficits among elected representatives, especially first-time women and SC/ST members.
- Interference by state governments and bureaucrats.
- Irregular elections or delays in some states in the past (though the constitutional mandate has improved the situation).
- Weak Gram Sabhas in practice – low attendance, elite capture, and lack of awareness.
- Limited financial autonomy and poor revenue generation.

Despite these challenges, PRIs have increased political participation of women and marginalised groups. Reservation has brought a large number of women into formal politics, creating a new generation of local leaders.

## The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The 74th Amendment provides the constitutional framework for urban local bodies (ULBs). It recognises three types of municipalities:

- Nagar Panchayat for transitional areas (rural to urban).
- Municipal Council for smaller urban areas.
- Municipal Corporation for larger urban areas.

Key provisions mirror those of the 73rd Amendment: direct elections, reservation for SC/ST and women (not less than one-third), five-year tenure, State Election Commission, State Finance Commission, and a Twelfth Schedule listing 18 functional items such as urban planning, regulation of land use, water supply, public health, sanitation, fire services, urban forestry, slum improvement, and urban poverty alleviation.

## Structure of Urban Local Bodies

Municipal Corporations have a Mayor (often ceremonial) and a Municipal Commissioner (executive authority, usually an IAS officer). Municipal Councils have a President or Chairperson. Ward Committees may be constituted in larger cities to bring governance closer to neighbourhoods.

The Amendment also provides for District Planning Committees (to consolidate rural and urban plans) and Metropolitan Planning Committees in metropolitan areas.

## Issues in Urban Local Governance

Urban local bodies face acute challenges:

- Rapid urbanisation has outpaced the capacity of municipalities.
- Severe shortage of financial resources; property tax collection remains weak in many cities.
- Overlapping jurisdictions with state agencies and parastatals.
- Inadequate human resources and technical capacity.
- Political interference and frequent supersession in the past.
- Poor service delivery in water, sanitation, solid waste management, and public transport.
- Growth of informal settlements and challenges of inclusion.

Recent reforms such as the Smart Cities Mission, AMRUT, and Swachh Bharat Mission have sought to strengthen urban infrastructure and governance, but the core issues of financial and functional autonomy persist.

## Constitutional Significance and Democratic Value

The 73rd and 74th Amendments represent a major step towards deepening democracy. They institutionalised the principle of subsidiarity – decisions should be taken at the lowest appropriate level. They also advanced social justice through reservation. The success of local self-government ultimately depends on the willingness of state governments to devolve real power and resources, the capacity of local representatives, and active citizen participation through Gram Sabhas and ward committees.

Local bodies are not merely administrative units; they are schools of democracy where ordinary citizens learn the art of self-rule. Strengthening them is essential for inclusive development and responsive governance in a diverse and large country like India.

## Key Points for Retention

- 73rd Amendment: Part IX, Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects), three-tier PRIs, Gram Sabha, reservation (SC/ST + 1/3 women), State Election Commission, State Finance Commission.
- 74th Amendment: Part IX-A, Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects), Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council, Municipal Corporation.
- Duration: five years; elections within six months of dissolution.
- District Planning Committee and Metropolitan Planning Committee.
- Major challenge: incomplete devolution of the three Fs – functions, functionaries, funds.
- Ripon’s Resolution of 1882 is a historical milestone.
- Balwant Rai Mehta Committee recommended the three-tier structure.

This framework of local self-government is central to understanding democratic decentralisation in India and remains a living area of constitutional and political evolution.
