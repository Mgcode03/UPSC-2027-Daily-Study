# Indian Agriculture: Features, Challenges, and Reforms

## Significance of Agriculture

Agriculture remains the largest source of livelihood in India even though its share in GDP has declined over the decades. It employs nearly half of the workforce (depending on the data source and definition) and is critical for food security, raw materials for industry, and demand for industrial goods. The performance of agriculture influences rural incomes, poverty, inflation (especially food inflation), and overall economic stability.

## Key Features of Indian Agriculture

### Dependence on Monsoon
A large part of cultivated area is still rain-fed. The south-west monsoon accounts for the bulk of annual rainfall. Variability in monsoon timing and quantity causes fluctuations in production and farmer incomes. Irrigation has expanded, yet rain-fed agriculture remains significant, especially for coarse cereals, pulses, and oilseeds in many regions.

### Small and Fragmented Holdings
The average size of operational holdings is small (around 1 hectare in recent agricultural censuses) and has been declining due to population pressure and subdivision. Fragmentation increases the cost of cultivation, reduces the scope for mechanisation, and makes investment in land improvement difficult. A large number of holdings are marginal (less than 1 hectare).

### Cropping Pattern
Food grains dominate – rice and wheat are the major cereals. The Green Revolution concentrated on wheat and rice in irrigated areas of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, and parts of other states. Coarse cereals (jowar, bajra, maize, ragi), pulses, and oilseeds are important in rain-fed and semi-arid regions. Commercial crops include sugarcane, cotton, jute, tea, coffee, rubber, spices, and horticultural crops. Diversification towards high-value crops (fruits, vegetables, floriculture, dairy, poultry) has been increasing but remains limited relative to potential.

### Low Productivity
Yields of many crops are lower than in major producing countries and often below potential. Causes include small holdings, inadequate irrigation, low use of quality seeds and fertilisers in some regions, poor soil health, pest and disease losses, and post-harvest losses. There is a wide gap between demonstration yields and actual farm yields.

### Seasonality and Underemployment
Agricultural activity is seasonal. This leads to underemployment and disguised unemployment in rural areas. Rural non-farm employment and migration are responses to this reality.

### Institutional Features
A large proportion of farmers are small and marginal. Tenancy exists in various forms, though formal tenancy is often restricted by law. Access to institutional credit, insurance, and markets varies widely. Women contribute substantially to agricultural labour but often have limited land rights and access to resources.

## The Green Revolution and Its Legacy

The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s involved high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, chemical fertilisers, irrigation, and pesticides. It transformed India from a food-deficit country facing shortages to one that achieved self-sufficiency in food grains and built buffer stocks. Production and productivity rose sharply in the north-western region and some other irrigated areas.

Positive outcomes: food security at the national level, reduced dependence on imports, and the creation of a surplus that supported public distribution.

Limitations and problems:
- Regional concentration – benefits accrued mainly to irrigated areas; rain-fed regions lagged.
- Crop concentration – wheat and rice expanded at the expense of coarse cereals, pulses, and oilseeds in some areas.
- Environmental costs – over-extraction of groundwater, soil degradation, nutrient imbalance, and pesticide-related health and ecological issues.
- Social differentiation – larger farmers with access to irrigation and credit benefited more; small farmers and tenants faced constraints.
- Plateauing of yields in the Green Revolution belt and the need for a second generation of reforms.

## Major Challenges

### Low Income and Agrarian Distress
Farmer incomes remain low and uncertain. Cost of cultivation has risen; output prices are volatile. Indebtedness, crop failure due to weather or pests, and inadequate risk coverage contribute to distress. Incidents of farmer suicides in certain regions have highlighted the severity of the problem.

### Marketing and Price Realisation
A significant share of produce is sold immediately after harvest at low prices. Agricultural markets (mandis) under the APMC Acts have often been characterised by limited competition, high transaction costs, and intermediaries. Although reforms have aimed at creating a national market and allowing alternative marketing channels, implementation and outcomes vary across states.

### Post-Harvest Losses and Value Addition
Inadequate storage, cold chains, and processing facilities lead to losses, especially of perishables. The share of processing in agriculture is relatively low compared to potential. Strengthening the food processing industry can raise farmer incomes and reduce waste.

### Soil Health and Sustainability
Imbalanced use of fertilisers (excessive nitrogen relative to phosphorus and potassium in many areas), declining organic matter, micronutrient deficiencies, and soil erosion affect long-term productivity. The Soil Health Card scheme aims to promote balanced nutrient management.

### Water Stress
Agriculture accounts for the bulk of water use. Inefficient irrigation methods and the cultivation of water-intensive crops in water-stressed regions (e.g., rice and sugarcane in certain areas) exacerbate groundwater depletion and inter-sectoral competition for water.

### Climate Change
Rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and extreme events (droughts, floods, unseasonal rains) pose increasing risks. Adaptation measures – resilient varieties, improved weather advisories, crop insurance, and diversified farming systems – are essential.

### Credit, Insurance, and Extension
Access to timely and adequate institutional credit remains uneven. Crop insurance coverage and claim settlement have improved with schemes like PM Fasal Bima Yojana but face challenges of awareness, assessment, and delayed payments. Agricultural extension services need strengthening to disseminate technology and best practices.

## Policy Measures and Reforms

### Input Support
Subsidies on fertilisers, power, and irrigation have been important but have also contributed to imbalances and fiscal costs. Direct benefit transfer for fertilisers and attempts to rationalise subsidies are ongoing. Quality seed systems and public research (ICAR and state agricultural universities) remain critical.

### Price Support
Minimum Support Prices (MSP) for major crops aim to provide a floor price. Procurement is effective mainly for wheat and rice in certain states. Expanding the reach of MSP or alternative income support mechanisms is a continuing debate. The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends MSPs.

### Market Reforms
Efforts to reform agricultural marketing include allowing private markets, contract farming, direct marketing, and the creation of e-NAM (electronic National Agriculture Market) to integrate mandis. The farm laws of 2020 (later repealed) sought to liberalise marketing, facilitate contract farming, and remove stock limits; they generated significant debate and protest. The underlying need for competitive and efficient markets remains.

### Irrigation and Water Management
Expansion of irrigation, promotion of micro-irrigation (drip and sprinkler), watershed development, and participatory irrigation management are key. The Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana aims at “Har Khet Ko Pani” and improved water use efficiency.

### Diversification and Allied Activities
Promotion of horticulture, dairy, poultry, fisheries, and agro-forestry can raise incomes and reduce risk. Operation Flood transformed dairy. Blue Revolution and similar initiatives target fisheries. Animal husbandry provides supplementary income and nutrition.

### Institutional and Structural Measures
Land reforms (abolition of intermediaries, tenancy regulation, ceiling laws) had mixed success; tenancy remains informal in many areas. Consolidation of holdings, promotion of Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs), and cooperative models can improve bargaining power and scale. Digital agriculture, precision farming, and start-up innovations are emerging areas.

### Income Support and Social Security
Schemes such as PM-KISAN provide direct income support to farmer families. Crop insurance, pension schemes for farmers, and rural employment programmes (MGNREGA) provide a safety net and can support agricultural labour demand in lean seasons.

## Way Forward

Sustainable and inclusive agricultural growth requires:
- Raising productivity through technology, extension, and soil and water management.
- Diversification towards high-value and resilient crops and allied activities.
- Efficient and competitive markets with better price realisation for farmers.
- Risk mitigation through insurance, weather advisories, and diversified income sources.
- Sustainable use of water and soil resources.
- Strengthening of FPOs, cooperatives, and value chains.
- Policy stability and investment in rural infrastructure (roads, storage, cold chains, power).

Agriculture is not merely a sector of production; it is a way of life for millions and the foundation of food security and rural prosperity. Transforming it into a viable, sustainable, and remunerative activity remains one of the central challenges of Indian economic development.

## Key Points for Retention

- Agriculture: large share of employment, declining share of GDP, critical for food security.
- Features: monsoon dependence, small and fragmented holdings, low productivity, regional variation.
- Green Revolution: HYV seeds, wheat and rice, north-western focus; food self-sufficiency with regional and environmental costs.
- Challenges: low farmer incomes, marketing inefficiencies, post-harvest losses, soil and water stress, climate risk.
- MSP and procurement mainly effective for wheat and rice in select states.
- e-NAM, FPOs, micro-irrigation, Soil Health Cards, PM-KISAN, crop insurance – important contemporary interventions.
- Need for diversification, value addition, and sustainable resource use.
