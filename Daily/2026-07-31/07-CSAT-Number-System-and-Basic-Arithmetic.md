# CSAT: Number System and Basic Arithmetic Foundations

## Introduction to the Number System

A strong grasp of the number system is essential for CSAT quantitative aptitude. Questions often test conceptual understanding of types of numbers, divisibility, remainders, and basic operations rather than lengthy calculations.

## Types of Numbers

- **Natural numbers**: 1, 2, 3, 4, … (counting numbers). Some definitions include 0.
- **Whole numbers**: 0, 1, 2, 3, …
- **Integers**: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …
- **Rational numbers**: Numbers that can be expressed as p/q where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. Includes fractions and terminating or repeating decimals.
- **Irrational numbers**: Numbers that cannot be expressed as p/q. Their decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating (e.g., √2, π).
- **Real numbers**: All rational and irrational numbers.
- **Prime numbers**: Natural numbers greater than 1 that have no positive divisors other than 1 and themselves (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …). 2 is the only even prime.
- **Composite numbers**: Natural numbers greater than 1 that are not prime (4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …).
- **Co-prime (relatively prime) numbers**: Two numbers are co-prime if their greatest common divisor (GCD) is 1. They need not be prime themselves (e.g., 8 and 9).

## Divisibility Rules

Quick divisibility checks save time:

- **By 2**: Last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8).
- **By 3**: Sum of digits is divisible by 3.
- **By 4**: Last two digits form a number divisible by 4.
- **By 5**: Last digit is 0 or 5.
- **By 6**: Divisible by both 2 and 3.
- **By 8**: Last three digits form a number divisible by 8.
- **By 9**: Sum of digits is divisible by 9.
- **By 10**: Last digit is 0.
- **By 11**: Difference between the sum of digits in odd positions and the sum of digits in even positions is a multiple of 11 (including 0).

These rules help in checking options and simplifying problems involving factors and multiples.

## Factors and Multiples

- A **factor** (or divisor) of a number divides it exactly.
- A **multiple** of a number is the product of that number and an integer.
- **Highest Common Factor (HCF) / Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**: The largest number that divides two or more numbers exactly.
- **Least Common Multiple (LCM)**: The smallest number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

For two numbers a and b: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b.

This relation is useful when one of HCF or LCM is known.

Methods to find HCF and LCM:
- Prime factorisation: Take minimum powers for HCF and maximum powers for LCM.
- Division method (Euclidean algorithm for HCF): HCF(a, b) = HCF(b, a mod b), repeated until remainder is 0.

## Remainder Theorem and Basic Modular Ideas

When a number n is divided by d, we have n = q × d + r, where 0 ≤ r < d. r is the remainder.

Useful patterns:
- Remainders when successive powers are divided by a number often cycle.
- (a + b) mod m = [(a mod m) + (b mod m)] mod m.
- (a × b) mod m = [(a mod m) × (b mod m)] mod m.

These help in finding remainders of large expressions without computing the full number.

## Fractions and Decimals

- Proper fraction: numerator < denominator.
- Improper fraction: numerator ≥ denominator.
- Mixed number: whole number plus proper fraction.
- Operations: common denominator for addition/subtraction; multiply numerators and denominators for multiplication; multiply by reciprocal for division.

Conversion between fractions and decimals, and comparison of fractions, are frequently tested. Cross-multiplication is a quick way to compare two positive fractions: a/b vs c/d — compare a×d and b×c.

## Percentages

Percentage means “per hundred.” x% = x/100.

Key ideas:
- To convert a fraction to a percentage: multiply by 100.
- Increase or decrease by x%: multiply by (1 + x/100) or (1 − x/100).
- Successive percentage changes: if a number increases by a% and then by b%, the net change is not simply a + b. Net multiplier = (1 + a/100)(1 + b/100). The equivalent single change is a + b + (ab)/100.
- Percentage points vs percentage: a change from 20% to 30% is an increase of 10 percentage points, but a 50% increase relative to the original rate.

Percentages appear in data interpretation, profit and loss, and comprehension-linked quantitative questions.

## Ratio and Proportion

- Ratio a : b means a/b.
- Proportion: a : b = c : d means a/b = c/d, or ad = bc.
- If a : b = c : d, then a and d are extremes, b and c are means.
- Dividing a quantity in the ratio m : n: first part = (m/(m+n)) × quantity; second = (n/(m+n)) × quantity.

Ratios are used in mixture problems, sharing, and scaling.

## Averages

Average (arithmetic mean) = Sum of observations / Number of observations.

- If a number is replaced, the change in sum equals the change in average times the number of observations.
- Weighted average: when groups have different sizes, Average = (n1×a1 + n2×a2 + …) / (n1 + n2 + …).

Averages are central to data interpretation sets.

## Simplification and Approximation

CSAT often includes simplification using BODMAS/BIDMAS order: Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division and Multiplication (left to right), Addition and Subtraction (left to right).

Approximation is useful when options are sufficiently spread out: round numbers sensibly and compute.

## Squares, Cubes, and Number Properties

Knowing squares up to at least 30 and cubes up to 20 helps speed. Properties:

- A perfect square ends only with 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9 (never 2, 3, 7, 8).
- Digital root of a perfect square is 1, 4, 7, or 9 (or 0 for multiples of 9 in certain cases – more precisely, digital roots of squares are limited).
- Difference of consecutive squares: (n+1)² − n² = 2n + 1 (odd numbers).

## Practice Orientation

CSAT questions on the number system typically involve:

- Finding HCF/LCM in word problems (e.g., bells ringing together, tile sizes).
- Remainder-based questions.
- Divisibility and digit-related problems.
- Percentage and ratio applications in everyday contexts.
- Average of consecutive numbers or with missing values.

Conceptual clarity and familiarity with patterns matter more than memorising many formulae. Regular practice with timed sets builds both accuracy and speed.

## Key Points for Retention

- Prime, composite, co-prime; only even prime is 2.
- Divisibility rules for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11.
- HCF × LCM = product of two numbers.
- Remainder: n = q × d + r, 0 ≤ r < d.
- Successive percentage change: a + b + (ab)/100.
- Ratio division and weighted average formulae.
- BODMAS order of operations.
- Perfect square ending digits: 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9.

Mastery of these foundations enables confident handling of a large portion of CSAT quantitative questions and supports logical reasoning that involves numerical relationships.
