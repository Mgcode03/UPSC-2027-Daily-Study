# India’s Relations with Neighbouring Countries – Conceptual Overview

## Strategic Significance of the Neighbourhood

India shares land or maritime boundaries with several countries: Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives. Afghanistan is also part of the extended neighbourhood. The neighbourhood occupies a central place in India’s foreign policy for reasons of security, economic interdependence, cultural ties, and regional stability.

A stable and cooperative neighbourhood enhances India’s development prospects and strategic autonomy. Conversely, instability or hostility in the neighbourhood consumes diplomatic and security resources and constrains policy choices.

## Guiding Principles and Approaches

### Neighbourhood First

India has articulated a “Neighbourhood First” policy that prioritises relations with immediate neighbours. The emphasis is on connectivity, economic cooperation, people-to-people ties, and addressing asymmetries through development partnership rather than dominance.

### Non-Reciprocity and Asymmetry Sensitivity

Given the disparity in size and power between India and most of its smaller neighbours, India has often adopted a non-reciprocal approach – offering greater market access, concessional finance, and unilateral gestures to build confidence.

### Bilateralism and Multilateralism

While bilateral engagement remains the primary mode, India also participates in regional and sub-regional frameworks (SAARC, BIMSTEC, BBIN, IORA) to promote cooperation. SAARC has faced limitations due to political differences; BIMSTEC and other sub-regional initiatives have gained relative prominence.

## Country-Specific Conceptual Themes

### Pakistan

Relations are shaped by the legacy of Partition, the Kashmir dispute, cross-border terrorism, and nuclear deterrence. Periods of engagement (composite dialogue, back-channel efforts) have alternated with periods of tension. Core challenges include terrorism emanating from Pakistani territory, the status of Jammu and Kashmir, and the management of a nuclearised rivalry. Trade and people-to-people contact remain limited relative to potential.

### China

The relationship combines elements of cooperation (trade, multilateral forums such as BRICS and SCO) and competition (border dispute, influence in the neighbourhood and the Indian Ocean, differing strategic outlooks). The boundary question remains unresolved; the 2020 Galwan clash marked a significant deterioration. Economic interdependence coexists with strategic mistrust. Managing the relationship requires a balance of deterrence, dialogue, and diversification of economic partnerships.

### Bangladesh

One of the more successful neighbourhood relationships in recent decades. Cooperation covers security (counter-terrorism, border management), connectivity, energy, trade, and water resources. The Land Boundary Agreement and resolution of maritime boundary issues removed major irritants. Challenges include water-sharing (Teesta), illegal migration concerns, and the need to sustain political consensus on both sides.

### Nepal

Deep historical, cultural, and open-border ties. India is a major development partner and transit provider. Periodic tensions arise over border issues, perceived interference, and Nepal’s desire to diversify its external relations. The relationship requires sensitive handling of sovereignty concerns while maintaining the special character of ties.

### Bhutan

A uniquely close relationship based on friendship, mutual security interests, and extensive development cooperation. The Treaty of Friendship has evolved; Bhutan’s sovereignty and guided foreign policy coordination remain distinctive features. Hydropower cooperation is a major economic pillar.

### Sri Lanka

Relations encompass ethnic issues (Tamil question), strategic concerns in the Indian Ocean, economic assistance, and connectivity. India’s approach has combined support for a united Sri Lanka with concern for the welfare of Tamil communities. Chinese presence and investments in Sri Lanka have added a strategic dimension. Debt, political stability, and maritime security are ongoing themes.

### Myanmar

India’s gateway to Southeast Asia and critical for the Act East Policy. Issues include border management, insurgency linkages, connectivity projects (Kaladan, India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway), and the political situation inside Myanmar. Balancing principles of democracy with strategic and security interests has been a persistent dilemma.

### Maldives

Maritime neighbour of high strategic importance in the Indian Ocean. Relations have fluctuated with changes in government in Male. India provides development assistance, capacity building, and security cooperation. Competition with other external powers for influence is a structural feature.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

- **Connectivity**: Physical (roads, railways, ports, inland waterways), digital, and energy connectivity are central to integrating the neighbourhood and reducing the costs of distance.
- **Security**: Counter-terrorism, border management, maritime domain awareness, and defence cooperation.
- **Development Partnership**: Lines of credit, grants, capacity building, and technical assistance as instruments of influence and goodwill.
- **People-to-People Ties**: Cultural affinities, diaspora, education, and tourism as stabilising factors.
- **External Powers**: The growing presence of China and other actors in the neighbourhood complicates India’s traditional influence and requires calibrated responses.

## Conceptual Challenges

- Managing asymmetry without appearing hegemonic.
- Domestic politics in neighbouring countries that sometimes use anti-India sentiment.
- Institutional weaknesses in regional organisations.
- Balancing immediate security concerns with long-term cooperative goals.
- Coordinating economic, security, and soft-power instruments into a coherent neighbourhood strategy.

A stable, prosperous, and cooperative neighbourhood remains a core objective of India’s foreign policy. Achieving it requires sustained political attention, institutional capacity, and the ability to adapt to the changing strategic landscape of South Asia and the Indian Ocean region.
