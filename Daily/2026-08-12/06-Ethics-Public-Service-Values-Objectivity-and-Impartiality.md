# Public Service Values: Objectivity and Impartiality

## Foundational Values in Civil Services

Civil servants in a democracy are expected to uphold a set of values that ensure the administration remains fair, efficient, and trustworthy. Among these, objectivity and impartiality occupy a central place. They are closely linked to the principles of rule of law, equality before law, and non-arbitrariness.

## Meaning of Objectivity

Objectivity in public service means basing decisions on facts, evidence, and rational analysis rather than on personal feelings, prejudices, or external pressures. An objective civil servant:

- Collects and evaluates information systematically.
- Weighs alternatives on the basis of merit and public interest.
- Avoids letting personal likes, dislikes, or ideological preferences distort judgement.
- Documents the reasoning behind decisions so that they can be scrutinised.

Objectivity does not mean the absence of values; it means that values are applied consistently and transparently through established procedures and criteria.

## Meaning of Impartiality

Impartiality means treating all persons and groups equally without favouritism or discrimination. In the administrative context it requires:

- Equal application of rules and procedures to everyone, regardless of status, wealth, political affiliation, caste, religion, gender, or personal relationship.
- Refusal to favour or disfavour any individual or group for extraneous reasons.
- Maintaining distance from political parties and partisan controversies while remaining accountable to the elected government of the day.

Impartiality is both a personal virtue and an institutional requirement. The civil service as a permanent institution must serve successive governments with equal loyalty while remaining neutral in party politics.

## Why These Values Matter

1. **Legitimacy of Administration**: Citizens accept administrative decisions more readily when they perceive them as fair and based on rules rather than on personal whim or influence.
2. **Rule of Law**: Objectivity and impartiality operationalise the principle that law applies equally to all.
3. **Efficiency and Effectiveness**: Decisions grounded in evidence are more likely to achieve intended policy outcomes.
4. **Protection of the Vulnerable**: Impartial application of welfare schemes and protective laws prevents capture by powerful interests.
5. **Trust in Institutions**: Repeated demonstration of fairness builds public confidence in the civil service.

## Challenges to Objectivity and Impartiality

- **Political Pressure**: Elected representatives or powerful interests may seek favourable decisions.
- **Personal Bias**: Unconscious prejudices based on social background, education, or past experience.
- **Corruption and Nepotism**: Direct financial or familial incentives to deviate from rules.
- **Administrative Culture**: Organisational norms that reward loyalty over merit or that discourage dissent.
- **Information Asymmetry and Complexity**: Incomplete data or technical complexity can make purely objective assessment difficult.
- **Media and Public Opinion**: High-visibility cases may create pressure to act in a particular way regardless of merits.

## Institutional Safeguards

- Clear rules, manuals, and standard operating procedures that limit discretion or structure it.
- Written reasons for decisions, especially in quasi-judicial or high-stakes matters.
- Hierarchical supervision and internal audit.
- External oversight: judiciary, Lokpal, Central Vigilance Commission, audit institutions, Right to Information.
- Transfer and posting policies that reduce prolonged local entrenchment.
- Training that emphasises ethical reasoning and case-based discussion of dilemmas.
- Code of Conduct rules that explicitly require political neutrality and prohibit certain forms of influence.

## Practical Application in Decision-Making

When faced with a decision, an objective and impartial approach involves:

1. Clarifying the legal and policy framework that governs the matter.
2. Gathering relevant facts from reliable sources.
3. Identifying the stakeholders and the public interest involved.
4. Examining alternatives and their consequences.
5. Applying the rules or criteria consistently.
6. Recording the reasons.
7. Remaining open to review or appeal.

In situations where the law itself confers wide discretion, the civil servant must still articulate the principles on which discretion is exercised so that the decision is not arbitrary.

## Relationship with Other Values

Objectivity and impartiality support and are supported by other foundational values:

- **Integrity**: Resistance to corruption makes impartiality possible.
- **Honesty**: Truthfulness in reporting facts underpins objectivity.
- **Empathy and Compassion**: These must operate within the framework of equal treatment rather than selective favour.
- **Courage of Conviction**: Sometimes required to resist pressure and uphold an objective decision.

## Illustrative Ethical Tensions

- A civil servant is asked to prioritise a project in a politically sensitive constituency. Objectivity requires evaluation on developmental criteria; impartiality requires that similar projects elsewhere receive comparable consideration.
- In recruitment or promotion, pressure to favour a particular candidate. Impartiality demands adherence to merit and prescribed procedures.
- In law-and-order situations involving different communities, the response must be based on facts and law rather than on majoritarian or minority sentiment.

## Conclusion

Objectivity and impartiality are not passive qualities; they require constant vigilance, procedural discipline, and moral courage. They form the bedrock of a professional civil service that can serve a diverse democracy with fairness and credibility. Cultivating these values is both an individual ethical responsibility and an institutional necessity.
