# Ordinance-Making Power and Related Emergency Provisions

## Concept of Ordinance

An ordinance is a temporary law promulgated by the President (at the Union level) or the Governor (at the State level) when the legislature is not in session. It has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament or the State Legislature, but it is provisional in nature.

The ordinance-making power is a legislative power vested in the executive to meet urgent situations that cannot wait for the legislature to reassemble.

### Constitutional Basis

- Article 123: Power of the President to promulgate ordinances during recess of Parliament.
- Article 213: Power of the Governor to promulgate ordinances during recess of the State Legislature.

Both provisions are almost identical in structure and purpose.

## Conditions for Promulgation

1. Both Houses of Parliament (or the State Legislature) must not be in session.
2. The President (or Governor) must be satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action.
3. The ordinance must be within the legislative competence of Parliament (or the State Legislature). It cannot deal with matters outside the Union List or Concurrent List (for Union) or State List (for State).

The "satisfaction" of the President is not justiciable in ordinary circumstances, but courts can examine whether the power was exercised mala fide or for a collateral purpose.

## Duration and Parliamentary Control

An ordinance remains in force for a maximum of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament (or the State Legislature). It must be laid before both Houses.

- If both Houses approve it, it becomes an Act.
- If either House disapproves it, it ceases to operate.
- If no action is taken, it automatically lapses after six weeks of reassembly.
- The President may withdraw an ordinance at any time.

If the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six-week period is calculated from the later date of reassembly.

An ordinance can be re-promulgated, but repeated re-promulgation without placing it before the legislature has been held unconstitutional by the Supreme Court (as it subverts the legislative process).

## Key Judicial Pronouncements

- **R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970)**: The President's satisfaction is not beyond judicial review if it is shown to be based on extraneous considerations.
- **A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982)**: Ordinance-making power is co-extensive with the legislative power of Parliament. An ordinance can create offences and provide for punishments, subject to constitutional limitations.
- **D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987)**: Repeated re-promulgation of ordinances without legislative approval is a fraud on the Constitution.
- **Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017)**: A seven-judge bench held that re-promulgation is constitutionally impermissible. An ordinance is temporary and must be placed before the legislature. The Court also clarified that actions taken under a lapsed ordinance do not automatically survive unless validated by subsequent legislation.

## Differences from Ordinary Legislation

- Ordinance is executive legislation; Act is parliamentary legislation.
- Ordinance requires subsequent parliamentary approval to continue permanently.
- Ordinance cannot amend the Constitution (Article 368 procedure is exclusive to Parliament).
- Ordinance cannot abridge Fundamental Rights any more than an Act can.

## Practical Significance

Ordinances are used for urgent economic measures, security-related issues, or administrative necessities. Overuse or misuse attracts criticism because it bypasses the deliberative process of the legislature. The constitutional scheme balances the need for speed with the principle of legislative supremacy by requiring subsequent parliamentary scrutiny.

## Related Emergency Provisions (Brief Conceptual Link)

While ordinance-making is a routine legislative power of the executive during recess, the Constitution also provides for extraordinary situations under Part XVIII (Emergency Provisions).

### National Emergency (Article 352)

Proclaimed by the President when the security of India or any part is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. Effects include:

- Extension of Union executive and legislative power over State subjects.
- Suspension of certain Fundamental Rights (Articles 19 can be suspended; Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even during emergency after the 44th Amendment).
- Parliament can extend the life of the Lok Sabha by one year at a time.

### President's Rule (Article 356)

Imposed when the constitutional machinery in a State fails. The President assumes the functions of the State Government. The State Legislature may be dissolved or kept in suspended animation. Subject to parliamentary approval and judicial review after S.R. Bommai (1994).

### Financial Emergency (Article 360)

Proclaimed when the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. The President can direct States to observe financial propriety and reduce salaries of government servants, including judges.

Ordinance-making power continues to operate even during emergencies, subject to the same constitutional limitations.

## Key Points for Conceptual Clarity

- Ordinance is temporary legislation by the executive.
- It is co-extensive with the power of the legislature but not a substitute for it.
- Judicial review exists against mala fide or colourable exercise.
- Re-promulgation without legislative approval is unconstitutional.
- Fundamental Rights limitations apply equally to ordinances and Acts.
- Emergency provisions are distinct extraordinary powers and should not be confused with ordinary ordinance-making.

This framework ensures that the executive can act swiftly when necessary while remaining accountable to the elected legislature and the Constitution.
