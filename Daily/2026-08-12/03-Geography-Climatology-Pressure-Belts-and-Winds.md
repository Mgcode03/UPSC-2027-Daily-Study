# Climatology: Global Pressure Belts and Wind Systems

## Fundamental Concepts

Atmospheric pressure is the force exerted by the weight of the air column above a given point. It decreases with altitude. At sea level, the average pressure is approximately 1013.25 millibars (mb) or 1013.25 hectopascals (hPa).

Pressure differences drive the movement of air, creating winds. Understanding the global distribution of pressure and the resulting wind systems is foundational to climatology and to explaining India's monsoon system.

## Idealised Global Pressure Belts

On a non-rotating, uniform Earth, a simple thermal circulation would exist. In reality, the combination of differential heating, Earth's rotation (Coriolis force), and the distribution of land and water produces a more complex pattern of pressure belts.

### 1. Equatorial Low-Pressure Belt (Doldrums)

- Located roughly between 5°N and 5°S.
- Caused by intense solar heating leading to rising air (convection).
- Characterised by calm or light variable winds – historically called the Doldrums by sailors.
- Associated with heavy convectional rainfall and the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ).

### 2. Subtropical High-Pressure Belts (Horse Latitudes)

- Located around 30°–35° North and South latitudes.
- Formed by the descent of air that rose at the equator and moved poleward in the upper troposphere.
- Descending air is compressed and warmed, creating dry, stable conditions – many of the world's major deserts lie under these belts.
- Named Horse Latitudes because sailing ships were often becalmed and horses were thrown overboard to lighten the load.

### 3. Subpolar Low-Pressure Belts

- Located around 60°–65° North and South latitudes.
- Result from the meeting of cold polar air and warmer mid-latitude air, creating a zone of rising air and frequent cyclonic activity.
- More continuous in the Southern Hemisphere (over oceans) than in the Northern Hemisphere (disrupted by continents).

### 4. Polar High-Pressure Belts

- Located over the poles.
- Caused by extreme cold leading to dense, sinking air.
- Winds blow outward from the poles.

These belts shift seasonally with the apparent movement of the Sun. In the Northern Hemisphere summer they move northward; in winter they move southward. This seasonal shift is crucial for the Indian monsoon.

## Planetary Wind Systems

Winds are named according to the direction from which they blow. The major planetary winds are:

### Trade Winds

- Blow from the subtropical highs towards the equatorial low.
- In the Northern Hemisphere: North-East Trades.
- In the Southern Hemisphere: South-East Trades.
- Steady, reliable winds that played a major role in the age of sail.
- Converge near the equator, feeding the ITCZ.

### Westerlies

- Blow from the subtropical highs towards the subpolar lows.
- In the Northern Hemisphere: South-Westerlies.
- In the Southern Hemisphere: North-Westerlies.
- Stronger and more consistent in the Southern Hemisphere (Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties, Screaming Sixties) because of the uninterrupted oceanic expanse.

### Polar Easterlies

- Blow from the polar highs towards the subpolar lows.
- Cold, dry winds.
- Meet the westerlies at the polar front, generating mid-latitude cyclones.

## Coriolis Force and Deflection

The Coriolis force, arising from Earth's rotation, deflects moving air:

- To the right in the Northern Hemisphere.
- To the left in the Southern Hemisphere.

This deflection transforms simple north-south pressure-gradient winds into the diagonal trade winds and westerlies. The magnitude of Coriolis force increases with latitude and with wind speed; it is zero at the equator.

## Local and Seasonal Wind Systems of Relevance to India

### Monsoon Winds

The Indian monsoon is a large-scale seasonal reversal of winds driven primarily by differential heating of land and sea, modulated by the seasonal shift of pressure belts and the Himalayan barrier.

- Summer (Southwest Monsoon): Intense heating of the Indian landmass creates a low-pressure area. Moisture-laden winds from the Indian Ocean blow towards this low, bringing rainfall.
- Winter (Northeast Monsoon): High pressure over the cooled landmass; winds blow from land to sea. The northeast monsoon brings rainfall mainly to the southeastern coast (Tamil Nadu).

### Western Disturbances

Extratropical cyclonic systems originating over the Mediterranean that travel eastward and bring winter rainfall to northwestern India, crucial for rabi crops.

### Jet Streams

High-altitude, fast-moving westerly winds. The subtropical westerly jet and the tropical easterly jet influence the onset and withdrawal of the Indian monsoon. The shifting of the subtropical jet north of the Himalayas is associated with the onset of the southwest monsoon.

## Vertical Circulation and Cells

The global circulation can be conceptualised in three cells in each hemisphere:

1. **Hadley Cell**: Equator to ~30° – rising at equator, poleward upper flow, descending at subtropics, return flow as trades.
2. **Ferrel Cell**: ~30° to ~60° – indirect cell driven by the other two; surface westerlies.
3. **Polar Cell**: Poles to ~60° – sinking at poles, equatorward surface flow as polar easterlies, rising at subpolar lows.

These cells are idealisations; real atmosphere is more complex due to land-sea contrasts, topography, and seasonal changes.

## Key Points for Memorisation and Understanding

- Pressure belts: Equatorial Low, Subtropical Highs, Subpolar Lows, Polar Highs.
- Planetary winds: Trades, Westerlies, Polar Easterlies.
- Coriolis force causes deflection and shapes wind direction.
- Seasonal shift of belts is fundamental to monsoon dynamics.
- India's climate is dominated by the monsoon system, which is a modified form of the planetary circulation influenced by the Asian landmass and the Himalayas.

A clear grasp of these pressure and wind systems provides the foundation for understanding weather patterns, climate zones, and the specific climatic features of the Indian subcontinent.
