# Poverty and Poverty Alleviation – Conceptual Understanding

## Defining Poverty

Poverty is a multidimensional condition characterised by the lack of resources, capabilities, and opportunities necessary for a minimally acceptable standard of living. It is not merely low income; it encompasses deprivation in health, education, housing, sanitation, social participation, and security.

### Absolute and Relative Poverty

- **Absolute Poverty**: Inability to meet basic minimum requirements of food, clothing, and shelter. Measured against a fixed poverty line based on calorie intake or a basket of essential goods.
- **Relative Poverty**: Deprivation relative to the average standard of living in a society. Even if absolute needs are met, a person may be considered poor if significantly below the societal average.

### Multidimensional Poverty

The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) captures deprivations across health, education, and living standards. A household is multidimensionally poor if it is deprived in a certain proportion of weighted indicators. This approach recognises that income alone does not capture the full experience of poverty.

## Measurement of Poverty in India

India has a long history of official poverty estimation.

- Early estimates focused on calorie norms (e.g., Alagh Committee, Lakdawala Committee).
- Tendulkar Committee (2009) shifted towards a broader consumption basket and used Mixed Reference Period.
- Rangarajan Committee (2014) suggested a higher poverty line and a different methodology.
- Official estimates have used the Tendulkar methodology for a period; more recent discussions incorporate multidimensional approaches and the Socio-Economic and Caste Census data.

Poverty ratios have declined significantly since the 1990s, yet the absolute number of poor remains large, and vulnerability is high for many households just above the poverty line.

## Causes and Dimensions of Poverty

### Structural Causes

- Historical: Colonial economic policies that deindustrialised and extracted surplus.
- Agrarian structure: Unequal land distribution, low productivity in large parts of agriculture, dependence on monsoon.
- Demographic: High population growth in earlier decades increased pressure on resources and employment.
- Educational and skill deficits limiting access to higher-productivity jobs.
- Social exclusion based on caste, tribe, gender, and region.

### Contemporary Factors

- Informal nature of the majority of employment – low wages, no social security, vulnerability to shocks.
- Regional disparities in development.
- Health shocks that push households into poverty.
- Climate-related risks affecting rural livelihoods.

## Poverty Alleviation Strategies – Conceptual Evolution

### Growth-Mediated Strategy

The idea that rapid economic growth will trickle down and reduce poverty. Growth generates employment and raises incomes. However, the pattern of growth matters: if growth is concentrated in capital-intensive or skill-intensive sectors, its poverty-reducing impact is weaker.

### Direct Attack on Poverty

Recognition that growth alone is insufficient. This led to targeted programmes:

- Wage employment programmes (e.g., earlier Jawahar Rozgar Yojana, later MGNREGA).
- Self-employment and asset-creation programmes (Integrated Rural Development Programme, later National Rural Livelihoods Mission).
- Food security and nutrition (Public Distribution System, Integrated Child Development Services, mid-day meals).
- Housing, sanitation, and basic services.

### Rights-Based Approach

Shift from welfare as a discretionary grant to entitlements backed by law:

- Right to Information.
- Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (legal guarantee of 100 days of wage employment).
- National Food Security Act.
- Right to Education.
- Forest Rights Act.

This approach strengthens the bargaining position of the poor and creates accountability mechanisms.

### Capability Approach (Amartya Sen)

Poverty is understood as deprivation of capabilities – the real freedoms people have to live the kind of life they value. Income is instrumental; the ends are health, education, political voice, and social participation. Policy should expand these capabilities.

## Key Programmatic Pillars in India

- **Employment Guarantee**: MGNREGA as a demand-driven safety net and asset-creating programme.
- **Livelihoods Mission**: Promoting self-help groups, financial inclusion, and sustainable livelihoods, especially for women.
- **Financial Inclusion**: Bank accounts, direct benefit transfer, insurance and pension schemes to reduce leakage and improve targeting.
- **Human Development**: Investments in health, nutrition, education, and skill development.
- **Social Protection**: Pensions, disability support, and other transfers for the most vulnerable.

## Challenges in Poverty Alleviation

- Identification and targeting: Inclusion and exclusion errors.
- Leakages and corruption in delivery systems (partially addressed by technology and DBT).
- Quality of employment: Moving from precarious informal work to more stable and productive livelihoods.
- Last-mile delivery and administrative capacity.
- Inter-generational transmission of poverty through poor nutrition and education.
- Climate vulnerability and agrarian distress in certain regions.

## Conceptual Linkages

Poverty is linked to inequality, unemployment, health, education, gender, caste, and regional development. Effective poverty alleviation therefore requires a multi-pronged approach combining growth with equity, rights with delivery capacity, and universal provisions with targeted support for the most disadvantaged.

Understanding poverty as both a lack of income and a lack of capabilities provides a richer framework for analysing policy design and outcomes.
