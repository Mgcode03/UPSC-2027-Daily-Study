# Urbanisation in India: Trends, Issues, and Policy Responses

## Meaning and Measurement

Urbanisation refers to the increasing share of the population living in urban areas and the associated processes of social, economic, and spatial change. In India, an urban area is defined by criteria that include population size, density, and the proportion of male workers in non-agricultural occupations (Census definition), along with statutory towns notified by state governments.

India’s urban population has grown steadily in absolute numbers. The level of urbanisation (percentage of population in urban areas) has also risen, though it remains lower than in many countries at comparable levels of industrialisation. Growth occurs both through natural increase within urban areas and through rural-to-urban migration, as well as through the reclassification of rural areas as urban.

## Drivers of Urbanisation

- Economic opportunities in industry, services, and informal sector activities concentrated in cities.
- Relatively higher wages and perceived better access to education, healthcare, and amenities.
- Distress migration from rural areas affected by agrarian stagnation, landlessness, or climate-related stress.
- Expansion of transport and communication networks that link rural hinterlands to urban centres.

## Spatial Pattern

Urban growth in India is characterised by:

- Concentration in large metropolitan cities and their peripheries (urban agglomerations).
- Emergence of census towns (settlements that meet urban criteria but are not statutory towns).
- Growth of smaller towns, though many intermediate towns have grown more slowly.
- Regional variations: higher urbanisation in western and southern states compared with many eastern and central states.

The concentration of growth in a few large cities creates both dynamism and severe pressure on infrastructure and environment.

## Major Issues Associated with Urbanisation

### Housing and Slums

A significant proportion of the urban population lives in informal settlements or slums characterised by insecure tenure, inadequate housing, and poor access to basic services. The demand for affordable housing far outstrips formal supply.

### Infrastructure and Services

Water supply, sanitation, solid waste management, drainage, electricity, and public transport often lag behind population growth. Congestion, pollution, and inadequate public transport reduce the quality of life and economic efficiency.

### Environmental Stress

Air and water pollution, loss of water bodies and green spaces, heat island effects, and vulnerability to flooding (especially in low-lying and poorly drained areas) are widespread problems.

### Informal Employment

A large share of the urban workforce is in the informal sector – low productivity, low social security, and high vulnerability to economic shocks.

### Social Issues

Inequality within cities is stark. Issues of safety (especially for women), social segregation, and the integration of migrant populations require continuous attention.

### Governance Challenges

Urban local bodies often lack adequate financial resources, technical capacity, and functional autonomy. Overlapping jurisdictions among multiple agencies complicate planning and service delivery. The 74th Constitutional Amendment aimed to strengthen urban local self-government, but implementation remains uneven.

## Policy and Institutional Responses

### Constitutional and Legal Framework

The 74th Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to municipalities, provided for the devolution of functions, and mandated the creation of Ward Committees, District Planning Committees, and Metropolitan Planning Committees. State Finance Commissions were to recommend resource sharing.

### Major Programmes and Missions

- **Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (earlier)** and subsequent missions.
- **Smart Cities Mission**: Focus on area-based development and smart solutions in selected cities.
- **Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT)**: Water supply, sewerage, and green spaces in selected cities.
- **Housing for All / Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban)**: Aiming to address the housing shortage for economically weaker sections and low-income groups.
- **Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban)**: Sanitation and solid waste management.
- **National Urban Transport Policy** and related initiatives for sustainable mobility.

### Planning Approaches

Master plans, development plans, and increasingly, attempts at integrated metropolitan planning. The challenge is to make planning more participatory, realistic, and enforceable, and to link spatial planning with economic and social planning.

### Financing Urban Development

Municipal revenues (property tax, user charges) are often inadequate. Dependence on state and central transfers is high. Innovations include municipal bonds, public-private partnerships, and land-value capture mechanisms, though these remain limited in scale.

## Conceptual Linkages

Urbanisation is both a consequence and a driver of economic development. Managed well, cities can be engines of growth, innovation, and social mobility. Managed poorly, they become sites of congestion, inequality, and environmental degradation. The quality of urban governance, the inclusiveness of growth, and the sustainability of infrastructure and environmental management determine which trajectory predominates.

Understanding the trends, the structural issues, and the evolving policy responses is essential for analysing one of the most significant social and economic transformations underway in India.
