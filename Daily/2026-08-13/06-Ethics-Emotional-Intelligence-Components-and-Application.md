# Emotional Intelligence: Components and Application in Public Service

## Concept of Emotional Intelligence

Emotional Intelligence (EI) refers to the ability to recognise, understand, manage, and use emotions effectively in oneself and in relation to others. It complements cognitive intelligence (IQ) and is particularly relevant in roles that involve human interaction, leadership, conflict resolution, and decision-making under pressure – all central to public administration.

The concept was popularised by psychologists such as Peter Salovey, John Mayer, and later Daniel Goleman, who brought it into wider organisational and leadership discourse.

## Major Components of Emotional Intelligence

While models vary slightly, the following components are widely recognised:

### 1. Self-Awareness

The ability to recognise one’s own emotions, strengths, weaknesses, values, and motives as they occur. A self-aware person understands how emotions influence thoughts and behaviour and has a realistic self-assessment.

In administration: Knowing when stress, anger, or bias is affecting judgement; recognising personal triggers in high-pressure situations.

### 2. Self-Regulation (or Self-Management)

The ability to manage disruptive emotions and impulses, maintain standards of honesty and integrity, take responsibility, adapt to change, and maintain composure under stress.

In administration: Remaining calm during crises, resisting the urge to react impulsively to provocation or pressure, and consistently adhering to ethical standards even when unsupervised.

### 3. Motivation (Internal)

A passion for work that goes beyond external rewards; drive to achieve, optimism even in the face of setbacks, and organisational commitment.

In administration: Persistence in implementing difficult reforms, maintaining energy and purpose despite bureaucratic obstacles or political changes.

### 4. Empathy

The ability to understand the emotional makeup of other people and to treat them according to their emotional reactions. It includes sensing others’ feelings and perspectives and taking an active interest in their concerns.

In administration: Understanding the impact of policies on different sections of society, especially the vulnerable; listening to grievances with genuine attention; reading the mood of a crowd or a negotiating counterpart.

### 5. Social Skills

Proficiency in managing relationships, building networks, finding common ground, and building rapport. It includes communication, conflict management, leadership, and collaboration.

In administration: Coordinating across departments, negotiating with stakeholders, leading teams, and communicating decisions effectively to the public and to political superiors.

## Relevance to Civil Services

Civil servants operate at the interface of the state and citizens, and between political executives and the permanent administration. Emotional intelligence contributes to:

- Better decision-making by reducing the distorting effect of unmanaged emotions.
- Improved citizen-centric administration through empathy and effective communication.
- Conflict resolution and consensus-building in diverse and often polarised environments.
- Leadership of teams and motivation of subordinates.
- Personal resilience and prevention of burnout in high-stress roles.
- Ethical conduct: self-regulation supports integrity; empathy supports fairness and compassion.

## Application in Specific Situations

### Handling Public Grievances

Empathy helps in listening without defensiveness; self-regulation prevents irritation from turning into rudeness; social skills help in explaining constraints and possible solutions.

### Crisis Management

Self-awareness and self-regulation enable clear thinking under pressure; social skills facilitate coordination among agencies; motivation sustains effort over prolonged emergencies.

### Working with Political Executives

Emotional intelligence helps in understanding the political context without becoming partisan, in communicating advice constructively, and in managing disagreement without damaging working relationships.

### Team Leadership

Recognising the emotions and motivations of team members, providing constructive feedback, resolving interpersonal conflicts, and creating a positive work climate all depend on EI competencies.

### Ethical Dilemmas

Self-awareness helps identify when personal interest or emotion is clouding judgement; empathy expands the consideration of those affected by a decision; self-regulation supports adherence to principle under pressure.

## Developing Emotional Intelligence

Unlike IQ, which is relatively stable, EI can be developed through:

- Reflective practice and feedback.
- Mindfulness and stress-management techniques.
- Seeking diverse experiences and perspectives.
- Mentoring and coaching.
- Deliberate practice of active listening and perspective-taking.

Training programmes for civil servants increasingly incorporate elements of emotional intelligence, ethics, and leadership.

## Relationship with Other Ethical Concepts

Emotional intelligence supports but does not replace other foundational values. Integrity, objectivity, and impartiality remain essential. EI provides the interpersonal and intrapersonal skills that make the consistent practice of those values more effective in real-world settings.

A civil servant with high cognitive ability but low emotional intelligence may struggle with leadership, citizen interaction, and stress. Conversely, emotional intelligence without a strong ethical compass can be used manipulatively. The ideal is the integration of intellectual competence, ethical commitment, and emotional intelligence.

## Key Points

- Emotional intelligence comprises self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills.
- It is highly relevant to the relational and decision-making demands of public service.
- It can be consciously developed.
- It enhances the practical application of ethical values in complex administrative environments.

Understanding and cultivating emotional intelligence is an important dimension of preparing for the responsibilities of civil service.
