# Climate Change: Science, Impacts, and India’s Context

## The Scientific Basis

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperature, precipitation, and other atmospheric conditions, primarily driven in the current era by anthropogenic emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs).

### Greenhouse Effect

Certain gases in the atmosphere (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone, and halocarbons) trap outgoing long-wave radiation, warming the Earth’s surface. This natural greenhouse effect makes the planet habitable. The enhanced greenhouse effect due to increased concentrations of GHGs from human activities is the principal cause of observed global warming since the mid-20th century.

### Key Greenhouse Gases

- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**: From burning of fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial processes. Long-lived in the atmosphere.
- **Methane (CH₄)**: From agriculture (livestock, rice paddies), fossil fuel extraction, and waste. More potent but shorter-lived than CO₂.
- **Nitrous oxide (N₂O)**: From agriculture (fertilisers) and industrial activities.
- **Fluorinated gases**: Industrial gases with very high global warming potentials.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) provides comprehensive assessments of the science, impacts, and mitigation/adaptation options. The scientific consensus is that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, and that continued emissions will cause further warming and long-lasting changes.

## Observed and Projected Impacts (Global)

- Rising global mean temperature.
- Melting of glaciers and ice sheets; rising sea levels.
- Changes in precipitation patterns – more intense rainfall in some regions, drought in others.
- Increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events (heatwaves, heavy precipitation, certain types of storms).
- Ocean acidification due to absorption of CO₂.
- Risks to ecosystems, biodiversity, food security, water resources, and human health.

## Impacts on India

India is highly vulnerable to climate change because of its dependence on the monsoon, long coastline, Himalayan glacier systems, large agrarian population, and significant numbers living in poverty.

### Key Vulnerabilities

- **Monsoon variability**: Changes in the timing, intensity, and distribution of monsoon rainfall affect agriculture and water availability.
- **Himalayan glaciers**: Retreat affects river flows in the northern river systems, with implications for irrigation, hydropower, and water supply.
- **Sea-level rise and cyclones**: Threat to coastal cities, agriculture in deltaic regions, and island territories. Increased intensity of cyclones in the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea.
- **Heat stress**: Rising temperatures increase heat-related mortality and reduce labour productivity, especially in outdoor work.
- **Agriculture**: Yields of major crops can be affected by temperature rise, changes in rainfall, and increased pests and diseases. Rainfed agriculture is particularly vulnerable.
- **Water resources**: Altered hydrological cycles, increased evaporation, and stress on groundwater.
- **Health**: Expanded range of vector-borne diseases, heat-related illness, and malnutrition risks linked to food insecurity.
- **Biodiversity and forests**: Shifts in species ranges, forest fires, and ecosystem stress.

## India’s Emissions Profile and Development Context

India is the third-largest emitter of GHGs in absolute terms, but per capita emissions remain far below the global average and those of developed countries. A large part of the population still lacks access to modern energy and adequate living standards. India’s climate policy therefore seeks to balance the imperatives of development, energy access, and poverty eradication with the need to contribute to global mitigation efforts.

## India’s Policy Response – Conceptual Outline

- **National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC)**: Eight missions covering solar energy, enhanced energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, sustaining the Himalayan ecosystem, Green India, sustainable agriculture, and strategic knowledge.
- **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)** under the Paris Agreement: Commitments on reducing emissions intensity of GDP, increasing non-fossil fuel based electricity capacity, and creating additional carbon sinks through forests and trees.
- **Adaptation measures**: Climate-resilient agriculture, water conservation, disaster risk reduction, coastal zone management, and livelihood diversification.
- **International engagement**: Participation in the UNFCCC process, advocacy of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities, and cooperation on technology and finance.

## Mitigation and Adaptation

- **Mitigation**: Reducing emissions through renewable energy expansion, energy efficiency, electrification of transport, industrial process improvements, and forest conservation/afforestation.
- **Adaptation**: Adjusting systems and practices to actual or expected climate impacts – climate-smart agriculture, early warning systems, resilient infrastructure, and social protection.

Both are necessary. Mitigation addresses the root cause; adaptation addresses the unavoidable impacts.

## Key Conceptual Takeaways

- Climate change is a scientifically established phenomenon driven primarily by anthropogenic GHG emissions.
- Impacts are uneven; developing countries and vulnerable communities within them face disproportionate risks.
- India’s approach integrates development goals with climate action, emphasising equity at the global level and co-benefits (energy security, air quality, green jobs) at the domestic level.
- Effective response requires science-based policy, institutional capacity, international cooperation, and behavioural and technological change.

Understanding the science, the nature of impacts on India, and the policy architecture is essential for analysing one of the defining challenges of the contemporary era.
