# Biodiversity Conservation: Concepts, Levels, Threats and Strategies

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life forms at all levels of biological organisation. It encompasses the diversity of genes, species and ecosystems. India is one of the 17 megadiverse countries of the world and contains four of the 36 global biodiversity hotspots.

## Levels of Biodiversity

1. **Genetic Diversity**  
   Variation of genes within a species. Example: different varieties of rice or mango in India. High genetic diversity enables a species to adapt to changing environments and resist diseases.

2. **Species Diversity**  
   Variety of species within a region or community. Measured by species richness (number of species) and species evenness (relative abundance).

3. **Ecosystem or Community Diversity**  
   Variety of ecosystems within a given area — forests, grasslands, wetlands, deserts, marine ecosystems, etc.

## Biodiversity Hotspots

To qualify as a hotspot, a region must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and must have lost at least 70 percent of its original primary vegetation. The four hotspots that fall wholly or partly in India are:

- The Himalaya
- The Western Ghats (and Sri Lanka)
- The Indo-Burma region
- Sundaland (includes the Nicobar Islands)

## Threats to Biodiversity (The Evil Quartet and Beyond)

1. **Habitat Loss and Fragmentation**  
   The most important cause. Conversion of forests to agriculture, urbanisation, mining, and infrastructure development.

2. **Over-exploitation**  
   Over-hunting, over-fishing, excessive collection of medicinal plants and timber.

3. **Invasive Alien Species**  
   Introduction of non-native species that outcompete native ones (example: Lantana, Parthenium, water hyacinth, African catfish).

4. **Co-extinctions**  
   When a species becomes extinct, the species that depend on it (parasites, mutualists, specialised predators) also face extinction.

Additional modern threats include climate change, pollution (especially plastic and chemical), and emerging diseases.

## Conservation Strategies

### In-situ Conservation (Conservation in Natural Habitat)

1. **Protected Areas**  
   - National Parks: Strictly protected; no human activity allowed except tourism and research in designated zones.  
   - Wildlife Sanctuaries: Protection of particular wild animal species; limited human activity permitted.  
   - Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves: Buffer zones and community-managed areas under the Wildlife (Protection) Amendment Act.

2. **Biosphere Reserves**  
   Large areas of unique ecosystems recognised under UNESCO’s Man and Biosphere Programme. They contain core, buffer and transition zones. India has 18 biosphere reserves, several of which are part of the World Network of Biosphere Reserves.

3. **Sacred Groves**  
   Forest patches protected by local communities on religious grounds. Found across India, especially in the Western Ghats, Northeast, Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. They preserve many rare and endemic species.

### Ex-situ Conservation (Conservation Outside Natural Habitat)

1. **Botanical Gardens and Zoological Parks**  
   Maintain living collections of plants and animals.

2. **Seed Banks, Gene Banks and Cryopreservation**  
   Storage of seeds, sperm, eggs or tissue at very low temperatures. The National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources and the Svalbard Global Seed Vault are important examples.

3. **Captive Breeding and Reintroduction Programmes**  
   Breeding of endangered species in controlled environments followed by release into the wild (example: project for the conservation of the Asiatic lion, snow leopard, and various vulture species).

## Important Indian Conservation Initiatives and Legal Framework

- Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (amended multiple times): Provides the legal framework for protected areas and schedules of protected species.
- Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980: Restricts diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes.
- Biological Diversity Act, 2002: Aims at conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the use of biological resources. Establishes the National Biodiversity Authority, State Biodiversity Boards and Biodiversity Management Committees.
- Project Tiger (1973), Project Elephant, Project Snow Leopard, and other species-specific programmes.
- National Wildlife Action Plan and National Biodiversity Action Plan.

## International Frameworks

- Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992: Three objectives — conservation, sustainable use, and equitable benefit sharing. India is a party.
- Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).
- Ramsar Convention on Wetlands.
- World Heritage Convention (natural sites).
- Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety and Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing (both under CBD).

## Key Points for Retention

- Biodiversity has three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem.
- India has four biodiversity hotspots: Himalaya, Western Ghats, Indo-Burma, Sundaland.
- Habitat loss is the single largest threat to biodiversity.
- In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitats; ex-situ conservation does so outside.
- The Biological Diversity Act, 2002 operationalises India’s commitments under the CBD.
- Sacred groves represent a traditional, community-based form of in-situ conservation.
