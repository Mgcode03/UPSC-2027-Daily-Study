# Role of Women and Women’s Organisations in Indian Society

The position of women in Indian society has been shaped by a complex interplay of historical traditions, social structures, economic conditions, legal reforms and political mobilisation. Understanding the evolving role of women and the contribution of women’s organisations is essential for analysing social change, development and justice in India.

## Historical Context

In the ancient period, texts and evidence suggest a relatively higher status for women in certain spheres (education, participation in assemblies in early Vedic times), followed by increasing restrictions in the later Vedic and classical periods. Medieval society further constrained women’s public roles in many regions, although there were notable exceptions of women rulers, poets and saints.

The nineteenth-century social reform movements marked a turning point. Reformers addressed issues such as sati, child marriage, widow remarriage, women’s education and the purdah system. The involvement of women in the freedom struggle — from the Swadeshi movement to the Quit India movement — expanded their public presence and political consciousness.

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

The Constitution of India guarantees equality before the law (Article 14), prohibits discrimination on grounds of sex (Article 15), and allows special provisions for women and children (Article 15(3)). Directive Principles call for equal pay for equal work and just and humane conditions of work. Fundamental Duties include the duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.

Important legislative measures include:

- Hindu Marriage Act, Hindu Succession Act (as amended), Dowry Prohibition Act, Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act.
- Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005.
- Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.
- Criminal Law Amendments strengthening laws against rape and sexual assault.
- Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act and other labour welfare provisions.
- Reservation of seats for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (73rd Amendment) and urban local bodies (74th Amendment). The proposed Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Eighth Amendment) Bill seeks reservation in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies.

## Changing Role of Women

### Economic Sphere
Women’s participation in the labour force has shown complex trends. While work participation rates remain lower than those of men, women constitute a large share of the agricultural workforce and are increasingly visible in services, education, health, information technology and entrepreneurship. The rise of self-help groups (SHGs) has enhanced financial inclusion and collective bargaining power for millions of women, particularly in rural areas.

### Political Sphere
The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments have brought more than a million women into local governance as elected representatives. Women’s representation in Parliament and state legislatures has increased gradually but remains below the desired level. Women have held high constitutional offices including President, Prime Minister, Speaker of the Lok Sabha, and Chief Ministers of several states.

### Social and Cultural Sphere
Literacy rates among women have risen steadily. Age at marriage has increased. Total fertility rate has declined. Yet challenges persist: gender-based violence, skewed sex ratio in some regions, unequal property rights in practice, unpaid care work, and social norms that restrict mobility and decision-making.

## Women’s Organisations and Movements

Women’s organisations in India have operated at multiple levels — local, regional and national — and have addressed a wide spectrum of issues.

### Early Phase
Organisations such as the All India Women’s Conference (founded 1927), the National Council of Women in India, and various regional associations focused on education, social reform, and legal rights during the colonial period.

### Post-Independence Phase
After 1947, women’s organisations continued to work on education, health, legal literacy and welfare. The 1970s and 1980s saw the rise of an autonomous women’s movement that highlighted issues of violence, dowry deaths, rape, and the gender-blind nature of development policies. Landmark campaigns included the anti-dowry movement, the campaign for reform of rape laws after the Mathura case, and the campaign against sati following the Roop Kanwar incident.

### Contemporary Landscape
Present-day organisations range from large national federations and NGOs to grassroots groups and self-help group federations. They work on issues including:

- Violence against women and access to justice.
- Political participation and leadership training.
- Livelihoods, skill development and financial inclusion.
- Health, nutrition and reproductive rights.
- Education and digital literacy.
- Environmental justice and climate resilience from a gender perspective.
- Advocacy for legal and policy reforms.

Self-help groups, promoted under programmes such as the National Rural Livelihoods Mission, have become one of the largest institutional platforms for women’s collective action in the world. They combine savings, credit, livelihood support and social empowerment.

## Challenges and Continuing Concerns

- Persistence of patriarchal social norms and gender stereotypes.
- Under-representation in higher decision-making positions in politics, bureaucracy, judiciary and corporate leadership.
- Gender pay gap and concentration of women in informal and low-paid work.
- High burden of unpaid domestic and care work.
- Violence and safety concerns in public and private spaces.
- Intersectionality: the compounded disadvantages faced by women from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, minority communities, and women with disabilities.

## Key Points for Retention

- The Constitution permits special provisions for women under Article 15(3).
- The 73rd and 74th Amendments have transformed women’s participation in local governance.
- Women’s organisations have historically combined social reform, legal advocacy and political mobilisation.
- Self-help groups represent a major contemporary form of women’s collective economic and social empowerment.
- Persistent gaps remain in political representation, economic equality and freedom from violence.
