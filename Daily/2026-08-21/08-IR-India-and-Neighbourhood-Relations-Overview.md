# India and Its Neighbourhood Relations: An Overview

India’s foreign policy has always accorded high priority to relations with its immediate neighbours. The neighbourhood comprises Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. Peace, stability and prosperity in the neighbourhood are regarded as essential for India’s own security and development. The policy approach is often summarised as “Neighbourhood First.”

## Guiding Principles and Approaches

- **Gujral Doctrine** (1990s): India should not ask for reciprocity from smaller neighbours but should give unilaterally what it can in good faith; no country should allow its territory to be used against the interests of another; non-interference in internal affairs; respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty; and peaceful settlement of disputes.
- **Neighbourhood First Policy**: Emphasis on connectivity, commerce, contacts and capacity-building with neighbouring countries.
- **SAGAR** (Security and Growth for All in the Region): Maritime vision focusing on the Indian Ocean region.
- Commitment to a rules-based international order, respect for sovereignty, and mutual benefit.

## Country-wise Overview

### Pakistan
Relations have been marked by conflict, mutual distrust and intermittent dialogue. Core issues include the Kashmir question, cross-border terrorism, and the unresolved territorial disputes. Major wars occurred in 1947–48, 1965, 1971 and the Kargil conflict of 1999. The Simla Agreement (1972) and the Lahore Declaration (1999) represent attempts at peaceful resolution. Terrorism emanating from Pakistani territory remains the principal obstacle to normalisation. Trade and people-to-people contacts exist but remain limited relative to potential.

### China
India and China are two large Asian powers with a long history of civilisational contact and a complex modern relationship. The boundary dispute remains unresolved; the 1962 war left a deep imprint. Confidence-building measures and border management agreements have been signed, yet incidents of friction continue along the Line of Actual Control. Economic engagement has grown substantially, with China becoming one of India’s largest trading partners, although the trade balance is heavily skewed. Cooperation exists in multilateral forums (BRICS, SCO, climate negotiations), while strategic competition is visible in the neighbourhood and the wider Indo-Pacific.

### Bangladesh
Relations have improved significantly since the 1971 Liberation War, in which India played a decisive role. Land boundary issues were settled by the 2015 Land Boundary Agreement. Cooperation covers trade, connectivity, energy, water resources, security and people-to-people ties. Issues that require continued management include water-sharing of common rivers (especially Teesta), border management, and the presence of undocumented migrants. Bangladesh is a key partner in India’s Act East and Neighbourhood First policies.

### Nepal
India and Nepal share a unique relationship characterised by an open border, deep cultural and religious ties, and the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship. India remains Nepal’s largest trade partner and a major source of investment and assistance. Periodic tensions arise over border issues (for example, Kalapani-Limpiyadhura-Lipulekh), political developments in Nepal, and the perception of Indian interference. Connectivity projects, hydropower cooperation and reconstruction assistance after the 2015 earthquake are important elements of engagement.

### Bhutan
Bhutan is India’s closest partner in the neighbourhood. India is Bhutan’s largest trade and development partner and has supported Bhutan’s socio-economic development through successive Five-Year Plan assistance. The relationship is guided by the 2007 Treaty of Friendship (updating the 1949 treaty). Hydropower cooperation is a major pillar; several hydroelectric projects have been developed jointly. Security cooperation is close; India has a strong interest in Bhutan’s stability given the strategic location of the Doklam plateau.

### Sri Lanka
Relations are multi-dimensional, covering political, economic, cultural and defence spheres. India has supported Sri Lanka’s development and was involved in humanitarian assistance during the civil war and subsequent reconstruction. The Indian-origin Tamil population and fisheries issues in the Palk Bay require careful management. China’s growing economic presence in Sri Lanka (including the Hambantota port) has added a strategic dimension. India emphasises respect for the interests of the Tamil population and the unity and territorial integrity of Sri Lanka.

### Maldives
As a maritime neighbour, Maldives is important for India’s Indian Ocean strategy. Relations have witnessed fluctuations with changes of government in Male. India has provided assistance in infrastructure, health, education and maritime security. The presence of extra-regional powers and domestic political shifts influence the trajectory of the relationship. India supports a stable, peaceful and prosperous Maldives and opposes any use of Maldivian territory that could threaten regional security.

### Myanmar
Myanmar is the land bridge to Southeast Asia and a critical partner for India’s Act East Policy. Cooperation covers border management, connectivity (Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project, India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway), energy, and security (including coordination against insurgent groups). Political developments and the humanitarian situation in Myanmar affect the bilateral relationship and regional stability.

### Afghanistan
India has been a major development partner of Afghanistan, investing in infrastructure, education, health and capacity-building. The relationship is based on historical ties and shared interests in a stable, independent and peaceful Afghanistan. The change of regime in 2021 altered the context of engagement; India continues to monitor the situation closely and has provided humanitarian assistance while remaining cautious about formal political recognition.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

- **Connectivity**: Road, rail, inland waterways, ports and digital connectivity are priorities.
- **Economic Integration**: Preferential trade arrangements, power trade, and investment.
- **Security Cooperation**: Counter-terrorism, border management, maritime security and defence dialogues.
- **People-to-People Ties**: Culture, education, tourism and diaspora links.
- **Multilateral Frameworks**: SAARC (currently constrained), BIMSTEC, BBIN, IORA and bilateral mechanisms.

## Key Points for Retention

- Neighbourhood First remains a stated priority of Indian foreign policy.
- The Gujral Doctrine emphasised non-reciprocal generosity towards smaller neighbours.
- Boundary and terrorism issues dominate the relationship with Pakistan; boundary and strategic competition shape relations with China.
- Land Boundary Agreement (2015) was a landmark with Bangladesh.
- Hydropower and development partnership define relations with Bhutan.
- Connectivity projects are central to engagement with Myanmar and the eastern neighbourhood.
