# Parliamentary System of Government in India

The Constitution of India establishes a parliamentary system of government both at the Union and the State levels. This system is modelled primarily on the British Westminster model, with important adaptations suited to Indian conditions and the republican character of the Indian polity.

## Meaning and Essence

In a parliamentary system the executive is drawn from and remains responsible to the legislature. The head of government (Prime Minister at the Centre, Chief Minister in the states) and the council of ministers must enjoy the confidence of the popularly elected house. If that confidence is lost, the government must resign or seek a fresh mandate.

The President of India is the constitutional head of the state, while the real executive power is exercised by the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. This dual executive is a defining feature of the parliamentary form.

## Key Features of the Indian Parliamentary System

1. **Nominal and Real Executives**  
   The President is the nominal or titular executive. All executive actions are taken in the name of the President, but actual decisions are made by the Council of Ministers. Article 74 makes it mandatory for the President to act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except in a few situations where the President may exercise discretion.

2. **Majority Party Rule**  
   The political party or coalition that secures a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the government. The leader of that majority becomes the Prime Minister. In the states the same principle applies with respect to the Legislative Assembly.

3. **Collective Responsibility**  
   Article 75(3) states that the Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People. This means that the entire ministry stands or falls together. A no-confidence motion against the Council of Ministers, if passed, forces the resignation of the whole government. Individual ministers may also resign or be removed, but collective responsibility remains the cornerstone.

4. **Political Homogeneity**  
   Members of the Council of Ministers normally belong to the same political party or a pre-poll or post-poll alliance that shares a common programme. This ensures coherence in policy and decision-making.

5. **Double Membership**  
   Ministers must be members of Parliament (or become members within six months of appointment under Article 75(5)). A person who is not a member of either House can be appointed minister but must secure membership within the stipulated period, otherwise the office is vacated.

6. **Leadership of the Prime Minister**  
   The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers, the leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha, and the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. The Prime Minister allocates portfolios, can recommend the removal of ministers, and exercises overall control over the administration.

7. **Dissolution of the Lower House**  
   The President may dissolve the Lok Sabha on the advice of the Prime Minister. This power is an important feature that distinguishes the parliamentary system from the presidential system, where the legislature has a fixed term.

8. **Secrecy of Procedure**  
   The proceedings of the Cabinet are confidential. Ministers are bound by the oath of secrecy and by the principle of collective responsibility not to disclose differences that may have existed inside the Cabinet.

## Distinction from the Presidential System

In a presidential system (for example, the United States) the executive is independent of the legislature. The President is both head of state and head of government, is elected for a fixed term, and cannot be removed by a simple vote of no confidence. Separation of powers is more rigid. In the Indian parliamentary system the executive is fused with the legislature, and continuous accountability is maintained through question hour, zero hour, adjournment motions, and no-confidence motions.

## Merits of the Parliamentary System

- Continuous accountability of the executive to the legislature ensures responsiveness.
- The system allows for flexible leadership: a change of Prime Minister or ministry does not necessarily require a general election.
- It is more representative of diverse political opinions through coalitions when no single party has a clear majority.
- Harmony between the legislature and the executive reduces the possibility of prolonged deadlocks.

## Demerits and Challenges in the Indian Context

- Instability can arise when no party has a clear majority, leading to frequent changes of government or dependence on external support.
- The dominance of the Prime Minister and the ruling party can sometimes reduce the effectiveness of parliamentary scrutiny.
- Defections and floor-crossing, though regulated by the Tenth Schedule, have historically affected the stability of governments.
- The system places heavy demands on the quality of political leadership and party organisation.

## Constitutional Provisions

- Article 74: Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President.
- Article 75: Appointment, tenure, collective responsibility, and other conditions of ministers.
- Article 78: Duties of the Prime Minister with respect to the furnishing of information to the President.
- Similar provisions exist for the states under Articles 163, 164 and 167.

## Evolution and Practice

Since 1950 India has mostly functioned under single-party majority governments or stable coalitions. Periods of instability (late 1960s, late 1980s and 1990s) demonstrated both the flexibility and the vulnerabilities of the system. The introduction of the anti-defection law (52nd Amendment, 1985) and later amendments sought to strengthen party discipline and governmental stability.

The parliamentary system remains the bedrock of Indian democracy. Its success depends on the observance of constitutional conventions, the quality of political parties, the vigilance of the legislature, and the impartiality of the higher judiciary in interpreting the Constitution.

## Key Points for Retention

- Real executive is the Council of Ministers; nominal executive is the President.
- Collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha is mandatory.
- Prime Minister is the keystone of the Cabinet arch.
- Dissolution of the lower house is possible on the advice of the Prime Minister.
- The system prioritises continuous accountability over rigid separation of powers.
