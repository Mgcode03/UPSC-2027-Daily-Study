# Attitude: Content, Structure, Function and Its Influence on Behaviour

Attitude is a psychological construct that represents an individual’s evaluation of a person, object, event or idea. It is a learned predisposition to respond in a consistently favourable or unfavourable manner. Attitudes are central to the study of ethics and human behaviour because they influence judgment, decision-making and action.

## Definition and Nature

An attitude can be defined as a relatively enduring organisation of beliefs, feelings and behavioural tendencies towards socially significant objects, groups, events or symbols. Attitudes are not innate; they are acquired through experience, socialisation, education and observation.

## Content of Attitude (The ABC Model)

Most social psychologists accept that attitudes have three components:

1. **Affective Component**  
   The emotional or feeling aspect. It involves positive or negative emotions associated with the attitude object. Example: feeling of pride towards the national flag or dislike towards corruption.

2. **Behavioural (Conative) Component**  
   The predisposition to act in a certain way towards the attitude object. Example: a person with a positive attitude towards environmental protection may participate in cleanliness drives or refuse single-use plastics.

3. **Cognitive Component**  
   The beliefs, thoughts and knowledge about the attitude object. Example: the belief that democracy is the best form of government or that honesty is essential for public life.

These three components are usually consistent with one another, but inconsistencies can occur (cognitive dissonance).

## Structure of Attitude

Attitudes can be understood in terms of their strength, accessibility, ambivalence and complexity.

- **Strength**: Strong attitudes are more resistant to change, more stable over time, and more likely to influence behaviour.
- **Accessibility**: The ease with which an attitude comes to mind. Highly accessible attitudes exert greater influence on behaviour.
- **Ambivalence**: Simultaneous possession of positive and negative evaluations of the same object.
- **Complexity**: The number of considerations or dimensions that underlie an attitude.

Attitudes also exist at different levels of specificity — from very general (attitude towards “politics”) to very specific (attitude towards a particular policy or candidate).

## Functions of Attitude (Functional Approach)

Daniel Katz and others proposed that attitudes serve important psychological functions for the individual:

1. **Knowledge Function**  
   Attitudes help organise and make sense of the complex social world. They provide a frame of reference for interpreting new information.

2. **Utilitarian (Instrumental) Function**  
   Attitudes help individuals gain rewards and avoid punishments. People develop favourable attitudes towards objects that satisfy their needs and unfavourable attitudes towards those that frustrate them.

3. **Ego-Defensive Function**  
   Attitudes protect the individual’s self-esteem and justify actions that might otherwise produce guilt or anxiety. Example: a person who has failed may develop a negative attitude towards the examination system.

4. **Value-Expressive Function**  
   Attitudes allow individuals to express their central values and self-concept. Supporting a particular social cause or political ideology affirms one’s identity.

Understanding the function an attitude serves is important for any attempt to change it. An attitude that serves an ego-defensive function is unlikely to change merely through the presentation of new information.

## Attitude and Behaviour

The relationship between attitude and behaviour is not always straightforward. Several factors moderate the attitude–behaviour link:

- **Strength and Clarity of the Attitude**: Stronger, clearer attitudes predict behaviour better.
- **Accessibility**: Attitudes that come to mind easily are more likely to guide behaviour.
- **Specificity**: Specific attitudes predict specific behaviours better than general attitudes.
- **Social Norms and Situational Constraints**: Even a strong attitude may not translate into behaviour if social pressure or practical obstacles intervene.
- **Direct Experience**: Attitudes formed through direct experience are better predictors of behaviour than those formed through second-hand information.

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen) expands the model by including subjective norms and perceived behavioural control as additional determinants of intention, which in turn predicts behaviour.

## Attitude Formation

Attitudes are formed through:

- Classical conditioning and operant conditioning.
- Observational learning and modelling (Bandura).
- Direct experience and personal involvement.
- Socialisation by family, peer groups, educational institutions and media.
- Cognitive learning — acquisition of information and beliefs.

## Attitude Change

Persuasion research has identified several routes and factors:

- **Source Factors**: Credibility, attractiveness and expertise of the communicator.
- **Message Factors**: One-sided versus two-sided arguments, fear appeals, order of presentation, repetition.
- **Audience Factors**: Intelligence, self-esteem, prior attitudes, involvement.
- **Channel Factors**: Face-to-face, print, electronic media.

The Elaboration Likelihood Model distinguishes between the central route (careful processing of arguments) and the peripheral route (reliance on superficial cues). Lasting attitude change is more likely through the central route.

Cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger) explains that when behaviour is inconsistent with attitude, individuals experience discomfort and are motivated to reduce it by changing either the attitude or the behaviour, or by adding new cognitions.

## Relevance to Civil Services and Ethics

For a civil servant, attitudes towards integrity, impartiality, public service, empathy and accountability are critical. A positive attitude towards the weaker sections, transparency and constitutional values shapes the quality of governance. Training, role models, organisational culture and personal reflection all play roles in the formation and reinforcement of desirable attitudes.

## Key Points for Retention

- Attitude has three components: affective, behavioural and cognitive (ABC model).
- Attitudes serve knowledge, utilitarian, ego-defensive and value-expressive functions.
- Strong, accessible and specific attitudes are better predictors of behaviour.
- Attitude change is more durable when it occurs through the central route of persuasion.
- Cognitive dissonance arises when attitude and behaviour are inconsistent.
