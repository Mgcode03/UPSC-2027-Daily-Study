# Post-Mauryan Period: Sungas, Kanvas, Satavahanas and Contemporary Powers

The decline of the Mauryan Empire after the death of Ashoka (circa 232 BCE) and the eventual disintegration under the later Mauryas created a political vacuum that was filled by several regional and successor dynasties. The period from roughly 185 BCE to the rise of the Guptas in the fourth century CE is often called the post-Mauryan age. It witnessed political fragmentation, the emergence of new centres of power, significant cultural developments, and increased interaction with Central Asian and Mediterranean regions.

## The Sunga Dynasty (circa 185–73 BCE)

Pushyamitra Sunga, the commander-in-chief of the last Mauryan ruler Brihadratha, assassinated the king and established the Sunga dynasty with its capital at Pataliputra. The Sungas ruled primarily over the Magadhan heartland and parts of northern and central India.

### Important Features

- Pushyamitra performed the Ashvamedha sacrifice, signalling a revival of Vedic ritualism after the Mauryan emphasis on Buddhism.
- The dynasty faced invasions from the Indo-Greeks (Yavanas). The Greek ruler Demetrius and later Menander are associated with campaigns into the Gangetic plain.
- Literary evidence includes the plays of Kalidasa (Malavikagnimitra mentions Agnimitra, son of Pushyamitra) and the Buddhist text Divyavadana, which portrays Pushyamitra as hostile to Buddhism. Archaeological evidence, however, shows continued Buddhist activity under the Sungas.
- The famous gateway (torana) of the Sanchi stupa and the Bharhut stupa belong to the Sunga period, indicating royal or elite patronage of Buddhist art even if the rulers themselves favoured Brahmanical traditions.
- The last Sunga ruler was Devabhuti, who was overthrown by his minister Vasudeva Kanva.

## The Kanva Dynasty (circa 73–28 BCE)

The Kanvas were Brahmana ministers who succeeded the Sungas. Their rule was short-lived and confined largely to Magadha. They were eventually displaced by the rising power of the Satavahanas from the Deccan.

## The Satavahana Dynasty

The Satavahanas (also called Andhras in some Puranic accounts) emerged as the most important power in the Deccan after the Mauryas. Their rule extended from the late second century BCE to the early third century CE, with fluctuating territorial control.

### Political History

- The earliest known ruler is Simuka. The dynasty is mentioned in the Puranas as Andhra-bhrityas.
- Gautamiputra Satakarni (circa first–second century CE) is regarded as the greatest Satavahana ruler. His achievements are recorded in the Nasik inscription of his mother Gautami Balashri. He defeated the Shakas (Western Kshatrapas), the Yavanas and the Pahlavas, and claimed to have restored the glory of the Satavahanas. He is described as the destroyer of the Shakas and the restorer of the order of the four varnas.
- His successor Vashishthiputra Pulumavi continued expansion and maintained relations with the Western Kshatrapas through matrimonial alliances.
- Yajna Sri Satakarni was another notable later ruler who revived maritime trade and issued coins depicting ships.

### Administration and Economy

- The Satavahanas followed a monarchical system with a strong emphasis on the king as the upholder of dharma.
- The kingdom was divided into aharas (districts) administered by amatyas or governors.
- They issued a large number of lead, potin and silver coins. The silver coins often bore the portrait of the king, showing Hellenistic influence.
- Agriculture remained the backbone, but trade flourished. The Satavahanas controlled important ports on the western coast (Sopara, Kalyan, Chaul) and eastern coast, facilitating trade with the Roman Empire. Roman gold coins have been found in large numbers in the Deccan.
- The famous Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (first century CE) describes the flourishing Indo-Roman trade during this period.

### Society and Culture

- The Satavahanas were the first major dynasty in the Deccan to issue inscriptions in Prakrit. Most of their inscriptions are in Prakrit written in the Brahmi script.
- They claimed Brahmana status and performed Vedic sacrifices, yet they also patronised Buddhism. Many Buddhist caves at Nasik, Karle, Kanheri and Amaravati received donations from Satavahana rulers and officials.
- Matronymics were common (Gautamiputra, Vashishthiputra), indicating the importance of the mother’s lineage, though the society remained patriarchal.
- The Amaravati school of art flourished under their patronage, characterised by narrative reliefs depicting the life of the Buddha and Jataka stories.

## Other Contemporary Powers

### Indo-Greeks
After the Mauryan collapse, Indo-Greek rulers controlled parts of north-western India. Menander (Milinda) is the most famous; his dialogues with the Buddhist monk Nagasena are recorded in the Milindapanho. Indo-Greek coins are noted for their artistic quality and bilingual legends (Greek and Kharoshthi).

### Shakas (Scythians) and Western Kshatrapas
The Shakas established several branches. The Western Kshatrapas ruled over western India (Malwa, Gujarat, Maharashtra) from the first to the fourth century CE. Rudradaman I (mid-second century CE) is famous for the Junagadh inscription, the first major inscription in chaste Sanskrit, which records the repair of the Sudarshana lake.

### Kushanas
The Kushanas, of Yuezhi origin, established a vast empire under Kanishka (circa first–second century CE) that stretched from Central Asia to the Gangetic plain. Their capital was Purushapura (Peshawar), with Mathura as a secondary centre. Kanishka is associated with the Fourth Buddhist Council and the spread of Mahayana Buddhism. The Gandhara and Mathura schools of art reached high levels of development under Kushana patronage.

## Significance of the Post-Mauryan Period

- Political fragmentation was accompanied by cultural synthesis. Indian, Greek, Central Asian and Iranian elements interacted.
- Trade networks expanded dramatically, linking India with the Roman world, Central Asia and Southeast Asia.
- Art and architecture saw the development of distinct regional styles (Gandhara, Mathura, Amaravati).
- Religious life became more diverse: Buddhism (especially Mahayana), Jainism, Bhagavatism and Shaivism all flourished.
- The use of Sanskrit gradually increased in official inscriptions, preparing the ground for the classical Sanskrit culture of the Gupta age.

## Key Facts for Memory

- Pushyamitra Sunga founded the Sunga dynasty after assassinating the last Mauryan king.
- Gautamiputra Satakarni was the greatest Satavahana ruler; his achievements are recorded in the Nasik prasasti of Gautami Balashri.
- Satavahanas issued coins with ship motifs, indicating maritime trade.
- Rudradaman’s Junagadh inscription is the earliest major Sanskrit inscription of a historical nature.
- Kanishka is linked with the Fourth Buddhist Council and the flourishing of Gandhara art.
