# Inflation: Concepts, Types, Causes, Measurement and Effects

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time. When the price level rises, each unit of currency buys fewer goods and services, resulting in a decline in the purchasing power of money.

## Basic Concepts

- **Inflation**: Continuous rise in the general price level.
- **Deflation**: Continuous fall in the general price level.
- **Disinflation**: Slowing down of the rate of inflation.
- **Reflation**: Deliberate attempt to raise the price level after a period of deflation or low inflation.
- **Stagflation**: Simultaneous occurrence of high inflation and high unemployment (or stagnant growth).
- **Hyperinflation**: Extremely rapid inflation, often exceeding 50 percent per month.
- **Core Inflation**: Inflation excluding volatile items such as food and fuel.
- **Headline Inflation**: Overall inflation including all items.

## Types of Inflation Based on Rate

1. **Creeping Inflation**: Rate of 1–3 percent per year; generally considered mild and sometimes beneficial for growth.
2. **Walking or Trotting Inflation**: 3–10 percent per year; requires policy attention.
3. **Galloping or Running Inflation**: Double-digit rates; harmful to the economy.
4. **Hyperinflation**: Extremely high rates leading to loss of confidence in the currency.

## Types Based on Cause

### Demand-Pull Inflation
Occurs when aggregate demand exceeds aggregate supply at the full-employment level. Classic description: “too much money chasing too few goods.” Causes include expansionary fiscal policy, increase in money supply, rise in exports, or increase in autonomous investment.

### Cost-Push Inflation
Arises from an increase in the cost of production that is passed on to consumers. Sources include:
- Rise in wages (wage-push).
- Increase in prices of raw materials or energy (especially oil).
- Increase in indirect taxes.
- Depreciation of the currency raising the cost of imports.

### Structural Inflation
Particularly relevant for developing economies. Caused by structural rigidities such as inadequate agricultural production, poor infrastructure, supply-chain bottlenecks, and market imperfections.

### Built-in or Expectational Inflation
When people expect future inflation, they demand higher wages and raise prices in anticipation, creating a self-fulfilling spiral.

## Measurement of Inflation in India

### Wholesale Price Index (WPI)
- Published by the Office of the Economic Adviser, Ministry of Commerce and Industry.
- Measures price changes at the wholesale level.
- Base year currently 2011–12.
- Components: Primary articles, Fuel and power, Manufactured products.
- Does not include services.

### Consumer Price Index (CPI)
- Measures changes in the prices of a basket of goods and services consumed by households.
- Several series existed earlier (Industrial Workers, Agricultural Labourers, Rural Labourers, Urban Non-Manual Employees).
- The current national CPI (combined) is published by the National Statistical Office with base year 2012.
- CPI is the measure used by the Reserve Bank of India for its inflation-targeting framework.

### GDP Deflator
- Ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP multiplied by 100.
- Broadest measure covering all goods and services produced in the economy.
- Available with a lag.

## Inflation Targeting in India

Under the Monetary Policy Framework Agreement (2015) and later the amended RBI Act, the Reserve Bank of India is mandated to maintain CPI inflation at 4 percent with a tolerance band of ±2 percent (that is, 2–6 percent). The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) decides the policy repo rate to achieve this target.

## Effects of Inflation

### Positive Effects (Mild Inflation)
- Encourages investment and production because real interest rates are lower and profits appear higher.
- Reduces the real burden of debt.
- May stimulate economic growth if demand is rising.

### Negative Effects
- Erodes purchasing power, especially of fixed-income groups (pensioners, salaried employees).
- Distorts relative prices and resource allocation.
- Creates uncertainty, discouraging long-term contracts and investment.
- Hurts exporters if domestic inflation is higher than in trading partners (loss of competitiveness).
- Leads to arbitrary redistribution of income from creditors to debtors.
- Can generate a wage-price spiral.
- In extreme cases, leads to flight from currency and collapse of the monetary system.

## Inflation and Unemployment: The Phillips Curve

In the short run, there is often an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment (Phillips Curve). Policymakers may face a trade-off. In the long run, according to the natural-rate hypothesis, unemployment returns to its natural rate regardless of the inflation rate; only unexpected inflation can temporarily reduce unemployment.

## Key Policy Instruments to Control Inflation

**Monetary Measures** (RBI)  
- Increase in repo rate / reverse repo rate.  
- Increase in Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) or Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR).  
- Open market sale of securities.  
- Moral suasion and credit controls.

**Fiscal Measures**  
- Reduction in government expenditure.  
- Increase in taxes.  
- Rationalisation of subsidies.

**Administrative and Supply-Side Measures**  
- Buffer-stock operations and open-market sale of food grains.  
- Import of essential commodities.  
- Action against hoarding and black marketing.  
- Improving logistics and reducing supply-chain bottlenecks.

## Key Points for Retention

- Headline inflation in India is measured by CPI (combined); the RBI targets 4 ± 2 percent.
- Demand-pull inflation results from excess demand; cost-push from rising production costs.
- WPI does not include services; CPI does.
- Mild inflation can be growth-friendly; high or volatile inflation is damaging.
- The Phillips Curve suggests a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
