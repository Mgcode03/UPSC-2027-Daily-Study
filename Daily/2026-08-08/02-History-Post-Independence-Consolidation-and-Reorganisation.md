# Post-Independence Consolidation and Reorganisation of States

## Challenges at Independence

When India became independent on 15 August 1947, the new nation faced multiple challenges of consolidation. The immediate tasks included the integration of princely states, the management of communal violence and refugee rehabilitation following Partition, the framing of a new Constitution, and the reorganisation of administrative and linguistic units.

## Integration of Princely States

At the time of independence there were 562 princely states covering nearly 40% of the territory of undivided India. Under the Indian Independence Act, 1947, these states were theoretically free to join India, Pakistan or remain independent. The Instrument of Accession provided for the transfer of three subjects—defence, external affairs and communications—to the Dominion of India.

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Minister of Home Affairs and States, and V.P. Menon as Secretary of the States Ministry, played the central role in securing the accession of almost all states by 15 August 1947. Three states posed special difficulties:

- **Junagadh**: The Nawab acceded to Pakistan despite a Hindu majority population. A plebiscite later confirmed accession to India.
- **Hyderabad**: The Nizam desired independence. After prolonged negotiations and the outbreak of communal violence and Razakar excesses, the Indian government launched “Operation Polo” in September 1948. Hyderabad was integrated into the Indian Union.
- **Jammu and Kashmir**: Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947 after tribal invaders from Pakistan entered the state. Indian troops were airlifted to defend the territory. The matter later went to the United Nations.

By 1950 most of the larger states had been merged or formed into unions of states, and the distinction between Part A, B, C and D states was introduced under the Constitution.

## Linguistic Reorganisation of States

The demand for linguistic states had been raised during the freedom struggle. The Indian National Congress had accepted the principle of linguistic provinces as early as 1920. After independence, however, the government was initially cautious because of the fear that linguistic division might encourage separatism.

The death of Potti Sriramulu after a fast unto death for a separate Andhra state in 1952 led to the creation of Andhra State in 1953. This intensified demands elsewhere.

The States Reorganisation Commission (SRC) was appointed in 1953 under Justice Fazl Ali, with K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru as members. Its report (1955) recommended reorganisation primarily on linguistic lines while rejecting the idea of “one language, one state” as absolute. The States Reorganisation Act, 1956, redrew the map of India. Fourteen states and six union territories were created. The distinction between Part A, B and C states was abolished.

Subsequent reorganisations included:

- Bombay State was bifurcated into Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960.
- Punjab was reorganised in 1966 into Punjab, Haryana and Himachal Pradesh (the latter initially a Union Territory).
- Several northeastern states were created between the 1960s and 1980s (Nagaland 1963, Meghalaya, Manipur and Tripura 1972, Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram 1987).
- Goa became a state in 1987.
- Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand were created in 2000.
- Telangana was carved out of Andhra Pradesh in 2014.

## Administrative and Institutional Consolidation

Apart from territorial integration, the new republic built key institutions:

- The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, establishing a democratic republic with a parliamentary system, federal features with a strong centre, and a comprehensive chapter on Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
- The Planning Commission was set up in 1950 to guide economic development through Five-Year Plans.
- The Indian Administrative Service and other All-India Services provided administrative continuity and a unified steel frame.
- The Election Commission conducted the first general elections in 1951–52 on the basis of universal adult franchise—an unprecedented experiment in a largely illiterate society.

## Key Features of the Consolidation Process

- Peaceful and largely negotiated integration of princely states, with force used only in exceptional cases.
- Acceptance of linguistic identity as a legitimate basis for state formation while maintaining national unity.
- Gradual democratisation of former princely territories and extension of constitutional rights.
- Building of national institutions that transcended regional and linguistic identities.

The post-independence consolidation successfully transformed a fragmented political landscape into a functioning federal democracy. The linguistic reorganisation, while creating new states, did not lead to the disintegration that many had feared; instead, it strengthened the sense of belonging within a larger Indian identity.
