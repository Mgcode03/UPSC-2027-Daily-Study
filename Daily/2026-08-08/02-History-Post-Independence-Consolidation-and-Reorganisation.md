# Post-Independence Consolidation and Reorganisation of States

## Challenges at Independence

When India became independent on 15 August 1947, the new nation faced multiple and simultaneous challenges of consolidation. The immediate tasks included the integration of 562 princely states, the management of large-scale communal violence and the rehabilitation of millions of refugees following Partition, the framing of a new Constitution for a democratic republic, the establishment of administrative continuity, and the eventual reorganisation of internal boundaries on a more rational basis.

The Partition itself had created two dominions and left deep scars. The refugee crisis, particularly in Punjab and Bengal, required massive relief and rehabilitation efforts. At the same time, the new government had to assert authority over a diverse and fragmented political landscape.

## Integration of Princely States

At the time of independence there were 562 princely states covering nearly 40% of the territory of undivided India and about 30% of the population. Under the Indian Independence Act, 1947, paramountcy of the British Crown lapsed and these states were theoretically free to join India, Pakistan or remain independent. In practice, geographic location, composition of population and the persuasion (and in some cases pressure) of the Indian leadership determined the outcome.

The Instrument of Accession provided for the transfer of three subjects—defence, external affairs and communications—to the Dominion of India, while the states retained autonomy in other matters initially.

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Minister of Home Affairs and States, and V.P. Menon as Secretary of the States Ministry, played the central role in securing the accession of almost all states by 15 August 1947. Their approach combined firmness, diplomatic skill and, where necessary, the use of force. Three states posed special difficulties:

- **Junagadh**: The Nawab, a Muslim ruler of a predominantly Hindu state, acceded to Pakistan. The Government of India rejected the accession as contrary to the principle of geographic contiguity and popular will. A provisional government was formed by local leaders, Indian forces entered, and a subsequent plebiscite confirmed accession to India by an overwhelming majority.

- **Hyderabad**: The Nizam desired independence and delayed accession. The state had a Hindu majority but a Muslim ruler and an influential paramilitary force called the Razakars. Communal violence and the breakdown of law and order led the Indian government to launch “Operation Polo” (also called the Police Action) in September 1948. Within a few days Hyderabad was integrated into the Indian Union. The Nizam later signed the Instrument of Accession.

- **Jammu and Kashmir**: Maharaja Hari Singh initially sought to remain independent. In October 1947, tribal invaders from Pakistan, supported by elements of the Pakistani army, entered the state. Facing collapse, the Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947. Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar the next day. The conflict led to the first India-Pakistan war and the matter was referred to the United Nations. A ceasefire left the state divided along the Ceasefire Line (later the Line of Control).

By 1949–50 most of the larger states had been merged with neighbouring provinces or formed into unions of states (such as Rajasthan, Madhya Bharat, PEPSU, Saurashtra and Travancore-Cochin). The Constitution of 1950 classified the units into Part A (former governors’ provinces), Part B (former princely unions with Rajpramukhs), Part C (chief commissioners’ provinces) and Part D (Andaman and Nicobar Islands).

## Linguistic Reorganisation of States

The demand for linguistic states had deep roots in the freedom struggle. The Indian National Congress had accepted the principle of linguistic provinces as early as the Nagpur session of 1920 and had reorganised its own provincial committees on linguistic lines. After independence, however, the government was initially cautious. The Dhar Commission (1948) and the JVP Committee (Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya, 1949) advised against immediate linguistic reorganisation, fearing that it might encourage separatism and undermine national unity at a fragile moment.

The death of Potti Sriramulu after a prolonged fast unto death for a separate Andhra state in December 1952 created a strong wave of public sentiment. The government conceded and created Andhra State in 1953 by separating the Telugu-speaking areas from Madras Presidency. This intensified demands for linguistic states elsewhere.

The States Reorganisation Commission (SRC) was appointed in December 1953 under Justice Fazl Ali, with K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru as members. Its report, submitted in 1955, recommended reorganisation primarily on linguistic lines while rejecting the idea of “one language, one state” as an absolute principle. It also recommended the abolition of the Part A/B/C classification and the creation of a uniform category of states along with union territories.

The States Reorganisation Act, 1956, implemented most of the SRC’s recommendations (with some modifications). Fourteen states and six union territories were created. The distinction between Part A, B and C states was abolished. The new map reflected linguistic majorities while attempting to balance administrative viability and national unity.

### Subsequent Reorganisations

- Bombay State was bifurcated into the linguistic states of Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960 after intense agitation.
- Punjab was reorganised in 1966 on linguistic lines into Punjab (Punjabi-speaking) and Haryana (Hindi-speaking); the hilly areas went to Himachal Pradesh (then a Union Territory).
- Several northeastern states were created between the 1960s and 1980s in response to ethnic and regional aspirations and security considerations: Nagaland (1963), Meghalaya, Manipur and Tripura (full statehood 1972), Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram (1987).
- Goa, which had been liberated from Portuguese rule in 1961, became a state in 1987 (after earlier functioning as a Union Territory).
- In 2000, three new states were created: Chhattisgarh (from Madhya Pradesh), Uttarakhand (from Uttar Pradesh) and Jharkhand (from Bihar), largely on grounds of regional development and distinct identity.
- Telangana was carved out of Andhra Pradesh in 2014 after a prolonged movement, becoming the 29th state of the Union.

## Administrative and Institutional Consolidation

Apart from territorial integration, the new republic built key national institutions that provided the framework for democratic governance and planned development:

- The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, establishing a democratic republic with a parliamentary system, a federal structure with a strong Centre, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy, and an independent judiciary.
- The Planning Commission was set up in March 1950 to formulate Five-Year Plans and guide the process of economic development in a mixed economy framework.
- The Indian Administrative Service, Indian Police Service and other All-India Services provided a unified administrative steel frame that helped maintain continuity and national integration across states.
- The Election Commission of India conducted the first general elections in 1951–52 on the basis of universal adult franchise—an unprecedented democratic experiment in a largely rural and illiterate society. The successful conduct of these elections strengthened the legitimacy of the new democratic order.
- Institutions such as the Union Public Service Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Finance Commission and the Inter-State Council (later) further reinforced the constitutional scheme of governance.

## Key Features and Significance of the Consolidation Process

- Peaceful and largely negotiated integration of princely states, with the use of force limited to exceptional cases (Hyderabad and, in a different sense, Junagadh and Kashmir).
- Acceptance of linguistic identity as a legitimate basis for state formation while carefully managing the risks of fragmentation.
- Gradual democratisation of the former princely territories and the extension of constitutional rights and representative institutions to all parts of the country.
- Building of strong national institutions that transcended regional, linguistic and princely identities and created a shared framework of citizenship and governance.
- Ability of the political leadership to balance firmness with flexibility, and national unity with regional aspirations.

The post-independence consolidation successfully transformed a fragmented political landscape—comprising British provinces, princely states of varying sizes and degrees of autonomy, and areas under foreign colonial pockets—into a functioning federal democracy. The linguistic reorganisation, while creating new states and occasionally generating tensions, did not lead to the disintegration that many had feared. Instead, by accommodating linguistic and regional identities within a democratic federal framework, it strengthened the overall sense of belonging to a larger Indian political community.

This process of consolidation remains one of the most significant achievements of the early years of the Indian Republic and continues to provide the territorial and institutional foundation of the Indian state.
