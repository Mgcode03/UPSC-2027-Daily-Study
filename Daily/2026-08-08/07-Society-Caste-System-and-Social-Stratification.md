# Caste System and Social Stratification in India

## Concept of Social Stratification

Social stratification refers to the ranking of individuals and groups in any society on the basis of unequal access to resources, power, prestige and opportunities. All complex societies exhibit some form of stratification. In India, the caste system has historically been the most distinctive and elaborate form of social stratification, although class, gender, ethnicity and region also structure inequality.

## Features of the Traditional Caste System

The classical understanding of caste (varna and jati) includes the following features:

- **Segmental division of society**: Society is divided into a large number of closed, mutually exclusive segments (jatis).
- **Hierarchy**: Castes are arranged in a rank order, traditionally linked to notions of purity and pollution.
- **Restrictions on interaction**: Rules governing commensality (inter-dining), marriage (endogamy) and occupation.
- **Occupational specialisation**: Hereditary association of castes with particular occupations, though the link was never absolute.
- **Ritual and religious sanctions**: The system was legitimised by religious texts and customary practices.

The four-fold varna scheme (Brahmana, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) provided a broad ideological framework, while the actual units of endogamy and social life were the numerous jatis.

## Untouchability and Exclusion

At the bottom of the traditional hierarchy were groups subjected to the practice of untouchability—denied access to temples, wells, schools and public spaces, and forced into occupations considered ritually polluting. The Constitution of India abolished untouchability (Article 17) and made its practice a punishable offence. Despite legal prohibition, residual social discrimination persists in varying degrees in different regions.

## Changes in the Caste System

Several forces have transformed the working of caste since the colonial period and especially after independence:

- **Urbanisation and industrialisation**: Reduced the rigidity of occupational specialisation and everyday interaction rules in cities.
- **Democratic politics**: Caste has become a basis for political mobilisation and assertion. The expansion of adult franchise gave numerical strength to lower castes political voice.
- **Affirmative action**: Reservations in education, public employment and legislatures for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and later Other Backward Classes have altered the opportunity structure.
- **Education and economic mobility**: Created new middle classes within previously disadvantaged castes and weakened the ritual hierarchy in many contexts.
- **Legal and constitutional framework**: Fundamental Rights, the Protection of Civil Rights Act, the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, and various welfare measures have provided instruments for challenging caste-based discrimination.

Caste has not disappeared; it has changed its form. Ritual hierarchy has weakened in many spheres, while caste as an identity for political and economic competition has often strengthened.

## Caste and Class

Caste and class are analytically distinct but empirically intertwined in India. Economic class (based on income, education and occupation) cuts across caste, yet average differences in assets, education and occupation between caste groups remain significant. The rise of a Dalit middle class and the persistence of poverty among sections of upper castes illustrate the complexity of the relationship.

## Contemporary Issues

- Persistence of caste-based discrimination and violence in certain regions and contexts.
- Debates over the extension, modification or review of reservation policies.
- The role of caste in electoral politics and the formation of political alliances.
- Intersection of caste with gender, resulting in compounded disadvantages for women from lower castes.
- Growing assertion of identity and demands for dignity alongside demands for material redistribution.

## Sociological Perspectives

Different sociological approaches have interpreted caste variously: as a unique cultural system based on purity and pollution (Louis Dumont), as a form of social stratification comparable to other systems, as a product of colonial enumeration and codification, and as a lived reality continuously reshaped by economic and political change. For a civil servant, a nuanced understanding that recognises both the historical depth of caste and its contemporary transformations is essential for sensitive and effective administration.
