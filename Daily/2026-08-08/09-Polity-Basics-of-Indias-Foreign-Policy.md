# Basics of India’s Foreign Policy

## Constitutional and Institutional Framework

The Constitution of India does not contain a detailed chapter on foreign policy, but several provisions are relevant. Article 51 (Directive Principles) directs the State to promote international peace and security, maintain just and honourable relations between nations, foster respect for international law and treaty obligations, and encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration. The Union List places foreign affairs, diplomatic representation, treaties and the United Nations under the exclusive competence of the Centre. The President is the formal head of state for external purposes; actual conduct of foreign policy is the responsibility of the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, with the Ministry of External Affairs as the nodal ministry.

## Continuities in Indian Foreign Policy

Certain principles have shown remarkable continuity since independence:

- **Strategic autonomy**: The determination to take decisions independently on the basis of national interest rather than as part of a rigid alliance system. This was expressed during the Cold War as non-alignment and continues in modified form in the contemporary multipolar environment.
- **Peaceful coexistence and non-interference**: Respect for the sovereignty and territorial integrity of other states and opposition to external intervention in internal affairs.
- **Support for decolonisation and anti-racism**: A consistent posture during the second half of the twentieth century.
- **Commitment to multilateralism**: Active participation in the United Nations and other international organisations, and advocacy of reformed multilateral institutions that better reflect contemporary realities.
- **Neighbourhood focus**: Recognition that a stable and cooperative neighbourhood is essential for India’s security and development.

## Evolution since Independence

**Nehru era**: Emphasis on non-alignment, Afro-Asian solidarity, and moral-diplomatic leadership. The 1962 conflict with China was a major setback.

**Post-1962 and Indira Gandhi years**: Greater realism, closer ties with the Soviet Union (Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation, 1971), and the 1971 intervention leading to the creation of Bangladesh.

**Post-Cold War period**: Adaptation to the collapse of the Soviet Union, economic liberalisation at home, and the search for diversified partnerships. The Look East Policy (later Act East) deepened engagement with Southeast and East Asia. Nuclear tests in 1998 and the subsequent dialogue with the United States marked a significant shift.

**Twenty-first century**: Multi-alignment or issue-based alignment—simultaneous engagement with the United States, Russia, European partners, Japan, Australia and others; active participation in groupings such as BRICS, SCO, Quad and IORA; growing emphasis on economic diplomacy, energy security, diaspora engagement and the Indo-Pacific construct.

## Key Determinants

- Geography and the extended neighbourhood (South Asia, Indian Ocean, Southeast Asia, West Asia, Central Asia).
- Economic development needs and the search for markets, investment, technology and energy resources.
- Security challenges, including border disputes, terrorism and maritime security.
- Domestic political consensus and the influence of public opinion on major foreign policy choices.
- Global power shifts and the rise of new centres of influence.

## Major Contemporary Themes

- Managing the relationship with China—competition, cooperation and border management.
- Deepening the strategic partnership with the United States while maintaining relations with Russia.
- Strengthening connectivity and economic integration with neighbours and the extended neighbourhood.
- Climate diplomacy, health diplomacy and reformed multilateralism.
- Diaspora as a soft-power and economic asset.

India’s foreign policy remains fundamentally pragmatic, seeking to expand strategic space, secure developmental interests and contribute to a stable international order while preserving autonomy of decision-making.
