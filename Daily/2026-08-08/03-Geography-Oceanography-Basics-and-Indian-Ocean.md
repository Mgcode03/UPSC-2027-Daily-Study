# Oceanography Basics and the Indian Ocean

## Scope of Oceanography

Oceanography is the scientific study of the oceans. It includes physical oceanography (currents, waves, tides, temperature and salinity), chemical oceanography, biological oceanography and geological oceanography (sea-floor topography, sediments and plate tectonics under the oceans).

## Major Oceans and Their Relative Size

The five oceans in order of size are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern (Antarctic) and Arctic. The Indian Ocean is the third largest and is unique in being almost completely enclosed by land on three sides—Asia to the north, Africa to the west and Australia to the east—with the Southern Ocean to the south.

## Physical Features of the Ocean Floor

The ocean floor is divided into several major topographic units:

- **Continental Shelf**: The gently sloping submerged extension of the continent. It is economically important for fishing, oil and gas, and minerals. The width varies greatly; it is wide along the eastern coast of India and narrower along the western coast in many stretches.
- **Continental Slope**: The steeper descent from the shelf edge to the deep ocean floor.
- **Abyssal Plains**: Vast, flat or gently sloping areas of the deep ocean floor, covered by fine sediments.
- **Oceanic Ridges and Rises**: Underwater mountain chains formed by divergent plate boundaries. The Mid-Indian Ridge is a major feature of the Indian Ocean.
- **Trenches**: Deep, narrow depressions formed at convergent plate boundaries. The Java Trench (Sunda Trench) is the deepest in the Indian Ocean.
- **Seamounts and Guyots**: Isolated underwater volcanoes; guyots are flat-topped seamounts.

## Temperature and Salinity

Ocean water temperature decreases with depth. The surface layer is warmer, followed by a thermocline (zone of rapid temperature change) and then the cold deep water. Salinity is the total amount of dissolved salts, expressed in parts per thousand. Average ocean salinity is about 35‰. Factors affecting salinity include evaporation, precipitation, river inflow, ice formation and melting, and ocean currents.

In the Indian Ocean, salinity is relatively high in the Arabian Sea because of high evaporation and limited freshwater inflow, while the Bay of Bengal has lower salinity due to the large freshwater discharge from the Ganga-Brahmaputra and other rivers.

## Ocean Currents

Ocean currents are large-scale horizontal movements of water. They are driven primarily by prevailing winds, differences in temperature and salinity (thermohaline circulation), the Coriolis force and the shape of ocean basins.

Currents are classified as warm or cold. Warm currents flow from equatorial regions towards the poles; cold currents flow from polar regions towards the equator.

### Indian Ocean Circulation

The Indian Ocean circulation is strongly influenced by the monsoon winds and therefore reverses seasonally in the northern part.

- During the southwest monsoon (summer), the Somali Current flows northward as a strong western boundary current and upwelling occurs along the Somali coast.
- The Southwest Monsoon Current flows eastward across the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal.
- During the northeast monsoon (winter), the North Equatorial Current is well developed and the Somali Current reverses.

In the southern Indian Ocean the circulation is more permanent and includes the South Equatorial Current, the Agulhas Current (warm) along the east coast of Africa, and the West Australian Current (cold).

## Tides

Tides are the periodic rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational attraction of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun. When the Sun, Moon and Earth are aligned (new moon and full moon), spring tides with higher range occur. When they form a right angle (quarter moons), neap tides with lower range occur.

The shape of the coastline and the bathymetry influence the tidal range. The Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch experience some of the highest tidal ranges in India.

## Importance of the Indian Ocean for India

- Strategic location astride major sea lanes connecting the Persian Gulf, Europe, East Africa and East Asia.
- Source of living resources (fisheries) and non-living resources (polymetallic nodules, hydrocarbons on the continental shelf).
- Influence on the Indian monsoon through ocean-atmosphere interaction.
- Growing significance in maritime security, blue economy and regional cooperation frameworks.

Understanding the physical characteristics of the Indian Ocean is essential for appreciating India’s maritime geography, climate, resources and strategic environment.
