# Speaker of the Lok Sabha and Parliamentary Committees

## Office of the Speaker

The Speaker is the principal presiding officer of the Lok Sabha, the lower house of the Parliament of India. The office is created by the Constitution under Article 93. The Speaker is elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves. The election normally takes place in the first sitting of the newly constituted House after a general election.

### Election and Tenure

- The Speaker is chosen by a simple majority of the members present and voting.
- Once elected, the Speaker continues in office until the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, resignation, or removal.
- Removal requires a resolution passed by an effective majority of the total membership of the House (more than 50% of the total strength). A notice of at least 14 days must be given before such a motion is moved.
- The Speaker can resign by writing to the Deputy Speaker.

### Powers and Functions

The Speaker performs legislative, administrative and quasi-judicial functions.

**Legislative and Procedural Powers**
- Presides over the sittings of the Lok Sabha and maintains order and decorum.
- Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110. This decision is final and cannot be questioned in any court.
- Interprets the Rules of Procedure and conducts the business of the House according to them.
- Adjourns the House or suspends the meeting in the absence of quorum.
- Permits the moving of various kinds of motions, resolutions and adjournment motions.
- Certifies a Money Bill and decides on questions of privilege.

**Administrative Powers**
- Heads the Lok Sabha Secretariat.
- Appoints the staff of the Secretariat and regulates their conditions of service (in consultation with the President in certain matters).
- Acts as the channel of communication between the House and the President.

**Quasi-Judicial Powers**
- Decides on disqualification of members under the Tenth Schedule (anti-defection law), except in cases where the Speaker’s own decision is under challenge.
- Protects the privileges of the House and its members.

### Special Position

Even after the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, the Speaker continues in office until immediately before the first meeting of the new House. This ensures continuity in the administrative machinery of the House.

The Speaker does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in case of a tie. This maintains the Speaker’s position of neutrality.

### Deputy Speaker

Article 93 also provides for a Deputy Speaker. The Deputy Speaker is elected in the same manner and performs the duties of the Speaker when the latter is absent. When both are absent, a member from the Panel of Chairpersons presides.

## Parliamentary Committees

Parliamentary Committees are essential instruments through which Parliament exercises detailed scrutiny of the executive and performs specialised functions that cannot be handled effectively in the full House.

### Classification

Committees are broadly classified into two categories:

1. **Standing Committees** – permanent and reconstituted periodically.
2. **Ad hoc Committees** – temporary, constituted for a specific purpose and dissolved after the task is completed.

### Important Standing Committees

**Financial Committees**

- **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**: Examines the annual audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General. It scrutinises the appropriation of funds and points out irregularities. It consists of 22 members (15 from Lok Sabha and 7 from Rajya Sabha). A member of the Opposition is conventionally appointed as Chairperson.
- **Estimates Committee**: Suggests economies in public expenditure and examines whether the money is well laid out. It has 30 members, all from Lok Sabha. It does not examine Public Accounts or Railways.
- **Committee on Public Undertakings**: Examines the reports and accounts of public sector undertakings. It has 22 members (15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha).

**Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)**

There are 24 DRSCs covering all ministries. Each has 31 members (21 from Lok Sabha and 10 from Rajya Sabha). They examine Bills, demands for grants, annual reports of ministries and long-term policy documents. Their recommendations are advisory.

**Other Important Standing Committees**

- Business Advisory Committee: Recommends allocation of time for discussion of government and other business.
- Committee on Privileges: Examines questions of privilege and recommends action.
- Committee on Petitions: Examines petitions from the public.
- Committee on Government Assurances: Scrutinises the assurances, promises and undertakings given by ministers on the floor of the House.
- Committee on Subordinate Legislation: Examines whether the powers to make rules, regulations and by-laws conferred by the Constitution or statutes are being properly exercised.
- Committee on the Welfare of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- Committee on Empowerment of Women.

### Ad hoc Committees

These include Select Committees and Joint Committees on Bills, and Inquiry Committees constituted for specific investigations.

### Significance of Committees

- They enable detailed examination of complex issues which the House as a whole cannot undertake due to constraints of time.
- They provide a forum for inter-party consensus and reduce the adversarial atmosphere of the floor of the House.
- They strengthen the accountability of the executive to the legislature.
- Membership of committees gives back-benchers an opportunity to specialise and contribute meaningfully.

The committee system is one of the most effective instruments of parliamentary control over the executive in the Indian constitutional scheme.
