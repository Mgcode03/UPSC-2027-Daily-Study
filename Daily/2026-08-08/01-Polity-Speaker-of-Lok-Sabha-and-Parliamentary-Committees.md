# Speaker of the Lok Sabha and Parliamentary Committees

## Office of the Speaker

The Speaker is the principal presiding officer of the Lok Sabha, the lower house of the Parliament of India. The office is created by the Constitution under Article 93. The Speaker is elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves. The election normally takes place in the first sitting of the newly constituted House after a general election.

### Election and Tenure

- The Speaker is chosen by a simple majority of the members present and voting.
- Once elected, the Speaker continues in office until the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, resignation, or removal.
- Removal requires a resolution passed by an effective majority of the total membership of the House (more than 50% of the total strength). A notice of at least 14 days must be given before such a motion is moved.
- The Speaker can resign by writing to the Deputy Speaker.

When a resolution for the removal of the Speaker is under consideration, the Speaker cannot preside. The Deputy Speaker or another member from the Panel of Chairpersons takes the chair. This ensures fairness in the process.

### Powers and Functions

The Speaker performs legislative, administrative and quasi-judicial functions.

**Legislative and Procedural Powers**
- Presides over the sittings of the Lok Sabha and maintains order and decorum.
- Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110. This decision is final and cannot be questioned in any court.
- Interprets the Rules of Procedure and conducts the business of the House according to them.
- Adjourns the House or suspends the meeting in the absence of quorum.
- Permits the moving of various kinds of motions, resolutions and adjournment motions.
- Certifies a Money Bill and decides on questions of privilege.
- Determines the order of speakers and allots time for debates.
- Can order the withdrawal of a member or name a member for disorderly conduct, leading to suspension by the House.

**Administrative Powers**
- Heads the Lok Sabha Secretariat.
- Appoints the staff of the Secretariat and regulates their conditions of service (in consultation with the President in certain matters).
- Acts as the channel of communication between the House and the President.
- Exercises control over the parliamentary precincts and the security arrangements within them.

**Quasi-Judicial Powers**
- Decides on disqualification of members under the Tenth Schedule (anti-defection law), except in cases where the Speaker’s own decision is under challenge.
- Protects the privileges of the House and its members.
- Decides on the admissibility of questions, motions and resolutions.

### Special Position and Neutrality

Even after the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, the Speaker continues in office until immediately before the first meeting of the new House. This ensures continuity in the administrative machinery of the House.

The Speaker does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in case of a tie. This maintains the Speaker’s position of neutrality. By convention, the Speaker is expected to conduct the proceedings of the House in an impartial manner, irrespective of the party to which he or she belonged before election. In practice, the degree of perceived neutrality has varied across Speakers.

### Deputy Speaker and Panel of Chairpersons

Article 93 also provides for a Deputy Speaker. The Deputy Speaker is elected in the same manner and performs the duties of the Speaker when the latter is absent. When both are absent, a member from the Panel of Chairpersons presides. The Panel is nominated by the Speaker from among the members of the House.

The offices of Speaker and Deputy Speaker become vacant if the holder ceases to be a member of the Lok Sabha. Both can be removed by a resolution of the House passed by an effective majority after 14 days’ notice.

## Parliamentary Committees

Parliamentary Committees are essential instruments through which Parliament exercises detailed scrutiny of the executive and performs specialised functions that cannot be handled effectively in the full House. The committee system allows for more informal, bipartisan and in-depth examination of issues.

### Classification

Committees are broadly classified into two categories:

1. **Standing Committees** – permanent and reconstituted periodically (usually every year or after a general election).
2. **Ad hoc Committees** – temporary, constituted for a specific purpose and dissolved after the task is completed.

Standing Committees are further divided into financial committees, departmentally related standing committees, and other subject-specific committees.

### Important Standing Committees

**Financial Committees**

- **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**: Examines the annual audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General relating to the appropriation of sums granted by Parliament. It scrutinises whether money has been spent as authorised and points out irregularities, waste and inefficiencies. It consists of 22 members (15 from Lok Sabha and 7 from Rajya Sabha). By convention, a member of the Opposition is appointed as Chairperson. The PAC does not question the policy underlying expenditure; it focuses on the regularity and propriety of expenditure.

- **Estimates Committee**: Suggests economies in public expenditure, examines whether the money is well laid out within the limits of the policy implied in the estimates, and suggests alternative policies to bring about efficiency and economy. It has 30 members, all from Lok Sabha. It does not examine Public Accounts or the accounts of public undertakings (the latter is the domain of the Committee on Public Undertakings). It also does not examine the estimates of autonomous bodies or those presented under a statute.

- **Committee on Public Undertakings**: Examines the reports and accounts of public sector undertakings and the reports of the CAG on them. It also examines whether the affairs of the public undertakings are being managed in accordance with sound business principles and prudent commercial practices. It has 22 members (15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha).

**Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)**

There are 24 DRSCs covering all ministries and departments of the Government of India. Each committee has 31 members (21 from Lok Sabha and 10 from Rajya Sabha). They examine:
- Bills referred to them,
- Demands for grants of the related ministries,
- Annual reports of the ministries, and
- Long-term policy documents presented to Parliament.

Their recommendations are advisory in nature. The DRSCs have significantly enhanced the quality of legislative and financial scrutiny by allowing continuous and specialised oversight.

**Other Important Standing Committees**

- Business Advisory Committee: Recommends allocation of time for discussion of government and other business. The Speaker is the chairperson.
- Committee on Privileges: Examines questions of breach of privilege of the House or its members and recommends appropriate action.
- Committee on Petitions: Examines petitions submitted by the public on bills or other matters of public importance.
- Committee on Government Assurances: Scrutinises the assurances, promises and undertakings given by ministers on the floor of the House and reports on the extent to which they have been implemented.
- Committee on Subordinate Legislation: Examines whether the powers to make rules, regulations, bye-laws and other statutory instruments conferred by the Constitution or by statutes are being properly exercised within the scope of the delegation.
- Committee on the Welfare of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- Committee on Empowerment of Women.
- Committee on Papers Laid on the Table.
- House Committee, Library Committee and Joint Committee on Salaries and Allowances of Members.

### Ad hoc Committees

These include Select Committees and Joint Committees appointed to examine particular Bills in detail, and Inquiry or Investigative Committees constituted for specific purposes (for example, to inquire into a particular incident or scandal). Once their report is presented, they cease to exist.

### Working of Committees

Committees generally function behind closed doors, which encourages frank discussion and reduces political posturing. They can summon officials, experts and stakeholders, call for documents and undertake study visits. Reports are presented to the House and, in many cases, the government is expected to furnish action-taken replies.

### Significance of the Committee System

- They enable detailed examination of complex legislative, financial and policy issues which the House as a whole cannot undertake due to constraints of time and the nature of floor debates.
- They provide a forum for inter-party consensus and reduce the adversarial atmosphere that often characterises proceedings on the floor of the House.
- They strengthen the accountability of the executive to the legislature by continuous scrutiny rather than only during budget sessions or question hour.
- Membership of committees gives back-benchers an opportunity to specialise in particular areas and contribute meaningfully to parliamentary work.
- They improve the quality of legislation by allowing clause-by-clause examination and incorporation of expert and stakeholder views.

The committee system is widely regarded as one of the most effective instruments of parliamentary control over the executive in the Indian constitutional scheme. Its effectiveness, however, depends on the quality of membership, the seriousness with which reports are treated by the government, and the willingness of the political executive to engage constructively with recommendations.
