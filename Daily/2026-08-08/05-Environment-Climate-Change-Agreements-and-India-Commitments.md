# Climate Change Agreements and India’s Commitments

## Scientific Basis of Climate Change

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperature and weather patterns. The dominant cause of the observed warming since the mid-20th century is the increase in atmospheric concentrations of greenhouse gases (GHGs) resulting from human activities—primarily the burning of fossil fuels, deforestation and certain industrial and agricultural processes.

The main greenhouse gases are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and fluorinated gases. CO₂ is the most significant in terms of total contribution to radiative forcing.

## Evolution of the International Climate Regime

**United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 1992**

Adopted at the Rio Earth Summit, the UNFCCC is the parent treaty. It recognises the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC). Annex I parties (developed countries) were expected to take the lead in reducing emissions. The Conference of the Parties (COP) is the supreme decision-making body.

**Kyoto Protocol, 1997**

The Kyoto Protocol set binding emission reduction targets for Annex I parties for the first commitment period (2008–2012). It introduced flexible mechanisms: Emissions Trading, Joint Implementation and the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM). India participated actively in the CDM. The Protocol reflected a sharp differentiation between developed and developing countries.

**Paris Agreement, 2015**

The Paris Agreement marks a shift to a universal, bottom-up approach. All parties submit Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) outlining their climate actions. The Agreement aims to limit global temperature increase to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. It also establishes a global goal on adaptation and a framework for climate finance, technology and capacity-building. A transparency framework and a global stocktake every five years are key features.

Subsequent COPs have focused on rules for implementation (Katowice Rulebook), ambition, finance, loss and damage, and the phase-down of unabated coal and fossil fuel subsidies.

## India’s Climate Commitments

India’s approach is guided by the principles of equity, climate justice and CBDR-RC. India emphasises that development and poverty eradication remain overriding priorities and that historical responsibility for cumulative emissions lies primarily with developed countries.

**Key elements of India’s NDC (updated)**

- Reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005 levels.
- Achieve about 50% cumulative electric power installed capacity from non-fossil fuel sources by 2030.
- Create an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent through additional forest and tree cover by 2030.
- Promote a healthy and sustainable way of living based on traditional values (LiFE – Lifestyle for Environment).
- Better adaptation in agriculture, water, disaster management and other vulnerable sectors.

India has also announced the goal of achieving net-zero emissions by 2070.

**Domestic Policy Framework**

- National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) with eight national missions covering solar energy, energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, Himalayan ecosystem, green India, sustainable agriculture and strategic knowledge.
- State Action Plans on Climate Change.
- Promotion of renewable energy (solar and wind capacity expansion), energy efficiency programmes, electric mobility, and green hydrogen.
- Participation in international initiatives such as the International Solar Alliance.

## Finance, Technology and Equity Issues

Developing countries, including India, have consistently highlighted the gap between the climate finance committed by developed countries and the amounts actually delivered. Technology transfer on favourable terms and capacity-building remain important demands. The concept of loss and damage has gained greater recognition in recent COPs, reflecting the reality that some impacts of climate change cannot be adapted to.

## Significance for India

India is highly vulnerable to climate change impacts—rising temperatures, changing monsoon patterns, glacial melt in the Himalayas, sea-level rise affecting coastal areas, and increased frequency of extreme weather events. At the same time, India is the world’s third-largest emitter in absolute terms (though per-capita emissions remain low). Balancing rapid development with low-carbon transition is therefore both a domestic imperative and an international responsibility.

Understanding the architecture of climate agreements and India’s specific commitments is essential for analysing environmental policy, energy transition and sustainable development questions.
