# Contributions of Moral Thinkers and Philosophers

## Importance for Ethics in Public Administration

The study of moral thinkers provides conceptual foundations for understanding right and wrong, duty, justice, virtue and the good life. In the context of civil services, these ideas help in clarifying foundational values, resolving ethical dilemmas, cultivating an ethical temperament, and justifying decisions in a principled manner. Familiarity with major ethical traditions enables a civil servant to move beyond intuition and personal preference to reasoned moral judgement.

## Indian Moral Thinkers and Traditions

**Kautilya (Chanakya)**

In the Arthashastra, Kautilya presents a realist yet ethically grounded view of statecraft. He emphasises that the happiness of the people is the ultimate end of the ruler: “In the happiness of the subjects lies the happiness of the king.” The king is advised to be energetic, accessible, disciplined and just. Corruption is treated as a serious and pervasive administrative problem requiring constant vigilance, institutional checks, and exemplary punishment. Kautilya discusses the qualities required of counsellors and officials, the importance of intelligence and information, and the need for accountability in the use of public resources. His ideas remain relevant for understanding the ethical dimensions of administration, anti-corruption measures and the responsibilities of those who wield power.

**Buddha**

The Buddha’s teachings centre on the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path. Key ethical concepts include non-violence (ahimsa), compassion (karuna), right speech, right action and right livelihood. The emphasis on mindfulness, self-restraint, the reduction of craving and the cultivation of mental purity has direct implications for personal integrity, emotional balance and the ability to act without attachment to personal gain in public life. Buddhist ethics also stress the interdependence of all beings, which can support a sense of universal responsibility.

**Mahavira and Jain Ethics**

Jain ethics stress extreme non-violence, truthfulness (satya), non-stealing (asteya), celibacy or restraint (brahmacharya) and non-possession (aparigraha). The doctrine of anekantavada (the many-sidedness of reality) and syadvada encourages intellectual humility and tolerance of multiple viewpoints. In a plural and diverse society, this attitude of non-absolutism in thought is valuable for public servants who must deal with competing claims and perspectives.

**Gandhi**

Mahatma Gandhi’s ethical framework rests on truth (satya) and non-violence (ahimsa) as cardinal principles. He regarded means and ends as inseparable; impure means cannot produce pure ends. His concept of trusteeship holds that wealth and power are held in trust for society, especially for the poorest. Gandhi emphasised simple living, fearlessness (abhaya), self-suffering as a method of political action, and the identification of the individual with the lowliest. For civil servants, Gandhi’s insistence on purity of means, moral courage, transparency, and service to the last person remains a powerful reference point. His life also illustrates the integration of personal ethics with public action.

**Ambedkar**

B.R. Ambedkar’s ethical and political thought is grounded in the values of liberty, equality and fraternity. He emphasised constitutional morality—the adherence to the forms and spirit of the Constitution even when it is inconvenient—and the annihilation of caste as essential for a genuine democracy. He argued that democracy is not merely a form of government but a mode of associated living based on respect for the dignity of every individual. His insistence on the rights of the oppressed, the importance of education and representation, and the moral necessity of social democracy has deep implications for social justice policies and the ethical orientation of the state.

**Other Indian Sources**

The Bhagavad Gita’s teaching of nishkama karma (action without attachment to fruits) has been widely drawn upon as a guide for duty-oriented work. The idea of dharma as righteous conduct appropriate to one’s role and situation has also shaped traditional ethical discourse, though it requires careful reinterpretation in a modern constitutional setting.

## Western Moral Thinkers

**Socrates, Plato and Aristotle**

Socrates emphasised the examined life and the unity of knowledge and virtue—the idea that wrongdoing stems from ignorance of the good. Plato linked justice in the individual soul with justice in the state and argued for rule by those who possess knowledge of the Form of the Good. Aristotle developed a systematic virtue ethics centred on the cultivation of character through habit and the doctrine of the mean (virtue as a mean between excess and deficiency). He regarded the purpose of the political community as enabling citizens to live a good and flourishing life (eudaimonia). Aristotle’s distinctions between different forms of justice—distributive, corrective and reciprocal—continue to influence thinking about fairness in the allocation of offices, resources and punishments.

**Immanuel Kant**

Kant’s deontological ethics places duty and the moral law at the centre of ethical life. The categorical imperative has several formulations; the most influential are: act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law; and act so as to treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. Kantian ethics stresses autonomy of the will, respect for persons as rational agents, and the primacy of intention and duty over consequences. It provides a strong philosophical foundation for integrity, impartiality, non-arbitrariness and respect for the rights and dignity of individuals in public service.

**Utilitarianism – Bentham and Mill**

Jeremy Bentham proposed that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number, understanding happiness in terms of pleasure and the absence of pain. He developed a hedonic calculus for measuring pleasures and pains. John Stuart Mill refined classical utilitarianism by distinguishing higher (intellectual and moral) and lower (bodily) pleasures and by giving a central place to individual liberty in On Liberty. Utilitarian reasoning is frequently employed in public policy when weighing aggregate costs and benefits. However, pure utilitarianism can raise serious concerns about the rights of minorities, the justice of means, and the possibility of sacrificing individuals for the greater good. Modern policy analysis often uses consequentialist tools while embedding them within a framework of rights and fairness.

**John Rawls**

John Rawls’s theory of justice as fairness, developed in A Theory of Justice, uses the device of the original position and the veil of ignorance. Rational persons choosing principles of justice without knowing their own place in society would, Rawls argues, choose equal basic liberties for all, and permit social and economic inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (the difference principle) and are attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity. Rawls provides a powerful framework for thinking about the design of public institutions, the justification of affirmative action, the distribution of primary goods, and the moral claims of the disadvantaged.

**Other Influential Ideas**

Contemporary discussions also draw on virtue ethics revivals, care ethics (emphasising relationships and responsibility), and discourse ethics. For public administration, the combination of virtue (character), deontology (duty and rights) and consequentialism (outcomes), tempered by a concern for justice and care, offers a rich toolkit.

## Relevance to Civil Services

- Virtue ethics (Aristotle, Gandhi and aspects of Indian traditions) highlights the importance of character, habitual right action, and the cultivation of virtues such as integrity, courage, temperance and justice.
- Deontology (Kant) reinforces the claims of duty, impartiality, respect for persons and the idea that certain actions are wrong regardless of consequences.
- Utilitarianism and consequentialism aid in the analysis of policy outcomes and the weighing of competing social benefits, while requiring safeguards for rights and fairness.
- Rawlsian justice provides criteria for evaluating institutional arrangements and redistributive policies.
- Indian traditions contribute distinctive emphases on non-violence, trusteeship, constitutional morality, compassion and the dignity of the individual.

A civil servant benefits from familiarity with these traditions not as rigid doctrines to be applied mechanically, but as intellectual and moral resources that enrich the capacity for reasoned, responsible and humane decision-making in complex and often conflicting situations.
