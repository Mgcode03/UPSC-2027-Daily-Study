# Indian Ocean and Maritime Geography

## Location and Extent of the Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean is the third-largest of the world's oceanic divisions. It is bounded by Asia on the north, Africa on the west, Australia on the east, and the Southern Ocean on the south. It covers approximately one-fifth of the total ocean area of the Earth.

The Indian Ocean is unique among the major oceans because it is largely landlocked in the north. This geographical feature has important implications for its climate, circulation patterns, and strategic significance.

## Major Seas and Gulfs

Several important seas and gulfs form part of the Indian Ocean system:

- **Arabian Sea**: Located between the Indian Peninsula and the Arabian Peninsula. It is a major route for international shipping and contains important ports.
- **Bay of Bengal**: Located to the east of the Indian Peninsula. It receives a large volume of freshwater from the major river systems of the Indian subcontinent.
- **Red Sea**: Connected to the Indian Ocean through the Gulf of Aden and the Bab-el-Mandeb strait. It is a vital link between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean via the Suez Canal.
- **Persian Gulf**: Connected through the Strait of Hormuz. It is of critical strategic and economic importance due to oil resources.
- **Andaman Sea**: Located between the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the Malay Peninsula.
- **Mozambique Channel**: Separates Madagascar from the African mainland.

## Ocean Floor Topography

The floor of the Indian Ocean consists of several major features:

### Mid-Oceanic Ridges

The most prominent feature is the Mid-Indian Ocean Ridge, which is part of the global mid-ocean ridge system. It runs roughly north-south and is associated with seafloor spreading. The Carlsberg Ridge in the northwestern part and the Southwest Indian Ridge are important segments.

### Ocean Basins

Major basins include the Arabian Basin, the Central Indian Basin, the Wharton Basin, and the Crozet Basin. These are relatively deep, flat areas of the ocean floor.

### Trenches

The Java Trench (also known as the Sunda Trench) is the deepest part of the Indian Ocean. It is associated with the subduction of the Indian Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate and is a zone of significant seismic and volcanic activity.

### Seamounts and Islands

Numerous seamounts and island groups are found in the Indian Ocean. Important island groups include the Maldives, the Lakshadweep, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, the Seychelles, Mauritius, and the Chagos Archipelago.

## Ocean Currents of the Indian Ocean

Ocean currents in the Indian Ocean are strongly influenced by the monsoon wind system. This makes the circulation pattern different from that of the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.

### Monsoon-Driven Circulation

In the northern Indian Ocean, the surface circulation reverses with the seasons:

- During the southwest monsoon (summer), surface currents flow in a clockwise direction in the northern Indian Ocean.
- During the northeast monsoon (winter), the circulation becomes anticlockwise.

This seasonal reversal is a distinctive feature of the Indian Ocean.

### Major Currents

Important currents include:

- **Somali Current**: A strong western boundary current that develops during the southwest monsoon. It is associated with intense upwelling along the Somali coast.
- **Agulhas Current**: Flows southward along the east coast of Africa. It is one of the strongest western boundary currents and plays a role in the exchange of water between the Indian and Atlantic Oceans.
- **Leeuwin Current**: Flows southward along the western coast of Australia.
- **South Equatorial Current**: Flows westward across the southern Indian Ocean.
- **West Australian Current**: Forms part of the subtropical gyre in the southern Indian Ocean.

## Temperature and Salinity

Surface temperatures in the Indian Ocean are generally high, especially in the northern parts. The Bay of Bengal has relatively lower salinity because of the large influx of freshwater from rivers such as the Ganga, Brahmaputra, and Irrawaddy. The Arabian Sea has higher salinity due to higher evaporation and limited freshwater input.

## Strategic and Economic Significance

The Indian Ocean is of immense strategic and economic importance:

- It carries a large proportion of the world's oil trade, especially from the Persian Gulf region.
- Major sea lanes of communication (SLOCs) pass through the Indian Ocean, connecting Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and East Asia.
- Important choke points include the Strait of Hormuz, the Bab-el-Mandeb, the Strait of Malacca, and the Sunda Strait.
- The ocean is rich in living and non-living resources, including fisheries, hydrocarbons, and mineral deposits on the ocean floor.
- The exclusive economic zones of littoral states are of great economic value.

## India’s Maritime Geography

India has a long coastline of over 7,500 kilometres, including the mainland and the island groups. The maritime zones of India include:

- Territorial waters (up to 12 nautical miles)
- Contiguous zone (up to 24 nautical miles)
- Exclusive Economic Zone (up to 200 nautical miles)
- Continental shelf (which may extend beyond 200 nautical miles under certain conditions)

India’s island territories — the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal and the Lakshadweep in the Arabian Sea — are of strategic importance for maritime security and surveillance.

## Ports and Maritime Trade

India has a large number of major and minor ports. Major ports on the west coast include Mumbai, Kandla, Cochin, and New Mangalore. On the east coast, important ports include Chennai, Visakhapatnam, Paradip, and Kolkata-Haldia. These ports handle the bulk of India’s international trade.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Indian Ocean is the third-largest ocean and is landlocked in the north.
- Major seas: Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal, Red Sea, Persian Gulf.
- Java Trench is the deepest part of the Indian Ocean.
- Circulation in the northern Indian Ocean reverses with the monsoons.
- Somali Current is associated with strong upwelling.
- Agulhas Current is a major western boundary current.
- Bay of Bengal has lower salinity than the Arabian Sea.
- Critical choke points: Strait of Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb, Strait of Malacca.
- India’s EEZ extends up to 200 nautical miles.

## Conceptual Notes

Understanding the physical geography of the Indian Ocean is essential for grasping India’s strategic maritime interests, monsoon dynamics, and resource potential. The monsoon-driven circulation makes the northern Indian Ocean unique. The concentration of energy trade routes and the presence of critical choke points give the Indian Ocean a central place in global geopolitics. India’s central location in the northern Indian Ocean underpins its maritime strategy and its engagement with littoral states.
