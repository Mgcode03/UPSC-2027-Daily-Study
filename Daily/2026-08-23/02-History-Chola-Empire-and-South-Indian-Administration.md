# Chola Empire and South Indian Administration

## Historical Background of the Cholas

The Cholas were one of the three major Tamil dynasties of ancient and early medieval South India, the other two being the Cheras and the Pandyas. The early Cholas are mentioned in Sangam literature and in the accounts of classical writers. However, the imperial phase of Chola history began with the rise of Vijayalaya in the ninth century CE.

Vijayalaya captured Thanjavur around 850 CE and established it as the Chola capital. His successors expanded the kingdom significantly. The most celebrated rulers of the imperial Cholas were Rajaraja I (985–1014 CE) and his son Rajendra I (1014–1044 CE).

## Rajaraja I and the Expansion of the Empire

Rajaraja I transformed the Chola kingdom into a powerful empire. He conquered the Chera country, the Pandya territories, and parts of Sri Lanka. He also subdued the Western Gangas and established Chola authority over parts of the Deccan. His naval expeditions demonstrated the maritime strength of the Cholas.

Rajaraja I is remembered for the construction of the Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur, a masterpiece of Dravidian architecture and a symbol of Chola power and devotion. The temple inscriptions provide detailed information about land grants, administration, and the organisation of temple management.

## Rajendra I and Overseas Expansion

Rajendra I continued and expanded the conquests of his father. He completed the conquest of Sri Lanka and led a successful expedition to the Ganga valley, earning the title Gangaikonda. To commemorate this victory, he founded a new capital called Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

Rajendra I also launched a major naval expedition against the Srivijaya kingdom in Southeast Asia. This expedition demonstrated the long-distance maritime capabilities of the Chola navy and the commercial and political interests of the Cholas in the Indian Ocean region.

## Administrative System of the Cholas

The Chola administrative system was highly organised and efficient. It combined centralised control with a remarkable degree of local self-government.

### Central Administration

The king was the central figure in the administration. He was assisted by a council of ministers and a large number of officials. The king was the supreme commander of the army and the final authority in judicial matters. Royal orders were issued in the form of inscriptions and copper-plate grants.

The empire was divided into provinces known as mandalams. Each mandalam was placed under a viceroy, often a member of the royal family. The mandalams were further divided into valanadus or districts, and these into nadus or groups of villages.

### Local Self-Government

One of the most distinctive features of Chola administration was the highly developed system of local self-government in villages. Village assemblies played a crucial role in local administration.

There were different types of village assemblies:

- **Ur**: The assembly of the common villages, consisting of all the tax-paying residents.
- **Sabha or Mahasabha**: The assembly of the Brahmin villages (agrahara villages). These were more exclusive and had detailed rules for membership and functioning.
- **Nagaram**: The assembly of the merchant towns.

These assemblies managed local affairs such as irrigation, temples, justice, and collection of taxes. They maintained their own accounts and had the authority to levy and collect local dues.

### Committees of the Sabha

The sabha functioned through a number of committees known as variyams. Different committees looked after specific functions such as:

- Garden supervision
- Tank maintenance and irrigation
- Temple management
- Justice and settlement of disputes
- Collection of taxes and management of finances

Membership of these committees was regulated by detailed rules. There were qualifications regarding age, property, knowledge of the Vedas, and character. There were also disqualifications for those who had committed certain offences or who had not rendered accounts properly.

The Uttaramerur inscriptions provide detailed information about the working of these committees and the method of selection of members through a system of lots (kudavolai system).

## Revenue Administration

Land revenue was the main source of income for the Chola state. Land was carefully surveyed and classified according to its fertility and the nature of the crop. Different rates of assessment were fixed for different categories of land.

Taxes were collected both in cash and in kind. In addition to land revenue, there were various other taxes and dues, including those on professions, trade, and cattle. Temples received extensive land grants and played an important role in the rural economy.

## Military Organisation

The Cholas maintained a large and well-organised army. The army consisted of infantry, cavalry, and elephants. The navy was particularly strong and played a key role in overseas expeditions and the protection of maritime trade routes.

Military officials held important positions in the administration. The king often led military campaigns personally. Forts and strategic locations were carefully maintained.

## Temple Administration and Society

Temples occupied a central place in Chola society and administration. Large temples functioned as economic, social, and cultural centres. They owned extensive lands, employed large numbers of people, and managed educational and charitable activities.

Temple inscriptions record grants of land, gold, and other resources. They also provide information about the organisation of temple services, the rights and duties of temple servants, and the management of temple finances.

## Cultural Achievements

The Chola period is regarded as a high point of Tamil culture and Dravidian architecture. The Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur and the temple at Gangaikonda Cholapuram are outstanding examples of Chola temple architecture.

Bronze sculpture reached great heights during this period. The Nataraja bronze images are among the finest achievements of Indian art. Literature in Tamil flourished, and the period saw the composition of important religious and secular works.

## Decline of the Chola Empire

After the death of Rajendra I, the empire continued under successive rulers but gradually faced challenges from the rising power of the Pandyas, the Hoysalas, and later the Delhi Sultanate. By the thirteenth century, the Chola power had declined significantly, and the dynasty came to an end.

## Key Points for Memorisation

- Imperial Cholas rose under Vijayalaya in the ninth century CE.
- Rajaraja I and Rajendra I were the greatest Chola rulers.
- Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur was built by Rajaraja I.
- Rajendra I founded Gangaikonda Cholapuram and led the Srivijaya expedition.
- Village assemblies (ur, sabha, nagaram) were central to local administration.
- The kudavolai system was used for selecting committee members.
- Land revenue was the main source of state income.
- Temples were major economic and social institutions.

## Conceptual Notes

The Chola administrative system is important for understanding the nature of early medieval South Indian polity. It combined strong central authority with significant local autonomy. The detailed functioning of village assemblies demonstrates a sophisticated tradition of local self-governance. The maritime achievements of the Cholas highlight the importance of the Indian Ocean in the political and economic history of South India. The integration of temple institutions into the administrative and economic structure is a distinctive feature of the period.
