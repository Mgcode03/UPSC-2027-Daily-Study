# Environmental Pollution and Control

## Meaning and Types of Pollution

Pollution is the introduction of contaminants into the natural environment that cause adverse change. It can take the form of chemical substances or energy such as noise, heat or light. The major types of pollution relevant for environmental studies are air pollution, water pollution, soil pollution, noise pollution and plastic pollution.

## Air Pollution

Air pollution is the presence of substances in the atmosphere that are harmful to the health of humans and other living beings, or cause damage to the climate or materials. Major air pollutants include:

- Particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10): fine particles that penetrate deep into the lungs
- Sulphur dioxide (SO2): from burning of fossil fuels containing sulphur
- Nitrogen oxides (NOx): from vehicle emissions and industrial combustion
- Carbon monoxide (CO): from incomplete combustion
- Ozone (O3) at ground level: formed by reaction of NOx and volatile organic compounds in sunlight
- Lead and other heavy metals
- Ammonia and other gases

Sources are both natural (volcanic eruptions, dust storms, forest fires) and anthropogenic (vehicles, power plants, industries, construction, burning of biomass and waste).

Effects include respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, reduced visibility, acid rain, damage to crops and forests, and contribution to climate change through greenhouse gases and black carbon.

Control measures include emission standards for vehicles and industries, promotion of cleaner fuels (CNG, LPG, electric vehicles), use of electrostatic precipitators and scrubbers in industries, afforestation, and public transport improvements. The National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) aims to reduce particulate pollution in identified cities.

## Water Pollution

Water pollution is the contamination of water bodies such as rivers, lakes, oceans, groundwater and aquifers. Major pollutants are:

- Pathogens from untreated sewage
- Organic waste leading to high biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)
- Nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) causing eutrophication
- Heavy metals (mercury, lead, arsenic, cadmium)
- Pesticides and industrial chemicals
- Plastics and microplastics
- Thermal pollution from power plants

Sources include domestic sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, and oil spills. In India the major rivers and many lakes suffer from severe pollution due to untreated sewage and industrial discharge.

Effects include water-borne diseases, destruction of aquatic ecosystems, bioaccumulation of toxins in the food chain, and shortage of safe drinking water.

Control measures involve sewage treatment plants, effluent treatment by industries, common effluent treatment plants for clusters of small industries, regulation under the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, and initiatives such as the Namami Gange programme for the Ganga.

## Soil Pollution

Soil pollution is the presence of toxic chemicals, salts, disease-causing agents or radioactive substances in soil in concentrations high enough to cause risk to human health or ecosystems. Causes include excessive use of chemical fertilisers and pesticides, industrial waste dumping, mining activities, and improper disposal of solid and hazardous waste.

Effects include reduced soil fertility, contamination of groundwater, entry of toxins into the food chain, and degradation of land. Remediation methods include bioremediation, phytoremediation, and physical or chemical treatment of contaminated sites.

## Noise Pollution

Noise pollution is unwanted or excessive sound that can have deleterious effects on human health, wildlife and environmental quality. Sources include traffic, industrial machinery, construction, loudspeakers and aircraft. Effects include hearing loss, stress, sleep disturbance, hypertension and reduced productivity. Standards for ambient noise are prescribed under the Environment (Protection) Act, and noise limits for vehicles and generators are regulated.

## Plastic Pollution

Plastic pollution has emerged as a major global and national concern. Single-use plastics, microplastics and the slow degradation of plastic waste affect terrestrial and marine ecosystems. Animals ingest plastics, and microplastics enter the food chain. India has banned certain single-use plastic items and is promoting extended producer responsibility, recycling and alternatives.

## Legal and Institutional Framework in India

India has a comprehensive legal framework for pollution control:

- Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974
- Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981
- Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 – an umbrella legislation
- The Pollution Control Boards at Central and State levels monitor and enforce standards
- Hazardous waste, biomedical waste, e-waste and plastic waste rules under the Environment Protection Act

The National Green Tribunal provides a specialised forum for environmental disputes.

## Key Concepts for Memory

- Air pollutants: PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NOx, CO, ground-level ozone
- Water pollution indicators: BOD, COD, dissolved oxygen, coliform count
- Eutrophication: nutrient enrichment leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion
- Bioaccumulation and biomagnification of toxins
- Major laws: Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981, Environment Protection Act 1986
- GST not relevant here; focus on environmental legislation
- NCAP for air quality; Namami Gange for river cleaning
- Noise and plastic pollution as emerging concerns

Pollution control requires a combination of technological solutions, regulatory enforcement, economic instruments and changes in consumption and production patterns. It is closely linked to public health, biodiversity conservation and sustainable development.
