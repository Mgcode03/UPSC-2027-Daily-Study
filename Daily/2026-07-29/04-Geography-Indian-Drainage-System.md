# Indian Drainage System

## Introduction to Drainage

Drainage refers to the river systems of an area. The drainage pattern of an area is the outcome of the geological time period, nature of rocks, topography, slope, and the amount and periodicity of rainfall. India has a large number of rivers that are broadly classified into two groups: the Himalayan rivers and the Peninsular rivers.

## Himalayan River Systems

The Himalayan rivers are perennial. They receive water from rain as well as from melted snow. They have long courses from their source to the sea and perform intensive erosional activity in their upper courses. In the middle and lower courses they form meanders, ox-bow lakes, and extensive flood plains and deltas.

### The Indus System

The Indus originates from a glacier near Bokhar Chu in the Tibetan region at an altitude of about 4,164 metres in the Kailash Mountain range. It flows west and northwest, enters India in Ladakh, and flows through Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Punjab before entering Pakistan. The total length is about 2,880 km, of which about 1,114 km lies in India.

Major tributaries of the Indus in India are the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Satluj. These five rivers together with the Indus form the Indus system. The Indus Water Treaty of 1960 between India and Pakistan allocates the waters of the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Satluj) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) largely to Pakistan, with limited Indian rights of use.

### The Ganga System

The Ganga rises in the Gangotri glacier near Gaumukh in the Uttarkashi district of Uttarakhand. At its source it is called the Bhagirathi. It is joined by the Alaknanda at Devprayag; from this confluence the river is known as the Ganga. It enters the plains at Haridwar.

The total length of the Ganga is about 2,525 km. It is joined by a large number of tributaries from both the left and the right banks. Important left-bank tributaries are the Ramganga, Gomati, Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi. Important right-bank tributaries are the Yamuna and the Son. The Yamuna itself rises from the Yamunotri glacier and joins the Ganga at Prayagraj (Allahabad). The Chambal, Sind, Betwa and Ken are important tributaries of the Yamuna.

The Ganga flows through Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and West Bengal. It bifurcates into the Bhagirathi-Hooghly and the Padma in West Bengal and Bangladesh. The Hooghly flows through West Bengal and joins the Bay of Bengal. The combined waters of the Ganga and the Brahmaputra form the world’s largest delta, the Sunderbans.

### The Brahmaputra System

The Brahmaputra originates in the Chemayungdung glacier of the Kailash range near the Mansarovar lake. It flows eastwards parallel to the Himalayas in Tibet, where it is known as the Tsangpo. It takes a U-turn at Namcha Barwa and enters India in Arunachal Pradesh as the Dihang. It is joined by the Dibang and the Lohit; from this confluence it is known as the Brahmaputra.

In India the Brahmaputra flows through Arunachal Pradesh and Assam. It is a braided river with many channels and islands, the most famous being Majuli, one of the largest river islands in the world. The river receives numerous tributaries from the north and the south. It enters Bangladesh and finally joins the Ganga (Padma) before flowing into the Bay of Bengal.

The Brahmaputra is known for its high volume of water, frequent floods, and the large quantity of silt it carries.

## Peninsular River Systems

Peninsular rivers are mostly seasonal, depending on rainfall. They have shorter and shallower courses compared to Himalayan rivers. Many of them flow in valleys with graded profiles and have little erosional activity in the lower courses.

### East-Flowing Rivers

The Mahanadi rises in Chhattisgarh and flows through Odisha into the Bay of Bengal. It forms a large delta. The Godavari is the largest Peninsular river. It rises in the Nasik district of Maharashtra and flows across the Deccan plateau into the Bay of Bengal, forming an extensive delta. Its major tributaries include the Penganga, Wardha, Wainganga, Indravati and Manjra.

The Krishna rises near Mahabaleshwar in Maharashtra and flows through Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh into the Bay of Bengal. Its important tributaries are the Bhima, Tungabhadra, Ghatprabha and Musi.

The Kaveri rises in the Brahmagiri range of the Western Ghats in Karnataka and flows through Tamil Nadu into the Bay of Bengal. It is a relatively smaller but culturally and agriculturally significant river. Its major tributaries are the Hemavati, Kabini, Bhavani and Amravati.

### West-Flowing Rivers

The Narmada rises in the Amarkantak plateau in Madhya Pradesh and flows westwards through a rift valley between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges into the Arabian Sea. It forms the famous Dhuandhar falls near Jabalpur and an estuary rather than a delta.

The Tapi (or Tapti) also rises in Madhya Pradesh and flows westwards parallel to the Narmada through a rift valley into the Arabian Sea. The Sabarmati and the Mahi are other important west-flowing rivers of western India.

## Drainage Patterns

Different drainage patterns are observed in India:

- Dendritic: resembles the branches of a tree; common in the northern plains and on the Peninsular plateau where rocks are uniform.
- Trellis: develops where hard and soft rocks exist parallel to each other; seen in the upper Himalayan regions and parts of the Peninsular plateau.
- Rectangular: develops on strongly jointed rocky terrain.
- Radial: develops when streams flow in different directions from a central peak or dome; seen around Amarkantak.

## Significance of Indian Rivers

Rivers are the lifelines of India. They provide water for irrigation, drinking, industry and hydropower. The alluvial plains of the Ganga and other rivers support dense populations and intensive agriculture. Deltas and estuaries are rich fishing grounds and support unique ecosystems such as the Sunderbans mangroves. Rivers also have immense cultural and religious significance.

At the same time, floods, river-bank erosion, pollution and inter-state water disputes pose serious challenges. The management of river water through multipurpose projects, interlinking proposals, and pollution control remains a major developmental and environmental concern.

## Key Points for Memory

- Himalayan rivers: perennial; Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra systems
- Indus: originates near Kailash; major tributaries Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Satluj
- Ganga: Bhagirathi + Alaknanda at Devprayag; length about 2,525 km; joins Bay of Bengal via Hooghly and Padma
- Brahmaputra: Tsangpo in Tibet; enters as Dihang; known for floods and Majuli island
- Peninsular east-flowing: Mahanadi, Godavari (largest), Krishna, Kaveri – form deltas
- Peninsular west-flowing: Narmada, Tapi – flow in rift valleys, form estuaries
- Godavari: largest Peninsular river system
- Drainage patterns: dendritic, trellis, radial, rectangular
- Rivers crucial for irrigation, power, culture; challenges of flood, pollution, disputes

Understanding the drainage system is fundamental to the physical geography of India and has direct relevance to agriculture, settlement patterns, and resource management.
