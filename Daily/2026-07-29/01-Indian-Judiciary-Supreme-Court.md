# Indian Judiciary: Supreme Court

## Constitutional Framework and Position

The Supreme Court of India stands at the apex of the Indian judicial system. It is established under Article 124 of the Constitution and forms the highest court of appeal and the final interpreter of the Constitution. The judiciary in India is independent and integrated. Independence means that the judiciary is free from interference by the executive and the legislature in the discharge of its functions. The integrated character means that there is a single hierarchy of courts with the Supreme Court at the top, followed by High Courts and subordinate courts. This is different from the federal judicial systems of countries like the United States where separate federal and state court systems exist.

The Supreme Court was inaugurated on 28 January 1950, two days after the Constitution came into force. It replaced the Federal Court of India and the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council as the highest court. The Court sits in New Delhi, though it can sit at other places with the approval of the President.

## Composition and Appointment of Judges

Article 124 provides that the Supreme Court shall consist of a Chief Justice of India and such number of other judges as Parliament may by law prescribe. Originally the strength was the Chief Justice and seven other judges. Over time Parliament has increased the strength. Currently the sanctioned strength is the Chief Justice and 33 other judges, making a total of 34.

Judges of the Supreme Court are appointed by the President. The process of appointment has evolved through judicial interpretation. Originally the Constitution required the President to consult the Chief Justice of India. In the Second Judges Case (1993) the Supreme Court held that the Chief Justice of India must consult a collegium of senior judges and that the recommendation of the collegium is binding on the executive except in exceptional circumstances. The Third Judges Case (1998) further clarified the composition of the collegium. The collegium system continues to govern appointments despite attempts to replace it with a National Judicial Appointments Commission, which was struck down by the Court in 2015.

A person is qualified to be appointed a judge of the Supreme Court if he or she is a citizen of India and has been a judge of a High Court for at least five years, or an advocate of a High Court for at least ten years, or is, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist. There is no fixed minimum age, but in practice senior lawyers and High Court judges are appointed. The retirement age is 65 years.

## Tenure, Removal and Independence

A judge of the Supreme Court holds office until the age of 65 years. He or she may resign by writing to the President. A judge can be removed only by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting, on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The procedure for investigation and proof of misbehaviour is laid down in the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968. This difficult process of removal is an important safeguard of judicial independence.

Other safeguards of independence include:

- Security of tenure
- Fixed service conditions that cannot be varied to the disadvantage of a judge after appointment
- Expenses of the Supreme Court charged on the Consolidated Fund of India
- Ban on practice after retirement in any court or before any authority in India
- Power to punish for its own contempt
- Separation of judiciary from executive in the public services of the State (Article 50)

## Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court has a wide range of jurisdiction which can be classified under the following heads.

### Original Jurisdiction

Under Article 131 the Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction in any dispute between the Government of India and one or more States, or between the Government of India and any State or States on one side and one or more other States on the other, or between two or more States, if the dispute involves a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends. This jurisdiction does not extend to disputes arising out of any treaty, agreement or similar instrument which continues in operation after the commencement of the Constitution and which provides that the jurisdiction shall not extend to such dispute. Original jurisdiction is rarely invoked.

### Writ Jurisdiction

Article 32 confers the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court can issue directions, orders or writs including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari. This jurisdiction is original and forms part of the basic structure of the Constitution. Dr. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution.

### Appellate Jurisdiction

The Supreme Court is the highest court of appeal. Its appellate jurisdiction covers constitutional, civil and criminal matters.

- Constitutional matters: An appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, decree or final order of a High Court if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution (Article 132).

- Civil matters: An appeal lies if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance and that in the opinion of the High Court the said question needs to be decided by the Supreme Court (Article 133).

- Criminal matters: An appeal lies if the High Court has on appeal reversed an order of acquittal and sentenced the accused to death or to imprisonment for life or to imprisonment for a term of ten years or more, or has withdrawn for trial before itself any case from any court subordinate to its authority and has in such trial convicted the accused and sentenced him to death or to imprisonment for life or to imprisonment for a term of ten years or more, or certifies that the case is a fit one for appeal to the Supreme Court (Article 134). Parliament may by law confer further powers.

In addition, the Supreme Court has special leave jurisdiction under Article 136. It may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India, except military tribunals and court martial. This power is very wide and is exercised sparingly.

### Advisory Jurisdiction

Under Article 143 the President may refer to the Supreme Court any question of law or fact of public importance which has arisen or is likely to arise, and the Court may after such hearing as it thinks fit report to the President its opinion. The opinion is advisory and not binding, though in practice it carries great weight. The Court is not bound to answer every reference.

### Other Powers

The Supreme Court is a court of record (Article 129). It has the power to punish for contempt of itself. It has the power to review its own judgments (Article 137). It can transfer cases from one High Court to another or from a subordinate court in one State to a subordinate court in another State. It has the power to do complete justice in any matter pending before it (Article 142). Orders under Article 142 are enforceable throughout the territory of India.

## Judicial Review

Judicial review is the power of the Supreme Court and High Courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders. If a law or order is found to violate the Constitution it can be declared null and void. Judicial review is part of the basic structure of the Constitution. The Court has used this power to strike down constitutional amendments that damage the basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973), as well as ordinary laws that violate Fundamental Rights or other constitutional provisions.

## Key Points for Memory

- Apex court under Article 124
- Strength: Chief Justice + 33 judges (total 34)
- Appointment by President on recommendation of collegium
- Retirement age: 65 years
- Removal only by impeachment process for proved misbehaviour or incapacity
- Original jurisdiction: Centre-State and inter-State disputes (Article 131)
- Writ jurisdiction under Article 32 for Fundamental Rights
- Appellate jurisdiction in constitutional, civil and criminal matters
- Special leave under Article 136
- Advisory jurisdiction under Article 143
- Court of record; power to punish for contempt
- Judicial review is part of basic structure
- Article 142: power to do complete justice

The Supreme Court plays a vital role in upholding the Constitution, protecting Fundamental Rights, and maintaining the federal balance. Its independence and wide jurisdiction make it one of the most powerful courts in the world.
