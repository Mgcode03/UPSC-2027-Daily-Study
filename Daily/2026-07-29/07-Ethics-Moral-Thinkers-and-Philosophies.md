# Ethics: Moral Thinkers and Philosophies

## Introduction to Moral Philosophy

Ethics or moral philosophy is the branch of philosophy that deals with questions of right and wrong, good and bad, virtue and vice, and the principles that should guide human conduct. For civil services, an understanding of major moral thinkers and schools of thought helps in developing a reasoned approach to ethical dilemmas and in writing answers that show conceptual clarity.

## Indian Moral Thought

### Vedic and Upanishadic Ideas

Early Indian thought emphasised Rita (cosmic order) and later Dharma. Dharma is a complex concept encompassing duty, righteousness, law and moral order. The Upanishads focus on the realisation of the Self (Atman) and its identity with the ultimate reality (Brahman). Ethical living is seen as a preparation for spiritual knowledge. Values such as truth (Satya), non-violence (Ahimsa), self-restraint and compassion appear in various texts.

### Buddhist Ethics

Buddhism places great emphasis on ethics as part of the path to liberation. The Five Precepts (Panchasila) form the basic moral code: abstention from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech and intoxicants. The Noble Eightfold Path includes right speech, right action and right livelihood as ethical components. Compassion (Karuna) and loving-kindness (Metta) are central virtues. The doctrine of dependent origination and the emphasis on intention (cetana) make Buddhist ethics nuanced. The ideal of the Bodhisattva in Mahayana Buddhism stresses working for the liberation of all beings.

### Jain Ethics

Jainism carries the principle of Ahimsa to its most rigorous form. Non-violence in thought, word and deed is the foundation. The three jewels (Ratnatraya) are right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. Asceticism, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy and non-possession are key vows. Jain ethics has influenced Indian thought on non-violence and environmental care.

### Bhagavad Gita

The Gita presents a synthesis of different paths. Karma Yoga emphasises performing one’s duty without attachment to the fruits of action (Nishkama Karma). This idea of detached action is highly relevant for public servants. Bhakti Yoga stresses devotion, and Jnana Yoga stresses knowledge. The concept of Svadharma (one’s own duty) and the emphasis on equanimity (Samatva) provide a framework for ethical decision-making under difficult circumstances.

### Modern Indian Thinkers

Mahatma Gandhi integrated Ahimsa and Satya into a practical philosophy of political and social action. His concept of Trusteeship, emphasis on means being as important as ends, and the idea of Sarvodaya (welfare of all) remain influential. His experiments with truth and non-violent resistance offer a model of moral courage.

Swami Vivekananda emphasised the divinity of the soul, service to humanity as service to God, and the development of character and strength. His call for practical Vedanta and fearlessness resonates with the ethics of public service.

## Western Moral Thought

### Socrates, Plato and Aristotle

Socrates emphasised the examined life and the idea that virtue is knowledge; no one does wrong willingly if they truly know the good. Plato linked justice to the harmonious functioning of the soul and the ideal state. Aristotle developed virtue ethics. He argued that moral virtue is a mean between extremes (the doctrine of the golden mean) and that virtues are developed by habit. Happiness (Eudaimonia) is the highest good and is achieved by living a life of virtuous activity in accordance with reason.

### Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill developed utilitarianism. The principle of utility holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness and wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. Bentham proposed a quantitative calculus of pleasure and pain. Mill refined it by distinguishing higher and lower pleasures and emphasising the quality of happiness. Utilitarianism is consequentialist: the morality of an action is judged by its outcomes. It is influential in public policy where cost-benefit analysis and the greatest good of the greatest number are considered.

### Deontological Ethics

Immanuel Kant is the central figure of deontological (duty-based) ethics. He argued that the moral worth of an action lies in the intention and in acting from duty, not from inclination or expected consequences. The Categorical Imperative is the supreme principle of morality. One formulation is: act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law. Another formulation requires treating humanity, whether in oneself or in others, always as an end and never merely as a means. Kantian ethics emphasises autonomy, dignity and universal moral rules.

### Social Contract and Rights-Based Theories

Thinkers such as Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau developed ideas of social contract that underpin modern notions of political obligation and rights. Contemporary rights-based ethics emphasises the inherent dignity of persons and the protection of fundamental rights. John Rawls’s theory of justice as fairness, with the original position and the veil of ignorance, offers a modern framework for thinking about just institutions.

## Relevance for Civil Services

A civil servant encounters conflicts between rules and compassion, between individual rights and collective welfare, between short-term gains and long-term good, and between loyalty to the organisation and duty to the public. Exposure to different ethical traditions helps in:

- Clarifying one’s own moral compass
- Understanding the reasoning behind different positions
- Articulating justifications for decisions
- Balancing competing values such as justice, efficiency, equity and liberty

No single theory provides all answers. A practical ethical approach often draws on virtues of character, a sense of duty, consideration of consequences, and respect for the rights and dignity of persons.

## Key Points for Memory

- Dharma, Ahimsa, Nishkama Karma, Satya as central Indian ethical ideas
- Buddhist Panchasila and Eightfold Path; Jain rigorous non-violence
- Gita: duty without attachment to fruits
- Gandhi: means and ends, Trusteeship, Sarvodaya
- Aristotle: virtue as mean; Eudaimonia
- Utilitarianism: greatest happiness principle (Bentham, Mill)
- Kant: Categorical Imperative; duty and intention; humanity as end
- Rawls: justice as fairness; veil of ignorance
- Ethics for civil servants: integrity, objectivity, compassion, public interest

Moral philosophy does not provide ready-made solutions but cultivates the capacity for reflective judgment. That capacity is essential for ethical governance.
