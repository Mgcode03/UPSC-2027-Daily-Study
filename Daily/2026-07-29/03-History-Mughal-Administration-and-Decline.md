# Mughal Administration and Decline

## Administrative Structure under the Mughals

The Mughal Empire developed a sophisticated and centralised administrative system that combined elements of Central Asian, Persian and Indian traditions. The system reached its maturity under Akbar and continued with modifications under his successors.

### Central Administration

At the apex stood the Emperor, who was the supreme authority in civil, military, judicial and religious matters. The Emperor was assisted by a number of high officials.

The Wakil or Vakil was the prime minister in the early period, but later the office declined in importance. The Diwan or Wazir was the head of the revenue department and the most important minister after the Emperor. He supervised the collection of revenue, expenditure and the overall financial administration.

The Mir Bakshi was the head of the military department. He was responsible for recruitment, branding of horses, maintenance of descriptive rolls of soldiers, and the payment of salaries. He also presented candidates for appointment and promotion to the Emperor.

The Khan-i-Saman or Mir Saman headed the imperial household and the department of manufacturing (karkhanas). He looked after the personal needs of the Emperor, the stores, and the workshops that produced luxury goods and military equipment.

The Sadr-us-Sudur was the head of the ecclesiastical department and the department of charities. He supervised the appointment of qazis, the administration of grants to religious persons and institutions, and the moral and religious life of the empire.

Other important officials included the Muhtasib (censor of public morals), the Qazi-ul-Quzat (chief justice), and various secretaries who handled correspondence and records.

### Provincial Administration

The empire was divided into provinces called Subas. The number of Subas varied; under Akbar there were 15, later rising to 21 under Aurangzeb. Each Suba was headed by a Subadar or Nazim who was responsible for the overall administration, law and order, and the command of the provincial army. The Diwan of the Suba looked after revenue and finance and was independent of the Subadar, reporting directly to the central Diwan. This dual control was designed to prevent concentration of power.

Provinces were further divided into Sarkars (districts), and Sarkars into Parganas (groups of villages). At the village level the traditional headmen and accountants continued to function under imperial supervision.

### The Mansabdari System

One of the most distinctive features of Mughal administration was the Mansabdari system. Every officer was assigned a mansab (rank) which determined his status, salary and military obligations. The mansab was expressed in dual numbers: zat and sawar. Zat indicated personal rank and salary; sawar indicated the number of horsemen the mansabdar was required to maintain.

Mansabdars were paid either in cash or by assignment of jagirs (revenue assignments). The jagirdari system was closely linked to the mansabdari system. A jagirdar collected revenue from his assigned area but did not own the land; the assignment was transferable and was meant only for the maintenance of the required contingent and personal expenses.

The system enabled the Mughals to maintain a large army without a standing paid force in the modern sense, and it integrated nobles of diverse ethnic backgrounds—Turani, Irani, Rajput, Indian Muslim—into a single hierarchical structure.

### Revenue Administration

Akbar’s revenue system, refined by Raja Todar Mal, is known as the Zabti or Bandobast system. Land was measured, classification of soil was done, and average produce and prices of the previous ten years were taken into account to fix the revenue demand. The demand was generally one-third of the produce, payable in cash. The system was applied mainly in the core areas of the empire. In other regions different methods such as crop-sharing (bhaoli) or estimation (kankut) continued.

The Ain-i-Akbari, compiled by Abul Fazl, provides detailed information on the administrative and revenue arrangements of Akbar’s reign.

## Expansion and Consolidation under Major Rulers

Akbar (1556–1605) expanded the empire from a regional kingdom into a pan-Indian empire. He conquered Malwa, Gondwana, Gujarat, Bengal, Kashmir, Sindh and parts of the Deccan. His policy of Sulh-i-Kul (peace with all) and the inclusion of Rajputs into the nobility strengthened the empire.

Jahangir (1605–1627) consolidated the gains and maintained the administrative system. The rebellion of Prince Khurram (later Shah Jahan) and the influence of Nur Jahan marked his reign.

Shah Jahan (1628–1658) is remembered for architectural achievements and for campaigns in the Deccan and Central Asia. The empire reached a high point of wealth and cultural splendour, but the cost of wars and the growing number of mansabdars strained resources.

Aurangzeb (1658–1707) expanded the empire to its greatest territorial extent, annexing Bijapur and Golconda and campaigning extensively in the Deccan against the Marathas. However, continuous warfare, the reimposition of jizya, and conflicts with various groups created widespread discontent.

## Causes of Decline

The decline of the Mughal Empire after Aurangzeb was the result of multiple interconnected factors.

Military and administrative overstretch: The Deccan campaigns drained the treasury and kept the Emperor and large armies away from the north for long periods. The mansabdari and jagirdari systems came under strain as the number of claimants increased while the available land for assignment did not expand proportionally. This led to the jagirdari crisis.

Weak successors: After Aurangzeb a series of weak and short-lived emperors occupied the throne. Factionalism among the nobility—Iranis, Turanis, Hindustanis—intensified.

Rise of regional powers: The Marathas under the Peshwas, the Sikhs in Punjab, the Jats, the Rajputs, and later the Nawabs of Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad asserted autonomy. The empire gradually lost effective control over large territories.

Economic difficulties: Continuous warfare, decline in agricultural productivity in some regions, and disruption of trade routes affected revenue. The invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739 and later Ahmad Shah Abdali further weakened the empire and emptied the treasury.

External challenges: The growing presence and military superiority of European trading companies, especially the English East India Company, eventually transformed political decline into colonial domination.

By the mid-eighteenth century the Mughal Emperor had become a symbolic figure, and real power had passed to regional states and eventually to the British.

## Key Points for Memory

- Central officers: Diwan (finance), Mir Bakshi (military), Khan-i-Saman (household), Sadr (religious grants)
- Provincial division: Suba – Sarkar – Pargana – Village
- Dual control: Subadar and provincial Diwan independent of each other
- Mansabdari: zat (personal rank) and sawar (cavalry rank)
- Jagirdari: transferable revenue assignments linked to mansab
- Zabti system: measurement, classification, cash revenue based on average produce
- Akbar: expansion and Sulh-i-Kul; Todar Mal’s revenue reforms
- Aurangzeb: greatest territorial extent but prolonged Deccan wars
- Decline factors: jagirdari crisis, weak successors, rise of regional powers, foreign invasions, economic strain
- Symbolic survival of Mughal emperor until 1857

The Mughal administrative system provided a model of centralised governance that influenced later Indian states and even the early British administration in some respects. Its strengths in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and its eventual breakdown in the eighteenth century form an important chapter in the political history of India.
